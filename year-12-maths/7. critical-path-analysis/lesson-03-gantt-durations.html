Theory

Gantt charts

What a Gantt chart shows

A Gantt chart lists the activities down the left and time across the top. Each activity is a horizontal bar starting at its earliest start time, with length equal to its duration. Float is usually shown as a lighter extension of the bar.

The network diagram shows dependencies; the Gantt chart shows timing. Together they are how projects are actually managed.

Gantt chart for the sample project

Project: A (4, —), B (6, —), C (3, A), D (5, A and B), E (2, C and D). Bold bars are critical; the lighter extensions show float.

Reading it

  • Bars that overlap in time are activities running in parallel.
  • A bar with no lighter extension has zero float — it is critical.
  • The chart's total width is the minimum completion time.
  • An unbroken run of critical bars from left to right is the critical path.

Method

Drawing a Gantt chart from a network

The procedure

  1. Complete the forward and backward scans, and calculate every float.
  2. List the activities down the left, roughly in order of earliest start.
  3. Draw each bar from its EST, with length equal to its duration.
  4. Extend the bar by its float using a lighter shade or dashed outline.
  5. Mark the critical path — usually by drawing those bars solid or bold.

Worked example 1

Using the analysis from Lesson 2 (completion 13 days; floats A = 2, B = 0, C = 4, D = 0, E = 0):

ActivityESTDurationBar spansFloat
A04day 0 to 42
B06day 0 to 60
C43day 4 to 74
D65day 6 to 110

The critical bars B, D and E tile the timeline end to end with no gaps: 0–6, 6–11, 11–13. That continuous run is the critical path.

A useful check

The critical bars must join up with no gaps and no overlaps, covering the whole timeline from 0 to the completion time. If they do not, either the float calculations or the critical path is wrong.

Application

Reducing an activity's duration

The syllabus asks you to examine the impact of increasing or reducing the duration of an activity on the completion time. The answer depends entirely on whether the activity is critical.

The rule

  • Non-critical activity → no change to the completion time. Its float simply increases.
  • Critical activity → the project shortens, but only until another path becomes critical.

Worked example 2 — shortening a non-critical activity

Activity C (3 days, float 4) is reduced to 1 day. What happens?

C was never governing the finish date. Its float increases from 4 to 6 days, and the project still takes 13 days. No benefit at all.

Worked example 3 — shortening a critical activity

Activity D (5 days, critical) is reduced to 3 days. What happens?

New path lengths:

  • B–D–E: \(6 + 3 + 2 = 11\)
  • A–dummy–D–E: \(4 + 0 + 3 + 2 = 9\)
  • A–C–E: \(4 + 3 + 2 = 9\)

The longest is now 11, so the project finishes in 11 days, saving 2. B–D–E remains critical.

Theory

When the critical path shifts

Shortening a critical activity does not help indefinitely. Once the critical path drops to the length of the next-longest path, that path takes over.

Worked example 4

Reduce D from 5 days as far as possible. How much can the project be shortened?

The two non-D paths are A–C–E at \(4 + 3 + 2 = 9\) days and, once D shrinks, B–D–E at \(6 + D + 2\).

B–D–E stays critical while \(8 + D \ge 9\), that is while \(D \ge 1\).

The floor is 9 days. Reducing D below 1 day achieves nothing, because A–C–E then governs. The useful saving is 4 days, not 5.

The general principle

A project can only be shortened to the length of its next-longest path. Beyond that, a different path becomes critical and further effort on the original activity is wasted. To go further, you must shorten activities on every critical path.

Worked example 5 — exam style

A project has A (5, —), B (8, —), C (6, A), D (4, B), E (3, C and D), F (5, B).

(a) Find the critical path and completion time. [2 marks]

Paths: A–C–E \(= 14\), B–D–E \(= 15\), B–F \(= 13\). Critical path B–D–E, 15 days.

(b) D is reduced by 3 days. Find the new completion time. [1 mark]

B–D–E becomes 12, but A–C–E is 14. New completion time 14 days — a saving of only 1.

(c) Which single activity, reduced by 2 days, gives a completion time of 13? [2 marks]

E, because it lies on both of the two longest paths: A–C–E becomes 12, B–D–E becomes 13, and B–F stays 13. (Reducing B by 2 leaves A–C–E at 14.)

Application

Delays and overruns

The rule for delays

  • A delay within an activity's float → no effect on the finish date; the float is simply consumed.
  • A delay exceeding the float → the project is delayed by \((\text{delay} - \text{float})\).
  • A delay to a critical activity (float 0) → the project is delayed by the full amount.

Worked example 6

Activity A has 2 days of float. It overruns by 5 days. Find the delay to the project.

\[\text{Project delay} = 5 - 2 = 3 \text{ days}\]

The completion time rises from 13 to 16 days. A has now become critical — it has consumed all its slack and more.

Worked example 7 — in context

A construction project is due in 13 weeks. Wet weather delays activity C (float 4 weeks) by 3 weeks, and activity D (critical) by 1 week. Find the new completion time.

C: 3 weeks of delay against 4 weeks of float → absorbed, no effect.

D: critical, so the full 1 week transmits to the finish.

New completion: 14 weeks. Only the critical delay matters.

Technology

Modelling a project in a spreadsheet

The syllabus requires you to use a spreadsheet to model a project involving precedence tables and solve related problems.

A — ActivityB — Duration C — ESTD — EFTE — LSTF — Float
Row 2A40=C2+B22=E2-C2
Row 3B60=C3+B30=E3-C3

The key formulas

  • EFT (earliest finish time) \(=\) EST \(+\) duration: =C2+B2
  • EST of an activity \(=\) the MAX of its predecessors' EFTs. For an activity following both A and B: =MAX(D2,D3)
  • Float \(=\) LST \(-\) EST: =E2-C2
  • Completion time \(=\) =MAX(D2:D6) — the largest EFT in the project.

Reading and using the model:

  • Rows where the float column reads 0 are the critical activities.
  • Changing a duration in column B updates every dependent EST, the floats and the completion time automatically.
  • Conditional formatting on the float column highlights the critical path visually.
  • Testing "what if activity D took 2 days longer?" becomes a single cell edit — exactly the sensitivity analysis the syllabus asks for.

Why MAX is the heart of it

An activity cannot start until every predecessor has finished, so its EST is the maximum of their earliest finish times. That single function reproduces the entire forward scan.

Application

Shortening a project at the least cost

Shortening an activity usually costs money: overtime, extra workers, hired machinery. Shortening a project as cheaply as possible is called crashing. Each activity has a cost per day saved and a limit on how far it can be cut.

The method, one day at a time

  1. Find the critical path(s).
  2. Choose the cheapest way to shorten every critical path by one day. With one critical path, that is its cheapest activity. With several, you may need one activity on each — or a single activity they share.
  3. Recalculate the path lengths and repeat.

Never pay to shorten a non-critical activity unless it lies on a path that has just become critical.

Worked example 8

Project A (2, —), B (5, —), C (4, A), D (3, B), E (6, C and D) takes 14 days. Crashing costs (maximum cut):

ActivityABCDE
Cost per day$150$400$150$250$600
Max days cut12212

Paths: B–D–E \(= 14\) (critical), A–C–E \(= 12\).

To 13 days: cheapest on B–D–E is D at $250. D is now at its limit.

To 12 days: next cheapest on B–D–E is B at $400. Total $650. Now both paths are 12.

To 11 days: both paths must shorten. Options: E (shared) at $600, or B + C at \(\$400 + \$150 = \$550\). Choose B + C. Total \(\$1\,200\).

Your turn

  1. Activities on the only critical path cost $300, $180 and $450 per day to shorten. What is the cheapest way to save one day?
    Show working

    Shorten the $180 activity, assuming it can still be cut. Only critical activities matter.

  2. Two critical paths share activity K ($700/day). The cheapest activity on each path alone costs $300 and $350. What is the cheapest way to save one day?
    Show working

    Both paths must shorten. Cutting the two separate activities costs \(\$300 + \$350 = \$650\), less than $700 for K. Choose the two separate activities.
