Method

The forward scan

The forward scan works left to right through the network, finding the earliest start time (EST) at each vertex — the soonest that every activity arriving there can be complete.

The forward scan rule

\[\text{EST at a vertex} = \textbf{maximum} \big(\text{EST of previous vertex} + \text{duration}\big)\]

Start with EST \(= 0\) at the start vertex. Where several activities arrive, take the largest total — everything must be finished before work can continue.

Worked example 1

Project: A (4, —), B (6, —), C (3, A), D (5, A and B), E (2, C and D).

Vertex 1 (start): EST = 0.

Vertex 2 (end of A): \(0 + 4 = 4\).

Vertex 3 (end of B, and of the dummy from vertex 2): two arrivals —

\[\max(0 + 6,\ 4 + 0) = \max(6, 4) = 6\]

Vertex 4 (end of C): \(4 + 3 = 7\).

Vertex 5 (end of D): \(6 + 5 = 11\).

Vertex 6 (end of E, from both 4 and 5): here E leaves vertex 5 only, but taking the general case —

\[11 + 2 = 13\]

Minimum completion time = 13 days.

The key result

The EST at the finish vertex is the minimum completion time for the whole project. It is also the length of the longest path.

Forward scan walkthrough

Step through the scan one vertex at a time and see each maximum being taken.

Your turn

Project: A (2, —), B (5, —), C (4, A), D (3, B), E (6, C and D). C and D both end at the vertex where E starts.

  1. Carry out the forward scan. What is the minimum completion time?
    Show working

    Start 0. End of A: 2. End of B: 5. Start of E: \(\max(2 + 4,\ 5 + 3) = \max(6, 8) = 8\). Finish: \(8 + 6 = 14\).

    Minimum completion time: 14.

  2. At the vertex where E starts, which arriving activity "wins" the maximum? What does that tell you?
    Show working

    D (via B), with 8. E cannot start until D is finished, so the chain B–D is holding the project up; A–C arrives 2 days earlier.

Method

The backward scan

The backward scan works right to left, finding the latest start time (LST) at each vertex — the latest moment work can leave without delaying the project.

The backward scan rule

\[\text{LST at a vertex} = \textbf{minimum} \big(\text{LST of next vertex} - \text{duration}\big)\]

Start at the finish vertex, where LST = EST. Where several activities leave, take the smallest — the earliest deadline governs.

The symmetry worth memorising

Forward scan: work forwards, add durations, take the maximum.
Backward scan: work backwards, subtract durations, take the minimum.

Worked example 2

Continuing the project above, where the completion time is 13.

Vertex 6 (finish): LST = EST = 13.

Vertex 5 (E, duration 2, leads to 6): \(13 - 2 = 11\).

Vertex 4 (leads to 6 via a zero-length join): \(13 - 2 = 11\).

Vertex 3 (D, duration 5, leads to 5): \(11 - 5 = 6\).

Vertex 2 — two activities leave: C (3) to vertex 4, and the dummy (0) to vertex 3:

\[\min(11 - 3,\ 6 - 0) = \min(8, 6) = 6\]

Vertex 1 (start): two leave — A (4) to vertex 2, B (6) to vertex 3:

\[\min(6 - 4,\ 6 - 6) = \min(2, 0) = 0\]

Check: the LST at the start vertex must be 0. If it is not, there is an arithmetic error somewhere.

Theory

Float time

Float time

\[\text{Float} = \text{LST at end vertex} - \text{EST at start vertex} - \text{duration}\]

Float time (also called slack) is the amount by which an activity can be delayed, or run over, without delaying the whole project.

Worked example 3

Activity C runs from vertex 2 (EST 4) to vertex 4 (LST 11) and takes 3 days. Find its float.

\[\text{Float} = 11 - 4 - 3 = 4 \text{ days}\]

C can start up to 4 days late, or take 4 days longer than planned, without pushing back the project's finish.

Worked example 4 — a critical activity

Activity D runs from vertex 3 (EST 6) to vertex 5 (LST 11) and takes 5 days.

\[\text{Float} = 11 - 6 - 5 = 0\]

D has zero float. Any delay to D delays the entire project by the same amount. D is a critical activity.

Interpreting float

  • Float = 0 → the activity is critical. It has no slack at all.
  • Float > 0 → the activity is non-critical, with that much slack.
  • Float is never negative in a correctly analysed network. A negative value means a scanning error.

Method

Finding the critical path

The critical path

The critical path is the continuous chain of activities from start to finish for which every activity has zero float. Its length is the minimum completion time.

How to identify it

  1. Do the forward scan for every EST.
  2. Do the backward scan for every LST.
  3. Calculate the float for every activity.
  4. The zero-float activities form the critical path.

A vertex where EST = LST lies on the critical path, but you must still check the activities — a zero-float vertex at each end does not by itself make an activity critical.

Why it matters

  • Delaying any critical activity delays the whole project.
  • Shortening the project requires shortening a critical activity.
  • Speeding up a non-critical activity achieves nothing — it just increases that activity's float.

A project may have more than one critical path if two chains happen to be the same length. In that case every critical path must be shortened to shorten the project.

Your turn

  1. A project's paths have lengths 18, 21, 21 and 15 days. What is the minimum completion time, and how many critical paths are there?
    Show working

    21 days, the longest. Two paths have length 21, so there are two critical paths.

  2. For that project, a manager shortens one activity that lies on only one of the 21-day paths. Does the project finish sooner?
    Show working

    No. The other 21-day path is unchanged, so the project still takes 21 days. An activity common to both paths, or one activity on each, must be shortened.

Method

Scanning without a diagram: the activity table

The scans can be done directly on the precedence table, one activity per row. This is how a spreadsheet does it (see Lesson 3), and it avoids drawing dummies.

Four times for each activity

  • ES (earliest start) = the largest EF of its predecessors (0 if none).
  • EF (earliest finish) = ES + duration.
  • LF (latest finish) = the smallest LS of the activities that follow it (the completion time if none).
  • LS (latest start) = LF − duration.

Float \(= \text{LS} - \text{ES}\). Fill ES and EF top to bottom, then LF and LS bottom to top.

Class activity

The delay drill

About 10 minutes · pairs. You manage the project A (4), B (6), C (3), D (5), E (2) from this lesson, due in 13 days. Five pieces of bad news arrive. For each one, predict the new completion time using float, then check it in the full project analyser and reset.

  1. C will take 3 days longer.
  2. A will take 3 days longer.
  3. B will take 2 days longer.
  4. C will take 5 days longer.
  5. D will take 1 day longer.

Then discuss: after news 2, the critical path changes. Which activities become critical, and why?

Discussion points
NewsFloatNew completionWhy
C +3413absorbed by float
A +32142 days absorbed, 1 day passed on
B +2015critical: every day is passed on
C +54144 absorbed, 1 passed on
D +1014critical

Rule: new completion time \(= 13 + \max(0,\ \text{delay} - \text{float})\), as long as only one activity is delayed.

After news 2, A–dummy–D–E (\(7 + 0 + 5 + 2 = 14\)) is the longest path, so A becomes critical and B gains a day of float.
