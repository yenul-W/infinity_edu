Theory

Solving by graphing

A pair of simultaneous linear equations is two straight-line equations considered together. Solving them means finding the single \(x\) and \(y\) that make both equations true at the same time.

Graphically, each equation is a line. The solution is the point of intersection — the one point that lies on both lines.

The three possible outcomes

  • One solution — the lines cross once (different gradients).
  • No solution — the lines are parallel and never meet (same gradient, different \(y\)-intercept).
  • Infinitely many — the two equations describe the same line.

Worked example 1

Solve graphically: \(y = 2x - 1\) and \(y = -x + 5\).

Build a small table of values for each line, plot both, and read off where they cross.

\(x\)01234
\(y = 2x - 1\)−11357
\(y = -x + 5\)54321

Both lines pass through \((2, 3)\), so the solution is \(x = 2,\ y = 3\).

Always check by substituting into both equations: \(2(2) - 1 = 3\) ✓ and \(-(2) + 5 = 3\) ✓.

Theory

Algebraic methods

Graphing is quick but only as accurate as your reading of the grid. When the solution is not a neat whole number, solve algebraically.

Substitution

Use this when one equation already has \(x\) or \(y\) on its own.

  1. Rearrange one equation so a variable is by itself (often it already is).
  2. Substitute that expression into the other equation.
  3. Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
  4. Substitute back to find the second variable.

Worked example 2 — substitution

Solve \(y = 3x + 1\)  (1)  and \(2x + y = 11\)  (2).

Equation (1) already gives \(y\). Substitute into (2):

\[2x + (3x + 1) = 11\] \[5x + 1 = 11 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 5x = 10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 2\]

Substitute \(x = 2\) back into (1): \(y = 3(2) + 1 = 7\).

Solution: \((2, 7)\). Check in (2): \(2(2) + 7 = 11\) ✓

Elimination

Use this when both equations are in the form \(ax + by = c\).

  1. Multiply one or both equations so that one variable has matching coefficients.
  2. Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
  3. Solve for the remaining variable, then substitute back.

Worked example 3 — elimination

Solve \(3x + 2y = 16\)  (1)  and \(5x - 2y = 8\)  (2).

The \(y\) coefficients are \(+2\) and \(-2\) — adding eliminates \(y\):

\[(3x + 2y) + (5x - 2y) = 16 + 8\] \[8x = 24 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 3\]

Substitute into (1): \(3(3) + 2y = 16 \Rightarrow 2y = 7 \Rightarrow y = 3.5\).

Solution: \((3,\ 3.5)\).

Worked example 4 — elimination needing a multiplier

Solve \(2x + 3y = 13\)  (1)  and \(4x - y = 5\)  (2).

Multiply (2) by 3 so the \(y\) terms become \(+3y\) and \(-3y\):

\[(2)\times 3:\quad 12x - 3y = 15 \quad (3)\]

Add (1) and (3):

\[14x = 28 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 2\]

Substitute into (2): \(4(2) - y = 5 \Rightarrow y = 3\). Solution: \((2, 3)\).

Application

Developing equations from a worded situation

Exam questions rarely hand you the equations. You build them. The reliable routine:

  1. Define the variables in words — "let \(x\) be the number of adult tickets". Write this down; marks are awarded for it.
  2. Find the two separate facts in the question. Each fact becomes one equation. Usually one is a count and the other a total value.
  3. Solve using substitution or elimination.
  4. Answer in context — with units, in a sentence.

Worked example 5

A cinema sells adult tickets at $22 and child tickets at $14. On Saturday it sold 180 tickets and took $3,240. How many of each were sold?

Define: let \(a\) = adult tickets, \(c\) = child tickets.

Count equation: \(a + c = 180\)  (1)

Value equation: \(22a + 14c = 3240\)  (2)

From (1), \(c = 180 - a\). Substitute into (2):

\[22a + 14(180 - a) = 3240\] \[22a + 2520 - 14a = 3240\] \[8a = 720 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = 90\]

So \(c = 180 - 90 = 90\).

In context: 90 adult tickets and 90 child tickets were sold.

Worked example 6 — two plans compared

Gym A charges a $60 joining fee plus $12 per visit. Gym B charges no joining fee but $20 per visit. After how many visits do they cost the same?

Let \(n\) = number of visits and \(C\) = total cost.

\[\text{Gym A: } C = 60 + 12n \qquad \text{Gym B: } C = 20n\]

Set them equal:

\[60 + 12n = 20n \quad\Rightarrow\quad 60 = 8n \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 7.5\]

Because visits must be whole numbers, the costs are never exactly equal. For 7 visits Gym A costs $144 and Gym B $140 — B is cheaper. From 8 visits on (A: $156, B: $160), Gym A is cheaper.

This is a limitation of the model: the algebra gives a continuous answer, but the context only allows whole numbers.

Your turn

  1. A café sells small coffees for $4.50 and large coffees for $5.50. One morning it sold 140 coffees and took $698. Define variables, write two equations and find how many of each size were sold.
    Show working

    Let \(s\) = small coffees, \(l\) = large coffees.

    \[s + l = 140 \;\;(1) \qquad 4.5s + 5.5l = 698 \;\;(2)\]

    From (1), \(s = 140 - l\): \(4.5(140 - l) + 5.5l = 698 \Rightarrow 630 + l = 698 \Rightarrow l = 68\).

    Answer: 72 small and 68 large coffees.

  2. Car hire company A charges $55 per day plus $0.25 per km. Company B charges $40 per day plus $0.40 per km. For a one-day hire, at what distance do they cost the same, and which is cheaper for a 180 km trip?
    Show working
    \[55 + 0.25k = 40 + 0.40k \;\Rightarrow\; 15 = 0.15k \;\Rightarrow\; k = 100\]

    At 180 km: A costs \(55 + 0.25(180) = \$100\), B costs \(40 + 0.40(180) = \$112\).

    Answer: equal at 100 km; company A is cheaper for 180 km.

Application

Break-even analysis

A business has two linear relationships in the number of items \(n\):

Cost, revenue and profit

\[C = \text{fixed costs} + (\text{variable cost per item}) \times n\] \[R = (\text{selling price per item}) \times n\] \[P = R - C\]

Fixed costs are paid whether you sell anything or not (rent, equipment). Variable costs depend on how many you make (materials, packaging).

The break-even point is where \(C = R\) — the business covers its costs exactly and profit is zero. It is the point of intersection of the cost line and the revenue line.

  • Selling fewer than the break-even quantity → \(R < C\) → a loss.
  • Selling more than the break-even quantity → \(R > C\) → a profit.

Worked example 7

A candle maker pays $450 per month in rent and $4 in materials per candle. Candles sell for $13 each.

(a) Write the cost and revenue equations.

\[C = 450 + 4n \qquad R = 13n\]

(b) Find the break-even point. Set \(C = R\):

\[450 + 4n = 13n \quad\Rightarrow\quad 450 = 9n \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 50\]

At \(n = 50\), \(R = 13 \times 50 = \$650\). Break-even is 50 candles, at $650.

(c) Find the profit on 80 candles.

\[P = R - C = 13(80) - (450 + 4 \times 80) = 1040 - 770 = \$270\]

(d) What happens at 30 candles?

\[P = 13(30) - (450 + 120) = 390 - 570 = -\$180\]

A loss of $180, because 30 is below the break-even quantity.

Cost \(C = 450 + 4n\) and revenue \(R = 13n\) cross at \(n = 50\). Left of the crossing is loss; right of it is profit.

Worked example 8 — exam style

A potter has fixed costs of $960 per month. Each mug costs $8 to make and sells for $20. At this price she sells about 90 mugs a month. She is considering raising the price to $24, which she expects would cut sales to about 70 mugs a month.

(a) Find the break-even quantity at each price. [2 marks]

\[\$20:\; n = \frac{960}{20 - 8} = 80 \text{ mugs} \qquad \$24:\; n = \frac{960}{24 - 8} = 60 \text{ mugs}\]

(b) Which price gives the greater monthly profit? Justify with calculations. [2 marks]

\[\$20:\; P = 12(90) - 960 = \$120 \qquad \$24:\; P = 16(70) - 960 = \$160\]

The $24 price earns $40 more per month, even though she sells 20 fewer mugs.

(c) Give one reason the prediction in (b) may not hold. [1 mark]

The sales figure of 70 at the higher price is only an estimate. If sales fell to 67 mugs or fewer (\(16n - 960 = 120\) gives \(n = 67.5\)), the old price would be better.

Your turn

  1. A screen printer has fixed costs of $1,200 per month, spends $7 per shirt and sells each shirt for $22. Find (a) the break-even point, including the revenue there, (b) the profit on 130 shirts and (c) the result of selling only 50 shirts.
    Show working

    (a) \(1200 + 7n = 22n \Rightarrow 15n = 1200 \Rightarrow n = 80\) shirts; revenue \(22 \times 80 = \$1,760\).

    (b) \(P = 22(130) - (1200 + 7 \times 130) = 2860 - 2110 = \$750\).

    (c) \(P = 22(50) - (1200 + 350) = -\$450\) — a loss of $450.

  2. A bakery breaks even at 120 loaves. Each loaf sells for $6.50 and costs $2.50 in ingredients. What are the bakery's fixed costs?
    Show working

    At break-even, fixed costs = contribution per item \(\times\) break-even quantity.

    \[F = (6.50 - 2.50) \times 120 = \$480\]

Application

Planning for a target profit

Breaking even only means the business stops losing money. Owners usually want to know how many items they must sell to make a particular profit, or what price they need to charge when they can only make a limited number.

Write profit directly in terms of \(n\). With selling price \(s\), variable cost \(v\) and fixed costs \(F\):

Profit equation

\[P = (s - v)\,n - F\]

To reach a target profit \(T\), set \(P = T\) and solve for the unknown. If you are solving for \(n\) and the answer is not a whole number, round up — rounding down would fall short of the target.

Break-even is just the special case \(T = 0\). Graphically, the profit line has gradient \(s - v\) (the contribution per item) and \(P\)-intercept \(-F\): it crosses the \(n\)-axis at the break-even point.

Worked example 9

The candle maker from Worked example 7 has \(F = 450\), \(v = 4\) and \(s = 13\).

(a) How many candles give a profit of $360 per month?

\[9n - 450 = 360 \;\Rightarrow\; 9n = 810 \;\Rightarrow\; n = 90 \text{ candles}\]

(b) She can only make 75 candles a month. What price reaches the same $360 profit?

\[75(s - 4) - 450 = 360 \;\Rightarrow\; 75(s - 4) = 810 \;\Rightarrow\; s - 4 = 10.8 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \$14.80\]

Your turn

  1. The screen printer above (\(F = 1200\), \(v = 7\), \(s = 22\)) wants a profit of $900 a month. How many shirts must be sold?
    Show working
    \[15n - 1200 = 900 \;\Rightarrow\; 15n = 2100 \;\Rightarrow\; n = 140 \text{ shirts}\]
  2. A juice stand pays $300 per weekend for its site, spends $1.50 per cup and can sell at most 160 cups. What price per cup gives a profit of $340?
    Show working
    \[160(s - 1.50) - 300 = 340 \;\Rightarrow\; 160(s - 1.50) = 640 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \$5.50\]

Shortcut worth knowing

\[n_{\text{break-even}} = \frac{\text{fixed costs}}{\text{selling price} - \text{variable cost}}\]

The denominator is the contribution per item — how much each sale puts towards covering the fixed costs. Here \(450 \div (13-4) = 50\) ✓

Class activity

Which lever moves break-even most?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Scroll back to the break-even calculator. Start every step from the candle maker's settings: fixed costs $450, cost per item $4, price per item $13 (break-even 50 items).

  1. Raise the price by $2. Record the new break-even quantity.
  2. Reset. Instead, lower the cost per item by $2. Record the break-even quantity. Why is it the same as in step 1?
  3. Reset. Double the fixed costs. What happens to the break-even quantity? Explain using \(n = F \div (s - v)\).
  4. Set the price equal to the cost per item. What does the calculator report, and why?
  5. Discuss: the model assumes every candle made is sold. Which of the three changes is most likely to break that assumption, and why?
Discussion points

1 and 2. Both give 40.9 items, so 41 candles. Each change raises the contribution per item from $9 to $11, and break-even depends only on that contribution and the fixed costs.

3. Break-even doubles to 100 items. Fixed costs are the numerator, so break-even is directly proportional to them.

4. It never breaks even: each sale contributes $0, so the fixed costs are never covered. The cost and revenue lines are parallel — no point of intersection.

5. Raising the price: customers may buy fewer candles, so sales could fall below the new break-even. Cutting variable cost (a cheaper supplier) leaves the customer's price unchanged.
