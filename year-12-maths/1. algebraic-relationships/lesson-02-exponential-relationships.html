Exponential Relationships

When a quantity is multiplied by the same factor every period — rather than having the same amount added — the relationship is exponential. This is the mathematics of populations, bacteria, radioactive decay and compound growth.

Theory

Recognising an exponential relationship

In a linear relationship you add the same amount each step. In an exponential relationship you multiply by the same factor each step. That factor is the base, \(a\).

The test

Take consecutive \(y\)-values from a table. If the differences are constant → linear. If the ratios are constant → exponential.

Worked example 1

Decide whether each table is linear or exponential.

\(x\)01234
Table A58111417
Table B510204080

Table A: differences are \(3, 3, 3, 3\) — constant. Linear, \(y = 5 + 3x\).

Table B: differences are \(5, 10, 20, 40\) — not constant. Try ratios: \(10 \div 5 = 2\), \(20 \div 10 = 2\), \(40 \div 20 = 2\), \(80 \div 40 = 2\) — constant. Exponential with base 2, \(y = 5 \times 2^x\).

Your turn

  1. For \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\), decide whether each table is linear or exponential and write its equation. (a) 3200, 1600, 800, 400   (b) 7, 11.5, 16, 20.5
    Show working

    (a) Ratios are all \(0.5\): exponential, \(y = 3200 \times 0.5^x\).

    (b) Differences are all \(4.5\): linear, \(y = 7 + 4.5x\).

  2. Which of these are exponential? \(y = 5^x\), \(y = x^5\), \(y = 2 \times 0.9^x\), \(y = 3x + 2\)
    Show working

    Only \(y = 5^x\) and \(y = 2 \times 0.9^x\) have the variable in the exponent. \(y = x^5\) is a power of \(x\), and \(y = 3x + 2\) is linear.

You can also recognise them from the equation. An exponential relationship has the variable in the exponent:

  • \(y = 3^x\) — exponential (variable is the power).
  • \(y = x^3\) — not exponential; this is a cubic, where the variable is the base.
  • \(y = 200 \times 1.05^x\) — exponential.

Linear or exponential?

Classify each sequence of \(y\)-values. Type linear or exponential.

Theory

Graphing \(y = a^x\) and \(y = a^{-x}\)

For \(a > 0\) and \(a \neq 1\), the graph of \(y = a^x\) always has the same shape.

Key features of \(y = a^x\)

  • Passes through \((0, 1)\) — because \(a^0 = 1\) for every base.
  • Never touches the \(x\)-axis. \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote — the curve gets arbitrarily close but never reaches it.
  • \(y > 0\) always — the range is \(y > 0\).
  • If \(a > 1\): increasing (growth). If \(0 < a < 1\): decreasing (decay).

The graph of \(y = a^{-x}\) is the reflection of \(y = a^x\) in the \(y\)-axis. Note that

\[a^{-x} = \frac{1}{a^x} = \left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^{x}\]

so \(y = 2^{-x}\) and \(y = 0.5^{x}\) are the same curve — a decay curve.

\(y = 2^x\) — growth

\(y = 2^{-x}\) — decay

Parameter explorer

Change \(k\) and \(a\) in \(y = ka^x\), and flip the sign of the exponent. Notice that \(k\) is always the \(y\)-intercept, and the curve grows when \(a > 1\) but decays when \(a < 1\).

Your turn

  1. For \(y = 0.4^x\), state the \(y\)-intercept, whether the graph increases or decreases, and the equation of its asymptote.
    Show working

    \(y\)-intercept 1 (since \(0.4^0 = 1\)); decreasing because \(0 < 0.4 < 1\); asymptote \(y = 0\).

  2. Write \(y = 5^{-x}\) in the form \(y = b^x\). Which single point lies on both \(y = 5^x\) and \(y = 5^{-x}\)?
    Show working

    \(5^{-x} = \left(\frac{1}{5}\right)^x = 0.2^x\). The curves are reflections in the \(y\)-axis, so they meet only on it, at \((0, 1)\).

Theory

The model \(y = ka^x\)

Interpreting the parameters

\[y = ka^{x}\]

\(k\) is the initial value — the value of \(y\) when \(x = 0\), and therefore the \(y\)-intercept.
\(a\) is the growth or decay factor — what you multiply by each period.
\(x\) is the number of periods elapsed.

Converting a percentage rate into the factor \(a\) is the step students most often get wrong:

SituationFactor \(a\)Example
Growth of \(r\%\) per period\(a = 1 + \frac{r}{100}\)8% growth → \(a = 1.08\)
Decay of \(r\%\) per period\(a = 1 - \frac{r}{100}\)15% decay → \(a = 0.85\)
Doubling each period\(a = 2\)\(y = k \times 2^x\)
Halving each period\(a = 0.5\)\(y = k \times 0.5^x\)

Worked example 2 — finding the equation from a table

Find the exponential equation for this data.

\(x\)0123
\(y\)12182740.5

The value at \(x = 0\) is 12, so \(k = 12\).

The ratio is \(18 \div 12 = 1.5\); check \(27 \div 18 = 1.5\) ✓ and \(40.5 \div 27 = 1.5\) ✓. So \(a = 1.5\).

\[y = 12 \times 1.5^{x}\]

Check at \(x = 3\): \(12 \times 1.5^3 = 12 \times 3.375 = 40.5\) ✓

Worked example 3 — finding \(a\) when \(k\) is not at \(x=0\)

An exponential model passes through \((0, 500)\) and \((4, 2000)\). Find \(a\).

Since \(x = 0\) gives \(y = 500\), \(k = 500\). Then:

\[500 \times a^{4} = 2000 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a^{4} = 4\] \[a = \sqrt[4]{4} = 4^{0.25} \approx 1.414\]

So \(y = 500 \times 1.414^{x}\), a growth of about 41.4% per period.

Your turn

  1. Find the exponential equation for \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\) and \(y = 80, 60, 45, 33.75\). Is it growth or decay, and by what percentage per period?
    Show working

    \(k = 80\); ratio \(60 \div 80 = 0.75\) (check \(45 \div 60 = 0.75\) ✓).

    Answer: \(y = 80 \times 0.75^x\) — decay of 25% per period.

  2. An exponential model passes through \((0, 300)\) and \((2, 432)\). Find \(a\) and the percentage growth per period.
    Show working
    \[300a^2 = 432 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 = 1.44 \;\Rightarrow\; a = 1.2\]

    Answer: \(y = 300 \times 1.2^x\), growth of 20% per period.

Theory

Reading a model from its graph

Exam questions often give only a graph. Two clearly marked points are enough to write the model \(y = ka^x\):

  1. Read the \(y\)-intercept: that is \(k\), the initial value.
  2. Take a second point \((x_1, y_1)\) and solve \(k a^{x_1} = y_1\) for \(a\).
  3. Interpret \(a\) in context: \(a > 1\) is growth of \((a - 1) \times 100\%\) per period, \(a < 1\) is decay of \((1 - a) \times 100\%\) per period.

Worked example 4

Graph A shows the number of followers of a new account after \(x\) weeks. It passes through \((0, 200)\) and \((2, 450)\).

\[k = 200: \quad 200a^2 = 450 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 = 2.25 \;\Rightarrow\; a = 1.5\]

Model \(y = 200 \times 1.5^x\): followers grow by 50% each week.

Graph B shows a medicine level (mg) \(x\) hours after a dose, through \((0, 80)\) and \((3, 10)\).

\[80a^3 = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; a^3 = 0.125 \;\Rightarrow\; a = 0.5\]

Model \(y = 80 \times 0.5^x\): the level halves every hour, so it falls by 50% per hour.

Your turn

  1. An exponential graph has \(y\)-intercept 50 and passes through \((2, 72)\). Write its equation and describe the rate of change.
    Show working
    \[50a^2 = 72 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 = 1.44 \;\Rightarrow\; a = 1.2\]

    Answer: \(y = 50 \times 1.2^x\) — growth of 20% per period.

  2. A graph of a drink's temperature above room temperature, \(T\) (°C), after \(t\) minutes passes through \((0, 64)\) and \((3, 27)\). Find the model and predict \(T\) after 5 minutes.
    Show working
    \[64a^3 = 27 \;\Rightarrow\; a^3 = \tfrac{27}{64} \;\Rightarrow\; a = \tfrac{3}{4} = 0.75\]

    \(T = 64 \times 0.75^t\): it falls by 25% each minute. At \(t = 5\): \(64 \times 0.75^5 \approx 15.2\)°C above room temperature.

Application

Growth and decay problems

Worked example 5 — population growth

A town has 8,000 residents and is growing at 3% per year.

(a) Write a model. \(k = 8000\), \(a = 1 + 0.03 = 1.03\):

\[P = 8000 \times 1.03^{t}\]

(b) Population after 10 years?

\[P = 8000 \times 1.03^{10} = 8000 \times 1.3439 \approx 10\,751\]

(c) When does it reach 12,000? By trial:

\(t\)12131415
\(P\)11 40611 74812 10112 464

The population first exceeds 12,000 during year 14.

Worked example 6 — decay

A car bought for $42,000 loses 18% of its value each year.

\(k = 42000\), \(a = 1 - 0.18 = 0.82\):

\[V = 42000 \times 0.82^{t}\]

Value after 5 years:

\[V = 42000 \times 0.82^{5} = 42000 \times 0.37074 \approx \$15\,571\]

Note the car never reaches $0 under this model — the curve approaches the asymptote \(V = 0\) without touching it.

Worked example 7 — half-life

A radioactive sample of 240 g has a half-life of 6 days. Find the mass after 30 days.

Let \(n\) = number of half-lives \(= 30 \div 6 = 5\).

\[M = 240 \times 0.5^{5} = 240 \times 0.03125 = 7.5\ \text{g}\]

Alternatively, in days: \(M = 240 \times 0.5^{t/6}\), and at \(t = 30\) this gives the same 7.5 g.

Worked example 8 — exam style

Town A has 15,000 residents and grows at 2% per year. Town B has 12,000 residents and grows at 5% per year.

(a) Write a model for each town. [1 mark]

\[A = 15000 \times 1.02^{t} \qquad B = 12000 \times 1.05^{t}\]

(b) In which year does Town B's population first exceed Town A's? [2 marks]

\(t\)6789
Town A16 89217 23017 57517 926
Town B16 08116 88517 72918 616

Town B first exceeds Town A at \(t = 8\), during the eighth year.

(c) A planner uses the models to predict the populations in 50 years. Comment on this. [1 mark]

It is a long extrapolation. Both models assume the growth rates stay at 2% and 5% for 50 years; Town B's model predicts over 137,000 residents, which housing, jobs and services are unlikely to support.

Your turn

  1. A phone bought for $1,400 loses 30% of its value each year. Write a model and find its value after 3 years.
    Show working

    \(a = 1 - 0.30 = 0.7\), so \(V = 1400 \times 0.7^t\).

    \(V = 1400 \times 0.7^3 = 1400 \times 0.343 = \$480.20\).

  2. A culture of 250 bacteria triples every 4 hours. How many are there after 12 hours?
    Show working

    12 hours is \(12 \div 4 = 3\) tripling periods: \(N = 250 \times 3^3 = 6750\) bacteria.

Evaluation

Limitations of exponential models

The syllabus explicitly asks you to explain the limitations of exponential models in practical contexts. These are the arguments that earn the marks:

1. Unbounded growth is unrealistic

An exponential growth model increases without limit. Real populations are constrained by food, space, disease and resources. A model predicting a town of 8,000 growing at 3% per year gives over 150,000 people after 100 years — almost certainly beyond what the area can support.

2. The rate is assumed constant

The model assumes \(a\) never changes. In reality growth rates fluctuate with economic conditions, policy, migration and seasons. A single fixed percentage is an approximation.

3. Decay never reaches zero

Because \(y = 0\) is an asymptote, a decay model never predicts a value of exactly zero. A car modelled by \(V = 42000 \times 0.82^t\) is still worth $1,000 after 19 years and never becomes worthless — but in practice a car eventually has only scrap value.

4. Extrapolation is risky

Predictions well beyond the range of the original data are unreliable. A model fitted to 5 years of data may be reasonable for year 6, but is speculative for year 50.

5. The domain may be restricted

Negative values of \(x\) often have no meaning (you cannot have −3 years of growth from the starting point), and values may need to be whole numbers — you cannot have 10,751.2 people.

Answering these questions in an exam

Name the limitation, then tie it to the specific context and numbers in the question. "The model assumes growth continues at 3% forever, but the town's land area limits how many people can live there, so the prediction of 150,000 after 100 years is unrealistic" scores; "the model is not accurate" does not.

Class activity

Discovering the rule of 72

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the growth & decay table generator with an initial value of 1000 and growth (not decay).

  1. Set the rate to 10%. Between which two periods does the value first pass 2000? Estimate the doubling time to one decimal place.
  2. Repeat for 12%, 18% and 24%. Record each rate and doubling time in a table.
  3. Multiply each rate by its doubling time. What do you notice?
  4. Use your discovery to predict how long money takes to double at 6% per year, then check with the formula \(1000 \times 1.06^t\) on a calculator.
  5. Does the starting value matter? Change it to 3000 and explain what you see.
