Theory

Recognising a quadratic relationship

A quadratic relationship has the form

\[y = ax^{2} + bx + c \qquad (a \neq 0)\]

The defining feature is the \(x^{2}\) term. Its graph is a parabola — a symmetric U-shaped curve.

Which way does it open?

If \(a > 0\) the parabola opens upwards (a valley) and has a minimum at its vertex.
If \(a < 0\) it opens downwards (a hill) and has a maximum at its vertex.

From a table, a quadratic has neither constant differences (linear) nor constant ratios (exponential). Instead its second differences are constant.

Worked example 1 — the second-difference test

\(x\)01234
\(y\)36132439

First differences: \(3,\ 7,\ 11,\ 15\) — not constant, so not linear.

Second differences: \(4,\ 4,\ 4\) — constant, so the relationship is quadratic.

(For the record, \(y = 2x^{2} + x + 3\).)

\(a > 0\): minimum

\(a < 0\): maximum

Theory

Intercepts, axis of symmetry and vertex

The \(y\)-intercept

Set \(x = 0\): every term with an \(x\) disappears, leaving \(y = c\). So the \(y\)-intercept of \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) is always \(c\).

The \(x\)-intercepts

These are where the curve crosses the \(x\)-axis, so \(y = 0\). A parabola may have two, one, or no \(x\)-intercepts. In this course you read them from a graph or from a graphing application rather than using the quadratic formula.

The axis of symmetry

A parabola is symmetric about a vertical line through its vertex. The syllabus method is to use the midpoint of the \(x\)-intercepts:

Axis of symmetry from the x-intercepts

\[x = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\]

where \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are the two \(x\)-intercepts. The vertex sits on this line.

The vertex

The vertex is the turning point — the maximum or minimum. To find it:

  1. Find the axis of symmetry, \(x = \dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\). This is the \(x\)-coordinate of the vertex.
  2. Substitute that \(x\) into the equation to get the \(y\)-coordinate.

Worked example 2

A parabola \(y = x^{2} - 6x + 5\) crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 5\). Find the \(y\)-intercept, the axis of symmetry and the vertex.

\(y\)-intercept: \(c = 5\), so \((0, 5)\).

Axis of symmetry:

\[x = \frac{1 + 5}{2} = 3\]

Vertex: substitute \(x = 3\):

\[y = 3^{2} - 6(3) + 5 = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4\]

The vertex is \((3, -4)\). Since \(a = 1 > 0\) this is a minimum.

\(y = x^2 - 6x + 5\): intercepts at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 5\), vertex \((3, -4)\), axis of symmetry \(x = 3\).

Application

Practical parabola problems

Quadratics model anything with a single maximum or minimum: the path of a thrown object, the area enclosed by a fixed length of fencing, or a revenue that rises then falls as price increases.

Worked example 3 — projectile

A ball is thrown and its height in metres after \(t\) seconds is \(h = -5t^{2} + 20t\).

(a) When does it land? Landing means \(h = 0\):

\[-5t^{2} + 20t = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad -5t(t - 4) = 0\]

So \(t = 0\) (thrown) or \(t = 4\) (lands). The \(t\)-intercepts are 0 and 4.

(b) When is it highest? The axis of symmetry is the midpoint of the intercepts:

\[t = \frac{0 + 4}{2} = 2 \text{ seconds}\]

(c) What is the maximum height? Substitute \(t = 2\):

\[h = -5(4) + 20(2) = -20 + 40 = 20\ \text{m}\]

The vertex is \((2, 20)\): maximum height 20 m at 2 seconds.

\(h = -5t^2 + 20t\): the ball peaks at 20 m after 2 s and lands at 4 s.

Worked example 4 — maximising area

A farmer has 40 m of fencing to enclose a rectangular pen against an existing wall (so only three sides need fencing). Find the dimensions giving the largest area.

Let the two sides perpendicular to the wall be \(x\) each. The remaining side is \(40 - 2x\).

\[A = x(40 - 2x) = 40x - 2x^{2}\]

This is a quadratic with \(a = -2 < 0\), so it has a maximum.

The \(x\)-intercepts are where \(x(40 - 2x) = 0\), i.e. \(x = 0\) and \(x = 20\).

\[x = \frac{0 + 20}{2} = 10\]

So \(x = 10\) m, the other side is \(40 - 20 = 20\) m, and

\[A = 10 \times 20 = 200\ \text{m}^{2}\]

Valid domain: \(0 < x < 20\). Outside this range the area is zero or negative, which is meaningless.

Worked example 5 — exam style

The arch of a tunnel is modelled by \(h = -0.2x^{2} + 4x\), where \(h\) is the height in metres and \(x\) is the horizontal distance in metres from one side of the base.

(a) How wide is the tunnel at ground level? [1 mark]

\(h = 0\) when \(x(-0.2x + 4) = 0\), so \(x = 0\) or \(x = 20\). The tunnel is 20 m wide.

(b) Find the maximum height of the arch. [2 marks]

\[x = \frac{0 + 20}{2} = 10 \qquad h = -0.2(100) + 4(10) = 20 \text{ m}\]

(c) A truck 4 m wide and 18 m tall drives through the centre of the tunnel. Will it fit? Justify your answer. [2 marks]

Centred, the truck's sides are at \(x = 8\) and \(x = 12\). By symmetry the arch is the same height at both:

\[h = -0.2(64) + 4(8) = 19.2 \text{ m}\]

The arch is 19.2 m high at the truck's edges, above 18 m, so the truck fits with 1.2 m to spare.

Theory

Reciprocal relationships and inverse variation

Two quantities are in inverse variation when one increases as the other decreases, in such a way that their product stays constant.

Inverse variation

\[y = \frac{k}{x} \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad xy = k\]

\(k\) is the constant of variation. To find it, multiply any matching pair of \(x\) and \(y\) values.

Double \(x\) and \(y\) halves. Triple \(x\) and \(y\) drops to a third. The graph is a hyperbola — two separate curved branches.

Key features of \(y = k/x\) (for \(k > 0\))

  • Two branches, one in the first quadrant and one in the third.
  • \(x = 0\) is a vertical asymptote — you cannot divide by zero.
  • \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote — \(y\) never reaches zero.
  • There is no \(x\)-intercept and no \(y\)-intercept.

\(y = 12/x\): both branches, with asymptotes along the axes.

Worked example 6 — finding \(k\)

\(y\) varies inversely with \(x\). When \(x = 4\), \(y = 15\). Find \(y\) when \(x = 10\).

Since \(y = k/x\), we have \(k = xy\):

\[k = 4 \times 15 = 60\]

So the relationship is \(y = \dfrac{60}{x}\). When \(x = 10\):

\[y = \frac{60}{10} = 6\]

Worked example 7 — workers and time

It takes 6 workers 20 days to complete a job. Assuming inverse variation, how long would 15 workers take?

Let \(w\) = workers and \(d\) = days. Then \(d = k/w\), so \(k = wd\):

\[k = 6 \times 20 = 120 \ \text{worker-days}\] \[d = \frac{120}{15} = 8 \ \text{days}\]

Interpretation: \(k = 120\) is the total amount of work. More workers means fewer days, but the total worker-days stays the same.

Your turn

  1. \(y\) varies inversely with \(x\), and \(y = 2.5\) when \(x = 16\). Find \(k\), then find \(y\) when \(x = 5\).
    Show working

    \(k = 16 \times 2.5 = 40\), so \(y = \frac{40}{x}\). When \(x = 5\), \(y = 8\).

  2. A trip takes 3 hours at 80 km/h. Time varies inversely with speed. How long does it take at 96 km/h? What does \(k\) represent?
    Show working

    \(k = 80 \times 3 = 240\) — the distance in km. At 96 km/h, \(t = 240 \div 96 = 2.5\) hours.

Theory

Which model fits? Linear, quadratic, exponential or reciprocal

Across this topic you have met four relationships. Exam questions often give a table or a graph and ask which one it is. Each has its own test:

ModelEquationTable test (equal steps in \(x\))Graph
Linear\(y = mx + c\)constant differencesstraight line
Quadratic\(y = ax^2 + bx + c\)constant second differencesparabola with one turning point
Exponential\(y = ka^x\)constant ratiosone asymptote (\(y = 0\)), crosses the \(y\)-axis at \(k\)
Reciprocal\(y = \dfrac{k}{x}\)constant product \(xy\)hyperbola, two asymptotes (both axes), no intercepts

Check the tests in this order: differences, then ratios, then products, then second differences. Stop at the first one that is constant.

Evaluation

Valid domain, range and model limitations

The syllabus asks you to explain the limitations of these models and identify valid domain and range values. The algebra will happily produce answers that make no physical sense — your job is to say which ones count.

Quadratic models

  • Negative values are often invalid. In \(h = -5t^2 + 20t\), the model gives \(h < 0\) for \(t > 4\). The ball does not go underground — the valid domain is \(0 \le t \le 4\), and the range is \(0 \le h \le 20\).
  • Endpoints may be excluded. In the fencing problem the valid domain is \(0 < x < 20\): at \(x = 0\) or \(x = 20\) the pen has zero width and encloses nothing.
  • The symmetry can mislead. A projectile model is symmetric, but real trajectories are distorted by air resistance.

Reciprocal models

  • \(x = 0\) is never in the domain. Division by zero is undefined, and it usually makes no sense in context either — zero workers never finish the job.
  • Negative branches are often irrelevant. For workers and days only \(x > 0\) is meaningful, so only the first-quadrant branch applies.
  • Whole numbers may be required. \(120 \div 7 = 17.14\) days is fine, but 7.5 workers is not.
  • The assumption of perfect inverse variation is strong. Doubling the workforce rarely exactly halves the time — coordination costs, limited equipment and workspace mean the real gain is smaller.

Writing a good limitation answer

State the mathematical fact, then the physical reason, then the consequence. "The model gives \(h = -25\) m at \(t = 5\), but height cannot be negative because the ball stops at ground level, so the model is only valid for \(0 \le t \le 4\)."
