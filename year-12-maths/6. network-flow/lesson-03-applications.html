Method

Solving a full flow problem

The exam routine

  1. Draw the network from the table, with arrows and capacities.
  2. Note the bounds — capacity out of \(S\) and into \(T\).
  3. List the cuts systematically, applying the direction rule.
  4. Identify the minimum — that is the maximum flow.
  5. Name the bottleneck edges and answer in context, with units.

Worked example 1 — a complete question

A courier moves parcels from a depot (S) to a hub (T) through sorting centres A, B and C. Capacities are in hundreds of parcels per day: S→A 9, S→B 12, A→C 7, B→A 4, B→C 8, C→T 13.

Bounds: out of S is \(9 + 12 = 21\); into T is 13. So the flow is at most 13.

Cuts:

Source sideForward edgesCapacity
\(\{S\}\)S→A 9, S→B 1221
\(\{S, A\}\)S→B 12, A→C 719
\(\{S, B\}\)S→A 9, B→A 4, B→C 821
\(\{S, A, B\}\)A→C 7, B→C 815
\(\{S, A, B, C\}\)C→T 1313

Note the cut \(\{S, B\}\): the edge B→A crosses forward (B is on the source side, A is not), so it counts.

Minimum = 13. The maximum flow is 1,300 parcels per day, limited by the single edge C→T.

Method

Constructing the actual flow

Knowing the maximum is 13 is one thing; showing how 13 units actually travel is another, and questions often ask for it.

The method

  1. Find a path from \(S\) to \(T\) with spare capacity on every edge.
  2. Push as much as the narrowest edge on that path allows.
  3. Reduce the remaining capacity on each edge used.
  4. Repeat until no path with spare capacity remains.
  5. Check the total equals the minimum cut — if so, you are done.

Worked example 2

Construct a flow of 13 for the courier network above.

Path 1: S→B→C→T. Narrowest is \(\min(12, 8, 13) = 8\). Push 8.

Remaining: S→B 4, B→C 0, C→T 5.

Path 2: S→A→C→T. Narrowest is \(\min(9, 7, 5) = 5\). Push 5.

Remaining: S→A 4, A→C 2, C→T 0.

No more paths: C→T is full, and every route must use it.

Total flow: \(8 + 5 = 13\) ✓ matching the minimum cut, so this is maximal.

Verify conservation: at C, in \(= 8 + 5 = 13\), out \(= 13\) ✓

Application

Increasing the capacity of an edge

The syllabus asks you to examine the impact of increasing or reducing the flow capacity of an edge. The answer is rarely what students first expect.

The three outcomes

  1. The edge is not in any minimum cut → the maximum flow does not change at all. The upgrade is wasted.
  2. The edge is in the only minimum cut → the flow increases, but only until the next smallest cut becomes binding.
  3. There are several minimum cuts → upgrading one edge changes nothing unless every minimum cut is upgraded together.

Worked example 3

In the courier network, the minimum cut is C→T alone, at 13. The next-smallest cut is \(\{A\to C = 7,\ B\to C = 8\}\) at 15. What happens if C→T is widened to 20?

The cut \(\{S,A,B,C\}\) becomes 20, so it is no longer the minimum. Now the smallest cut is \(\{S, A, B\}\) at 15.

The flow rises from 13 to 15, a gain of 2 — even though C→T gained 7 units of capacity. Five of those units can never be used.

Worked example 4 — a wasted upgrade

What if S→B were widened from 12 to 20 instead?

S→B appears in the cuts \(\{S\}\) (21 → 29) and \(\{S, A\}\) (19 → 27). Neither was the minimum. The cuts \(\{S,A,B\}\) = 15 and \(\{S,A,B,C\}\) = 13 do not involve S→B at all, so they are unchanged.

The maximum flow stays at 13. The upgrade delivers nothing, because the bottleneck is further downstream.

This is the single most important idea in the topic: upgrading a non-bottleneck edge is money wasted.

Application

Reducing capacity — roadworks and failures

The reverse question is just as common: a pipe bursts, a lane closes, a server fails. What happens to the maximum flow?

The two outcomes

  • If the edge had spare capacity in the maximum flow, a small reduction may change nothing.
  • If the edge was running at capacity in every maximum flow, the maximum flow falls.

Recalculate the cuts with the new capacity — that always gives the correct answer.

Worked example 5

In the courier network, roadworks cut B→C from 8 to 3. Find the new maximum flow.

Recalculate the affected cuts:

  • \(\{S, A, B\}\): \(7 + 3 = 10\) (was 15)
  • \(\{S, B\}\): \(9 + 4 + 3 = 16\) (was 21)
  • \(\{S, A, B, C\}\): 13 (unchanged)

The new minimum is 10, down from 13 — a loss of 300 parcels per day. B→C has become the bottleneck.

Worked example 6 — a reduction with no effect

What if S→B were reduced from 12 to 10 instead?

\(\{S\}\) becomes 19 and \(\{S, A\}\) becomes 17. Both still exceed the minimum of 13, and the cuts \(\{S,A,B\}\) = 15 and \(\{S,A,B,C\}\) = 13 are unaffected.

The maximum flow stays at 13. S→B had spare capacity, so losing some of it costs nothing.

Your turn

  1. In the courier network, a breakdown reduces A→C from 7 to 3. Find the new maximum flow.
    Show working

    \(\{S, A, B\}\): \(3 + 8 = 11\). \(\{S, A\}\): \(12 + 3 = 15\). Others are 13 or more.

    New maximum flow: 11 — 200 parcels a day lost.

  2. Instead, S→A is reduced from 9 to 6. What happens?
    Show working

    \(\{S\}\) becomes 18 and \(\{S, B\}\) becomes 18 — both still above 13. The maximum flow stays at 13.

Method

More than one source or sink

Real networks often have two reservoirs, or two towns to supply. The trick is to turn the problem back into a single source and a single sink:

  • Several sources: add a super-source S with an edge to each real source. Give each new edge the most that source can supply.
  • Several sinks: add a super-sink T with an edge from each real sink. Give each new edge that sink's demand.

Then find the minimum cut as usual.

Worked example 7

Reservoirs R (can supply 10 ML/day) and Q (12 ML/day) feed a town T through junctions A and B: R→A 8, Q→A 5, Q→B 9, A→T 10, B→T 7. Find the maximum supply to the town.

Add S→R 10 and S→Q 12. Checking the cuts, the smallest is the one into the town: A→T and B→T, \(10 + 7 = 17\). The next smallest is 19.

Maximum supply: 17 ML/day, although the reservoirs could provide 22. The pipes into the town are the bottleneck.

Your turn

  1. A depot S supplies towns X (demand 9) and Y (demand 12): S→A 15, S→B 10, A→X 9, A→Y 7, B→Y 8. Add a super-sink and decide whether both demands can be met.
    Show working

    Add X→T 9 and Y→T 12. The minimum cut is 21 (for example X→T and Y→T together), which equals the total demand \(9 + 12 = 21\). Both towns can be supplied in full.

  2. Why are the super-source edges given the reservoirs' supply limits, rather than left unlimited?
    Show working

    A reservoir cannot release more than it holds. Capping each super-source edge makes the model respect that limit — otherwise the cuts could report a flow the reservoirs cannot actually supply.

Evaluation

Does capacity meet demand?

The final dot-point asks you to explain why the flow capacity of a network is or is not sufficient to meet demand. This is a written answer, and it needs structure.

The four-part answer

  1. State the maximum flow and how you found it (the minimum cut).
  2. Compare it with demand, giving the surplus or shortfall numerically.
  3. Name the bottleneck edges — the minimum cut.
  4. Recommend which edge to upgrade and by how much, noting the next constraint.

Worked example 8 — a model answer

The courier network has a maximum flow of 13 (hundred parcels/day). Demand is 1,600 parcels per day. Advise the company.

"The minimum cut is the single edge C→T with capacity 13, so the network can carry at most 1,300 parcels per day. Demand is 1,600 parcels per day, so the network is insufficient, falling 300 parcels per day short.

The bottleneck is C→T, the only route into the hub. Widening it would raise the flow, but only to 15 (1,500 parcels/day), because the cut \(\{A\to C,\ B\to C\}\) at 15 then becomes binding. To reach 1,600 the company must widen C→T to at least 16 and increase A→C or B→C by a further 1, since both cuts must exceed 16."

Notice it states the number, compares it, names the bottleneck, and looks ahead to the next constraint.

Worked example 9 — exam style

A water network (ML/day) has S→A 45, S→B 35, A→B 10, A→C 30, B→C 15, B→D 25, C→T 40, D→T 30. A city needs 72 ML/day.

(a) Find the maximum flow. [2 marks]

The minimum cut has source side \(\{S, A, B, C\}\): C→T 40 + B→D 25 = 65. (Next are 70 and 70.) Maximum flow: 65 ML/day.

(b) Can widening a single pipe meet demand? [2 marks]

No. Widening C→T leaves the cut \(\{S, A, B\}\) (A→C 30 + B→C 15 + B→D 25 = 70) binding; widening B→D leaves the sink cut (C→T 40 + D→T 30 = 70) binding. The best single upgrade reaches 70.

(c) Find the smallest total widening that meets demand. [2 marks]

Three cuts must all reach 72: B→D needs at least 27 (+2), C→T at least 42 (+2), and \(\text{C→T} + \text{B→D} \ge 72\). For example B→D +2 and C→T +5: 7 ML/day of extra capacity in total.

Class activity

Which failure hurts most?

About 10 minutes · groups of three. Use the demand analyser with the courier network (S→A 9, S→B 12, A→C 7, B→A 4, B→C 8, C→T 13) and demand 13. Roadworks will reduce one edge by 4 units (to no less than 1).

  1. Share the six edges around the group. For each, reduce it by 4, record the new maximum flow, then reset.
  2. Rank the six failures from most to least damaging.
  3. Some failures cause no loss at all. Explain why, using the idea of spare capacity.
  4. The company can protect one edge with a backup route. Which should it protect, and why?
