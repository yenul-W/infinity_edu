Theory

What is a cut?

Definition

A cut is a line drawn through a network that completely separates the source from the sink. Every possible route from \(S\) to \(T\) must cross it.

Think of it as slicing the network in two: all the source-side vertices on one side, all the sink-side vertices on the other. Since every drop of flow must cross that line, the total capacity crossing it limits the flow.

The test for a valid cut

A cut is valid only if no path from \(S\) to \(T\) survives. If you can trace even one route from source to sink without crossing the line, the cut is invalid and its capacity is meaningless.

Two valid cuts. Cut 1 crosses S→A and S→B; Cut 2 crosses C→T and D→T.

Worked example 1

Verify that Cut 1 above is valid, and find its capacity.

Validity: the only edges leaving S are S→A and S→B, and the cut crosses both. No route from S to T avoids it, so it is valid.

Capacity: \(10 + 8 = 18\).

Cut 2 crosses C→T and D→T, the only edges into T, so its capacity is \(7 + 11 = 18\) as well.

Method

Capacity of a cut

Capacity of a cut

\[C(\text{cut}) = \sum \text{capacities of edges crossing from the source side to the sink side}\]

Add only the edges pointing from the source side toward the sink side.

Worked example 2

In the network above, find the capacity of the cut that separates \(\{S, A\}\) from \(\{B, C, D, T\}\).

Edges from the \(\{S, A\}\) side to the other side:

  • S→B, capacity 8
  • A→B, capacity 4
  • A→C, capacity 6
\[C = 8 + 4 + 6 = 18\]

Worked example 3

Find the capacity of the cut separating \(\{S, A, B\}\) from \(\{C, D, T\}\).

Edges crossing forward: A→C (6) and B→D (9). The edge A→B stays entirely on the source side, so it is not counted.

\[C = 6 + 9 = 15\]

This is smaller than the 18 found for the other cuts — a tighter constraint.

A common error

Only count edges that actually cross the cut line. Edges with both endpoints on the same side are irrelevant, no matter how small their capacity.

Theory

The direction rule

Forward and backward edges

An edge crossing the cut from the source side to the sink side is a forward edge — count its capacity.

An edge crossing backward, from the sink side to the source side, is a backward edge — count it as zero.

Why? A cut measures how much can get through toward the sink. An edge pointing backward carries flow the wrong way, so it contributes nothing to getting material across.

Worked example 4

A cut separates \(\{S, A\}\) from \(\{B, T\}\). The crossing edges are A→B (capacity 12), S→B (capacity 7) and B→A (capacity 5). Find the capacity of the cut.

A→B and S→B point forward, so they count. B→A points backward (from the sink side to the source side), so it contributes 0.

\[C = 12 + 7 + 0 = 19\]

Method

Finding the minimum cut

The systematic method

  1. List every valid cut. A cut corresponds to a choice of which vertices sit on the source side (S must be included, T must not).
  2. Calculate each capacity, applying the direction rule.
  3. The smallest is the minimum cut — and by the theorem, that value is the maximum flow.

Worked example 5 — a full analysis

For the network at the top of this lesson (S→A 10, S→B 8, A→B 4, A→C 6, B→D 9, C→T 7, D→T 11), list all cuts and find the maximum flow.

Source sideForward edgesCapacity
\(\{S\}\)S→A 10, S→B 818
\(\{S, A\}\)S→B 8, A→B 4, A→C 618
\(\{S, B\}\)S→A 10, B→D 919
\(\{S, A, B\}\)A→C 6, B→D 915
\(\{S, A, B, C\}\)C→T 7, B→D 916
\(\{S, A, B, D\}\)A→C 6, D→T 1117
\(\{S, A, B, C, D\}\)C→T 7, D→T 1118

The minimum is 15, at the cut separating \(\{S, A, B\}\) from \(\{C, D, T\}\).

Therefore the maximum flow is 15 units. The edges A→C and B→D form the bottleneck — every route must use one of them.

A practical shortcut

You rarely need every cut. Look for places where the network narrows — a small number of edges that all routes must pass through. Always check the cut right at the source and the one right at the sink, then any obvious narrow waists in between.

Theory

The maximum-flow minimum-cut theorem

The theorem

\[\text{Maximum flow} = \text{Capacity of the minimum cut}\]

The greatest flow that can pass from source to sink equals exactly the capacity of the smallest cut. They are always the same number.

Why it must be true. Every unit of flow crosses every cut, so the flow can never exceed any cut's capacity — in particular the smallest. That explains why max flow \(\le\) min cut. The deeper part of the theorem is that this bound is always achievable: you can always find a flow that actually reaches it.

What this gives you

  • A guaranteed answer — no need to hunt for clever paths.
  • A way to prove your flow is maximal: exhibit a cut with the same capacity.
  • The bottleneck identified — the minimum cut's edges are exactly the ones to upgrade.

Worked example 6 — using the theorem to advise

The network in Worked example 5 has a maximum flow of 15, limited by the cut \(\{A\to C = 6,\ B\to D = 9\}\). A council wants to increase throughput. What should it do?

Upgrade an edge in the minimum cut. Increasing A→C from 6 to 10 raises that cut's capacity to \(10 + 9 = 19\).

But the maximum flow does not become 19 — the next-smallest cut now binds. From the table, the cut \(\{S\}\) has capacity 18 and \(\{S, A, B, C\}\) becomes \(7 + 9 = 16\). So the new maximum flow is 16.

The lesson: upgrading a bottleneck moves the constraint elsewhere. Gains are limited by the next smallest cut, so improvements are usually smaller than expected.

Worked example 7 — exam style

A stadium (S) is evacuated to a transport hub (T) through junctions A to E. Capacities in people per hour:

EdgeS→AS→BS→CA→DB→DB→EC→ED→TE→T
Capacity8009005006004007004509001000

(a) Show that the maximum flow is 1,900 people per hour. [2 marks]

The cut at the sink crosses D→T and E→T: \(900 + 1000 = 1\,900\). Checking the other cuts, the next smallest are \(\{S, A, C\}\) (S→B 900, A→D 600, C→E 450 = 1,950) and the source cut (2,200). The minimum cut is 1,900, so the maximum flow is 1,900.

(b) Planners need 2,100 people per hour. They widen E→T to 1,300. Is this enough? [2 marks]

The sink cut rises to \(900 + 1300 = 2\,200\), but the cut \(\{S, A, C\}\) is still 1,950. The new maximum flow is 1,950 — still 150 short. A second upgrade on that cut (for example A→D) is also needed.

Method

Building the maximum flow path by path

The minimum cut tells you the maximum flow. To show how that flow is achieved, build it one path at a time:

  1. Choose any path from S to T with spare capacity on every edge.
  2. Send its bottleneck amount (the smallest spare capacity along the path).
  3. Reduce the spare capacity on each edge of that path.
  4. Repeat until no path from S to T has spare capacity everywhere.
  5. Add the amounts sent, then confirm with a cut of the same capacity.

Worked example 8

Build a maximum flow for the network at the top of this lesson.

PathSpare capacitiesSend
S→A→C→T10, 6, 76
S→B→D→T8, 9, 118
S→A→B→D→T4, 4, 1, 31

Now A→C (0 spare) and B→D (0 spare) are full, so no path remains. Total flow \(= 6 + 8 + 1 = 15\), matching the minimum cut of 15 — so it is maximal.

Class activity

The engineer's challenge

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the minimum cut finder, starting from the original capacities (S→A 10, S→B 8, A→B 4, A→C 6, B→D 9, C→T 7, D→T 11), maximum flow 15.

  1. Add 5 units to S→A. What happens to the maximum flow? Why?
  2. Reset. Try adding 5 units to each edge in turn, one at a time. Which single upgrade helps most?
  3. The challenge: raise the maximum flow to 18 by adding as few units of capacity as possible, spread over any edges. Record your best total.
  4. When you reach 18, how many cuts have capacity exactly 18? What does that tell you about upgrading further?
Discussion points

1. Nothing changes: S→A is not in the minimum cut, so the maximum flow stays 15.

2. B→D +5 gives 17 (the next cut, \(\{S, A, B, D\}\), binds). A→C +5 gives 16. Every other single upgrade leaves 15.

3. The best is 3 units: A→C +1 and B→D +2. This is the only 3-unit solution.

4. Three cuts tie at 18: \(\{S\}\), \(\{S, A, B\}\) and \(\{S, A, B, C\}\). Going any higher requires upgrades across several cuts at once, so each further unit becomes expensive.
