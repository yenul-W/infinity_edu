Network Flow Terminology

Water through pipes, data through cables, traffic through roads. A flow network models any system where something moves from a source to a sink through channels of limited capacity.

Theory

Directed networks

In Year 11 you met networks of vertices joined by edges. A flow network adds two things: direction and capacity.

Directed and weighted

A directed network has arrows on its edges — flow can only travel the way the arrow points.
A weighted network has a number on each edge. In a flow network that number is the flow capacity: the maximum that can pass along that edge per unit of time.

A flow network. S is the source, T is the sink, and each edge is labelled with its capacity.

Theory

The key terms

Flow network vocabulary

  • Source — where the flow originates. It has edges leaving it but none entering. Usually labelled \(S\).
  • Sink — the destination. It has edges entering but none leaving. Usually labelled \(T\).
  • Flow capacity — the maximum rate that can pass along an edge, shown as the edge's weight.
  • Flow — the amount actually travelling along an edge. It can never exceed the capacity.
  • Cut — a line drawn through the network that separates the source from the sink completely.
  • Capacity of a cut — the sum of the capacities of the edges the cut crosses, counting only those flowing from the source side to the sink side.

Everything in this topic comes back to one question: what is the greatest rate that can travel from source to sink? That is the maximum flow.

An important limit

The maximum flow can never exceed the total capacity of the edges leaving the source, nor the total capacity of the edges entering the sink. Those two numbers give you instant upper bounds — always calculate them as a sanity check.

Theory

What makes a flow valid

The two rules every flow must obey

  1. Capacity rule. The flow along an edge can never exceed that edge's capacity.
  2. Conservation rule. At every vertex except the source and the sink, flow in = flow out. Nothing accumulates and nothing appears from nowhere.

Worked example 3

A vertex has two edges entering it carrying 6 and 5 units, and two edges leaving with capacities 8 and 7. How much must leave, and is it possible?

Flow in: \(6 + 5 = 11\) units, so 11 units must leave.

Capacity out: \(8 + 7 = 15\) units.

Since \(11 \le 15\), it is possible. The 11 units could split as 8 and 3, or 7 and 4, or any combination totalling 11 within the limits.

Worked example 4 — an impossible situation

A vertex receives 14 units but its outgoing edges have capacities 6 and 5. What happens?

Only \(6 + 5 = 11\) units can leave, but 14 arrive. Conservation is violated, so the flow of 14 into that vertex is not achievable. In practice the incoming flow would be limited to 11 — this vertex is a bottleneck.

Method

Using conservation to find unknown flows

Exam questions often show a flow with some values missing. Conservation at each intermediate vertex gives an equation — solve them one vertex at a time, starting where only one value is unknown.

The routine

  1. Find a vertex with only one unknown flow touching it.
  2. Write flow in = flow out and solve.
  3. Move to the next vertex and repeat.
  4. Check every flow is within its capacity, and that flow out of S equals flow into T.

Worked example 5

A network carries these flows (flow/capacity): S→A 12/15, S→B \(x\)/10, A→B 4/6, A→T \(y\)/9, B→T 13/14. Find \(x\) and \(y\).

At A: in 12, out \(4 + y\). So \(12 = 4 + y\), giving \(y = 8\). (Within capacity 9 ✓)

At B: in \(x + 4\), out 13. So \(x = 9\). (Within capacity 10 ✓)

Check: out of S \(= 12 + 9 = 21\); into T \(= 8 + 13 = 21\) ✓

Your turn

  1. Flows: S→A 10, S→B \(x\), A→C 6, A→B \(y\), B→C 7, B→T 5, C→T \(z\). Find \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).
    Show working

    At A: \(10 = 6 + y \Rightarrow y = 4\).

    At B: \(x + 4 = 7 + 5 \Rightarrow x = 8\).

    At C: \(6 + 7 = z \Rightarrow z = 13\).

    Check: out of S \(= 18\), into T \(= 13 + 5 = 18\) ✓

  2. In the worked example, could the total flow be increased by sending more along S→B? Explain.
    Show working

    S→B has 1 spare unit (9/10), and B→T has 1 spare (13/14). So 1 more unit can travel S→B→T, raising the total to 22.

Application

Finding simple bottlenecks

A bottleneck is the constraint that limits the whole network. Before learning the formal cut method in Lesson 2, you can find the maximum flow on small networks by reasoning about the paths.

Flow along a single path

The flow along a path is limited by its smallest capacity edge — the narrowest pipe in the chain. A path with capacities 12, 5 and 9 can carry only 5 units.

Worked example 6

Find the maximum flow in a network with two independent paths: S→A→T with capacities 9 and 6, and S→B→T with capacities 4 and 7.

Path 1: limited by the smaller of 9 and 6, so 6 units.

Path 2: limited by the smaller of 4 and 7, so 4 units.

The paths do not share any edges, so the flows add:

\[\text{Maximum flow} = 6 + 4 = 10 \text{ units}\]

Check against the bounds: capacity out of S is \(9 + 4 = 13\); capacity into T is \(6 + 7 = 13\). Both exceed 10, so 10 is consistent.

Worked example 7 — in context

A water authority pumps from a reservoir (S) to a town (T) through two pipelines. Route 1 has sections of 400 and 350 kL/h; route 2 has sections of 280 and 310 kL/h. The town needs 700 kL/h. Can demand be met?

Route 1: \(\min(400, 350) = 350\) kL/h.

Route 2: \(\min(280, 310) = 280\) kL/h.

Total: \(350 + 280 = 630\) kL/h.

Since \(630 < 700\), demand cannot be met — the network falls 70 kL/h short. Upgrading the 280 kL/h section to at least 350 kL/h would lift the total to 700 kL/h.

Worked example 8 — exam style

Three independent pipelines carry gas from a gas field (S) to a city (T). Capacities are in terajoules per day (TJ/day):

RouteSection capacities
1120, 90, 150
280, 110
3100, 70, 95

(a) Find the maximum flow. [1 mark]

Bottlenecks: 90, 80 and 70, so the maximum flow is \(90 + 80 + 70 = 240\) TJ/day.

(b) The city needs 280 TJ/day. Can a single section upgrade meet demand? [2 marks]

The shortfall is 40. Upgrading a bottleneck helps only until the next-smallest section on that route: route 1 can gain at most 30 (90 → 120), route 2 at most 30 (80 → 110), route 3 at most 25 (70 → 95). No single upgrade gives 40, so no.

(c) Upgrades cost $2 million per 10 TJ/day. Find the minimum cost to meet demand. [1 mark]

Any combination adding 40 TJ/day, for example route 1 +30 and route 3 +10, costs \(4 \times \$2\text{M} = \$8\) million.

Class activity

Upgrade the network on a budget

About 10 minutes · pairs. In the two-path flow explorer, set S→A 12, A→T 7, S→B 5, B→T 9. Each extra unit of capacity on any edge costs $1,000.

  1. Find the current maximum flow.
  2. Demand is 20. Find the cheapest set of upgrades that meets it. What does it cost?
  3. Demand rises to 25. Find the cheapest plan now. Why does each extra unit eventually cost more?
  4. A rival suggests adding 20 units to S→A for $20,000. Explain why that wastes money.
Discussion points

1. Path 1 gives \(\min(12, 7) = 7\); path 2 gives \(\min(5, 9) = 5\); maximum flow 12.

2. 8 more units are needed. Raise A→T from 7 to 12 (+5, $5,000) and S→B from 5 to 8 (+3, $3,000): $8,000 in total. Only bottleneck upgrades help.

3. Raising A→T to 12 and S→B to 9 gives 21 for $9,000. Beyond that, both edges on a path are equal, so each extra unit needs both edges raised — $2,000 per unit. 4 more units cost $8,000: $17,000 in total.

4. S→A is not the bottleneck on path 1 (A→T is). Adding capacity there changes nothing.
