Theory

Multistage events

What they are

A multistage event happens in two or more steps: tossing a coin twice, drawing two marbles, or taking a test then a re-test. Each stage has its own outcomes, and the combinations build up quickly.

Two tools organise them: arrays (tables) work well for exactly two stages with few outcomes; tree diagrams work for any number of stages and handle changing probabilities.

The multiplication rule for independent events

\[P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)\]

This applies when the events are independent — the outcome of the first does not change the probability of the second.

Worked example 1

A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. Find \(P(\text{head and a six})\).

The coin does not affect the die, so the events are independent:

\[P = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{12}\]

Your turn

  1. A spinner lands on red with probability 0.3, and a die is rolled. Find \(P(\text{red and an even number})\).
    Show working

    Independent: \(0.3 \times \frac12 = 0.15\).

  2. Are the two stages independent? (a) Toss a coin, then roll a die. (b) Draw two cards from a deck without putting the first back.
    Show working

    (a) Yes — the coin cannot affect the die. (b) No — the first card changes what is left in the deck.

Method

Arrays and tables

An array lists one stage down the side and the other across the top, with every combination in the body. It is ideal for two dice or two spinners.

When arrays fall short

Arrays only handle two stages, and they assume the probabilities stay the same. For three or more stages, or for drawing without replacement, use a tree diagram.

    Show working
    1234
    12345
    23456
    34567

    Sum 5 appears 3 times out of 12: \(P = \frac{3}{12} = \frac14\).

  2. For the same spinners, find the probability that the product is even.
    Show working

    The product is odd only when both are odd: \(2 \times 2 = 4\) cells. So \(P(\text{even}) = 1 - \frac{4}{12} = \frac{2}{3}\).

Method

Tree diagrams

Building a tree

  1. Draw one branch per outcome of the first stage, writing its probability on the branch.
  2. From the end of each branch, draw the outcomes of the second stage.
  3. Continue for every stage.
  4. The probabilities leaving any single point must sum to 1.

Two coin tosses. Each path's probability is the product along its branches, and the four results sum to 1.

A check worth doing

The probabilities of all the final paths must sum to 1. If they do not, a branch probability is wrong.

Method

Multiply along, add between

The two operations

Multiply along a path (left to right) to find the probability of that complete sequence — the word is "and".

Add between paths to combine several ways of getting the same result — the word is "or".

Worked example 3

A coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of exactly one head.

Two paths give exactly one head: \(HT\) and \(TH\).

\[P(HT) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 \qquad P(TH) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14\] \[P(\text{exactly one head}) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12\]

Worked example 4 — unequal branches

A biased coin has \(P(H) = 0.7\). It is tossed twice. Find the probability of exactly one head.

\[P(HT) = 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.21\] \[P(TH) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21\] \[P(\text{exactly one head}) = 0.21 + 0.21 = 0.42\]

Check all four paths: \(HH = 0.49\), \(HT = 0.21\), \(TH = 0.21\), \(TT = 0.09\), summing to \(1.00\) ✓

Your turn

  1. The probability of rain is 0.3 on Saturday and 0.4 on Sunday, independently. Find the probability it rains on exactly one day.
    Show working

    Rain then dry: \(0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.18\). Dry then rain: \(0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.28\).

    Add: \(0.18 + 0.28 = 0.46\).

  2. Find the probability of rain on neither day, and check that all the outcomes sum to 1.
    Show working

    Neither: \(0.7 \times 0.6 = 0.42\). Both: \(0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12\).

    \(0.12 + 0.46 + 0.42 = 1\) ✓

Theory

With and without replacement

The crucial distinction

With replacement — the item is returned before the next draw. The total stays the same, and the stages are independent.

Without replacement — the item is kept out. Both the favourable count and the total decrease by one, so the second probability depends on the first.

Worked example 5 — with replacement

A bag has 4 red and 6 blue marbles. Two are drawn with replacement. Find \(P(\text{both red})\).

The bag is identical for both draws:

\[P = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{4}{10} = \frac{16}{100} = 0.16\]

Worked example 6 — without replacement

Same bag, but the first marble is not replaced. Find \(P(\text{both red})\).

After a red is removed, 3 reds remain out of 9 marbles:

\[P = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{3}{9} = \frac{12}{90} = \frac{2}{15} \approx 0.1333\]

Note both numbers changed: the reds fell from 4 to 3 and the total from 10 to 9. Changing only one is the most common error.

Worked example 7 — a full without-replacement question

From the same bag (4 red, 6 blue), two marbles are drawn without replacement. Find the probability they are different colours.

Two paths give different colours: red then blue, or blue then red.

\[P(RB) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{6}{9} = \frac{24}{90}\] \[P(BR) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{24}{90}\] \[P(\text{different}) = \frac{24}{90} + \frac{24}{90} = \frac{48}{90} = \frac{8}{15}\]

Alternative using the complement: \(P(RR) = \frac{12}{90}\) and \(P(BB) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90}\), so \(P(\text{same}) = \frac{42}{90}\) and \(P(\text{different}) = 1 - \frac{42}{90} = \frac{48}{90}\) ✓

Worked example 8 — exam style

A box of 12 light globes contains 3 faulty ones. Two globes are chosen at random, without replacement.

(a) Find the probability that both are faulty. [1 mark]

\[\frac{3}{12} \times \frac{2}{11} = \frac{6}{132} = \frac{1}{22}\]

(b) Find the probability that exactly one is faulty. [2 marks]

\[P(FW) + P(WF) = \frac{3}{12} \times \frac{9}{11} + \frac{9}{12} \times \frac{3}{11} = \frac{54}{132} = \frac{9}{22}\]

(c) Find the probability that at least one works. [1 mark]

\[1 - P(\text{both faulty}) = 1 - \frac{1}{22} = \frac{21}{22}\]

(d) Would both being faulty be more or less likely with replacement? Explain. [1 mark]

More likely: \(\left(\frac{3}{12}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{16}\), compared with \(\frac{1}{22}\). Without replacement, the first faulty globe leaves fewer faulty globes for the second draw.

Your turn

  1. A box holds 3 dark and 5 milk chocolates. Two are eaten, one after the other. Find \(P(\text{both dark})\) and \(P(\text{at least one dark})\).
    Show working

    \(P(\text{both dark}) = \frac38 \times \frac27 = \frac{6}{56} = \frac{3}{28}\).

    \(P(\text{at least one dark}) = 1 - \frac58 \times \frac47 = 1 - \frac{20}{56} = \frac{9}{14}\).

  2. If the first chocolate were put back before the second was chosen, what would \(P(\text{both dark})\) be?
    Show working

    \(\left(\frac38\right)^2 = \frac{9}{64}\).

Method

Three or more stages

Trees extend to any number of stages: three stages with two outcomes each gives \(2^3 = 8\) paths. You rarely need to draw them all — list only the paths you need.

Counting the paths

For "exactly two successes in three stages", the success and failure can be arranged as SSF, SFS or FSS — three paths, each with the same probability when the stages are independent and identical.

Worked example 9

A netballer scores each shot with probability 0.8, independently. She takes three shots.

Exactly two goals: paths GGM, GMG, MGG, each \(0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.128\).

\[P(\text{exactly 2}) = 3 \times 0.128 = 0.384\]

At least two goals: exactly two or all three:

\[0.384 + 0.8^3 = 0.384 + 0.512 = 0.896\]

Your turn

  1. Each of three sets of traffic lights is green with probability 0.4, independently. Find \(P(\text{all green})\) and \(P(\text{exactly one green})\).
    Show working

    All green: \(0.4^3 = 0.064\).

    Exactly one: three paths, each \(0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.144\), so \(3 \times 0.144 = 0.432\).

  2. Three students are chosen at random, without replacement, from 5 girls and 4 boys. Find the probability all three are girls.
    Show working
    \[\frac59 \times \frac48 \times \frac37 = \frac{60}{504} = \frac{5}{42}\]

Class activity

When does replacement matter?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the replacement comparator. Every bag below is 40% red.

  1. Record \(P(\text{both red})\) with and without replacement for bags of 2 red + 3 blue, 4 red + 6 blue, 6 red + 9 blue and 10 red + 15 blue.
  2. Why is the "with replacement" value the same for every bag?
  3. What happens to the gap between the two columns as the bag gets bigger? Explain why.
  4. A pollster selects 2 people from a city of 1 million without replacement. Would treating the selections as independent cause a noticeable error? Explain.
