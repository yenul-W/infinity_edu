Theory

Relative frequency

Relative frequency

\[\text{Relative frequency} = \frac{\text{number of times the event occurred}}{\text{total number of trials}}\]

Also called experimental probability. It is an estimate of the true probability, based on what actually happened.

Theoretical probabilityRelative frequency
Based onCounting equally likely outcomesObserving actual trials
RequiresFair, equally likely outcomesNothing — works for any situation
Example\(P(\text{six}) = \frac{1}{6}\) on a fair dieA drawing pin landed point-up 63 times in 100
AccuracyExactAn estimate, improving with more trials

When relative frequency is the only option

Use it whenever outcomes are not equally likely and cannot be counted: a biased die, a drawing pin, whether a bus is late, whether a patient responds to a drug. There is no theoretical calculation available — only data.

Worked example 1

A drawing pin is dropped 200 times and lands point-up 118 times. Estimate the probability of landing point-up.

\[\text{Relative frequency} = \frac{118}{200} = 0.59\]

There is no way to calculate this theoretically — the pin's shape and mass distribution determine it, so it must be measured.

Worked example 2 — testing for bias

A die is rolled 600 times and shows a six 142 times. Is it fair?

Relative frequency: \(\dfrac{142}{600} = 0.2367\).

Theoretical for a fair die: \(\dfrac{1}{6} = 0.1667\).

Expected number of sixes: \(600 \times \frac{1}{6} = 100\). Observing 142 is 42 more than expected — a large excess over 600 trials, which suggests the die is biased towards six. With a large sample like this, a gap this size is unlikely to be chance alone.

Theory

Why more trials give better estimates

The key principle

As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency gets closer in value to the theoretical probability. Short runs are erratic; long runs settle down.

Worked example 3

Method

Expected frequency

Expected frequency

\[\text{Expected frequency} = np\]

\(n\) is the number of times the experiment is repeated, and \(p\) is the probability the event occurs each time.

Worked example 4

A fair die is rolled 300 times. How many sixes would you expect?

\[np = 300 \times \frac{1}{6} = 50\]

Worked example 5

A factory produces items with a 3% defect rate. In a batch of 1,200, how many defective items are expected?

\[np = 1200 \times 0.03 = 36\]

What "expected" really means

The expected frequency is a long-run average, not a guarantee. Rolling 300 times will rarely give exactly 50 sixes — it will give something near it. Expected values can also be non-whole numbers: an expectation of 2.5 children per family is meaningful as an average even though no family has 2.5 children.

Worked example 6 — working backwards

A biased coin gives 340 heads in 500 tosses. Estimate \(p\), then predict the heads in 1,200 tosses.

Estimate: \(p = \dfrac{340}{500} = 0.68\).

Predict: \(np = 1200 \times 0.68 = 816\) heads.

Your turn

  1. Records show a 0.28 chance of rain on any July day in a town. How many rainy days are expected in July (31 days)?
    Show working

    \(np = 31 \times 0.28 = 8.68\), so about 9 days.

  2. A raffle sells 2,500 tickets and you buy 20. What is your chance of winning? If you entered 250 such raffles, how many would you expect to win?
    Show working

    \(p = \frac{20}{2500} = 0.008\). Expected wins: \(250 \times 0.008 = 2\).

Method

Two-way tables

What they show

A two-way table cross-classifies data by two attributes, with row and column totals in the margins. The syllabus limits this to combinations of two attributes.

Worked example 7

120 students were surveyed about whether they play sport and whether they play a musical instrument.

Plays sportNo sportTotal
Plays instrument282250
No instrument472370
Total7545120

One student is chosen at random.

(a) \(P(\text{plays sport})\): \(\dfrac{75}{120} = 0.625\)

(b) \(P(\text{both}):\) \(\dfrac{28}{120} \approx 0.2333\)

(c) \(P(\text{neither}):\) \(\dfrac{23}{120} \approx 0.1917\)

(d) \(P(\text{sport or instrument})\): everyone except the 23 who do neither, so \(\dfrac{97}{120} \approx 0.8083\)

Completing a table

Every row and column must add to its total, and the two grand totals must agree. Given enough entries you can always fill in the rest by subtraction — start wherever only one value is missing from a line.

Method

Venn diagrams

The syllabus pairs two-way tables with Venn diagrams. They hold the same four numbers, arranged differently: two overlapping circles inside a rectangle that stands for everyone.

The 120 students from Worked example 7. The overlap is "both"; outside both circles is "neither".

Reading the regions

  • A and B — the overlap.
  • A only — the part of A's circle outside the overlap.
  • A or B — everything inside either circle (including the overlap).
  • Neither — inside the rectangle, outside both circles.
\[P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)\]

Subtract the overlap because adding \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) counts it twice.

Worked example 8

Using the Venn diagram, find \(P(\text{sport or instrument})\) with the formula.

\[\frac{75}{120} + \frac{50}{120} - \frac{28}{120} = \frac{97}{120} \approx 0.8083\]

This matches the two-way table answer (everyone except the 23 who do neither) ✓

Your turn

  1. Of 60 people, 35 like tea, 28 like coffee and 12 like both. Find the number in each region of a Venn diagram.
    Show working

    Both 12; tea only \(35 - 12 = 23\); coffee only \(28 - 12 = 16\); neither \(60 - (23 + 12 + 16) = 9\).

  2. Find \(P(\text{tea or coffee})\) in two ways.
    Show working

    Formula: \(\frac{35 + 28 - 12}{60} = \frac{51}{60} = 0.85\). Complement: \(1 - \frac{9}{60} = 0.85\) ✓

Evaluation

Probability in society

The syllabus asks you to examine the use of statistics and the associated probabilities in shaping decisions made by the media, governments and companies.

WhoUse of probabilityWhat to watch for
Insurance Premiums set from the probability of a claim and its expected cost Premiums must exceed the expected payout, or the insurer fails
Medicine Drug approval from trial success rates and side-effect probabilities Sample size; whether risks are stated in absolute or relative terms
Government Policy from projected risks — disease spread, road tolls, climate The assumptions behind the model
Media Headlines from risk statistics Relative risk sounds alarming where absolute risk is tiny
Gambling Odds set so the expected return to the player is negative The house edge is built into every game

Worked example 9 — relative versus absolute risk

A headline reads: "New study: this food doubles your risk of a rare disease!" The disease affects 2 in 100,000 people. Evaluate.

"Doubling the risk raises it from 2 in 100,000 to 4 in 100,000 — an absolute increase of just 0.002%. The relative increase of 100% sounds dramatic, but the actual change in risk is negligible.

Reporting only the relative risk is misleading. A reader should ask for the absolute numbers, the sample size, and whether the study was observational (which cannot establish causation) or a controlled trial."

Worked example 10 — insurance

An insurer estimates a 1.2% annual chance of a $40,000 claim. What is the expected payout per policy, and what does this imply for the premium?

\[\text{Expected payout} = 0.012 \times 40\,000 = \$480\]

The premium must exceed $480 to cover administration, profit and the risk of worse-than-expected years. A premium of, say, $700 gives a $220 margin per policy — which is why insurers need large numbers of policies for the average to be reliable.

Worked example 11 — exam style

A store sells a television for $1,200 and offers a 3-year extended warranty for $180. Its records show that 6% of these televisions need a repair in that time, at an average cost of $450.

(a) Find the expected repair cost per television. [1 mark]

\(0.06 \times 450 = \$27\).

(b) Find the store's expected profit per warranty sold. [1 mark]

\(180 - 27 = \$153\).

(c) Should a customer buy the warranty? Discuss. [2 marks]

On average, no: the customer pays $180 to cover an expected cost of $27 — an expected loss of $153. But averages hide the individual risk: a customer who could not afford a $450 repair might still choose the certainty of the warranty. The store relies on selling many warranties so that the average holds.

Your turn

  1. A treatment lowers the risk of a condition from 12 in 10,000 to 9 in 10,000. Give the relative and absolute reductions.
    Show working

    Relative: \(\frac{3}{12} = 25\%\). Absolute: 3 in 10,000, which is 0.03 percentage points.

  2. A game costs $2. There is a 0.15 chance of winning $10, otherwise nothing. Find the expected result per game.
    Show working

    Expected winnings: \(0.15 \times 10 = \$1.50\). Expected result: \(1.50 - 2 = -\$0.50\) per game — a loss of 50 cents on average.

Class activity

How many trials are enough?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the convergence simulator.

  1. Set the true probability to 0.3. Record the relative frequency after 10, 50, 200, 1,000 and 2,000 trials, and how far each is from 0.3.
  2. Describe the shape of the graph: where is it most erratic, and where does it settle?
  3. Repeat with a true probability of 0.5. Is the pattern the same?
  4. A company tests a new product on 10 customers and 7 like it. It announces "70% of customers love it". Use your results to criticise this claim.
  5. Roughly how many trials would you want before trusting an estimate to within about 0.02? Justify from your table.
Discussion points

1–3. With few trials the relative frequency jumps around and can be far from the true value; with more trials it settles close to it. The same pattern appears for any true probability.

4. 10 customers is far too few: in the simulator, 10 trials often miss the true probability by 0.1 or more. The true proportion could easily be 50% or 85%.

5. Answers depend on the simulation, but typically several hundred to a thousand or more. The error shrinks slowly: to halve it, you need about four times as many trials.
