Theory

The probability scale

The range

\[0 \le P(\text{event}) \le 1\]

\(P = 0\) means impossible; \(P = 1\) means certain. Probabilities can be written as fractions, decimals or percentages — all three are acceptable.

A check worth doing

If an answer comes out above 1 or below 0, it is definitely wrong. The most common causes are adding probabilities that should have been multiplied, or dividing by the wrong total.

Theory

Sample space and outcomes

Definitions

  • An experiment is a process with an uncertain result — rolling a die, drawing a card.
  • An outcome is a single possible result.
  • The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes.
  • An event is a set of outcomes you are interested in.

Worked example 1

A standard die is rolled.

Sample space: \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) — six outcomes.

The event "even number": \(\{2, 4, 6\}\) — three outcomes.

The event "greater than 4": \(\{5, 6\}\) — two outcomes.

Worked example 2 — two coins

Two coins are tossed. Write the sample space.

\(\{HH,\ HT,\ TH,\ TT\}\) — four outcomes.

A common error: writing three outcomes (two heads, one of each, two tails). That is wrong because \(HT\) and \(TH\) are different outcomes — the first coin differs from the second. Treating them as one makes "one of each" appear equally likely to "two heads", which it is not.

Listing the sample space

Be systematic. For two dice, list all outcomes starting with 1, then all starting with 2, and so on. A table or tree diagram guarantees nothing is missed.

Method

Counting the number of outcomes

The multiplication principle

If one stage has \(m\) outcomes and the next has \(n\), then together they have \(m \times n\) outcomes. This extends to any number of stages.

Worked example 3

How many outcomes are there when rolling two dice?

\[6 \times 6 = 36 \text{ outcomes}\]

Three coins tossed: \(2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8\) outcomes. A die and a coin: \(6 \times 2 = 12\) outcomes.

Worked example 4 — a two-dice table

The 36 outcomes for two dice, shown as sums:

The table makes it easy to count: a sum of 7 appears 6 times, so \(P(7) = \dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}\), the most likely sum.

Method

Calculating probability

Theoretical probability

\[P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}\]

This formula applies only when all outcomes are equally likely, and when there is a finite number of them.

Equally likely outcomes

A fair die, a fair coin and a well-shuffled deck all give equally likely outcomes. A biased die, a spinner with unequal sectors, or the weather do not — for those you need relative frequency, covered in Lesson 3.

Worked example 5

A bag holds 5 red, 3 blue and 4 green marbles. One is drawn at random.

Total outcomes: \(5 + 3 + 4 = 12\).

\[P(\text{red}) = \frac{5}{12} \qquad P(\text{blue}) = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}\] \[P(\text{red or green}) = \frac{5 + 4}{12} = \frac{9}{12} = \frac{3}{4}\]

Check: the three probabilities \(\frac{5}{12} + \frac{3}{12} + \frac{4}{12} = 1\) ✓ — the probabilities of all possible outcomes always sum to 1.

Worked example 6 — two dice

Two dice are rolled. Find the probability the sum is greater than 9.

From the table, sums above 9 are 10, 11 and 12. Counting: 10 appears 3 times, 11 appears 2 times, 12 appears once.

\[P(\text{sum} > 9) = \frac{3 + 2 + 1}{36} = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}\]

Theory

The complement

Complementary events

\[P(\text{an event does not occur}) = 1 - P(\text{the event does occur})\]

The complement of an event is everything in the sample space that is not that event. The two probabilities always sum to 1.

Worked example 7

The probability of rain tomorrow is 0.35. Find the probability of no rain.

\[P(\text{no rain}) = 1 - 0.35 = 0.65\]

When the complement saves enormous work

Whenever a question asks for "at least one", the complement is "none" — and counting "none" is almost always far quicker.

\[P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})\]

Worked example 8 — the "at least one" trick

Three coins are tossed. Find the probability of at least one head.

The long way: count outcomes with one, two or three heads — that is \(3 + 3 + 1 = 7\) of the 8 outcomes.

The short way: the complement of "at least one head" is "no heads", which is only \(TTT\):

\[P(\text{no heads}) = \frac{1}{8}\] \[P(\text{at least one head}) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}\]

With ten coins the saving is dramatic: counting the 1,023 favourable outcomes is hopeless, but the complement is still a single outcome.

Worked example 9

A die is rolled twice. Find the probability of getting at least one six.

Complement: no sixes on either roll. Each roll has 5 non-six outcomes out of 6:

\[P(\text{no six}) = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{5}{6} = \frac{25}{36}\] \[P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - \frac{25}{36} = \frac{11}{36}\]

Worked example 10 — exam style

A building has smoke alarms that work independently. Each one fails, when needed, with probability 0.05.

(a) With 3 alarms, find the probability that at least one works. [2 marks]

The complement is "all three fail": \(0.05^3 = 0.000125\).

\[P(\text{at least one works}) = 1 - 0.000125 = 0.999875\]

(b) Safety rules require this probability to exceed 0.9999. What is the smallest number of alarms needed? [2 marks]

We need \(0.05^n < 0.0001\). With \(n = 3\): 0.000125 (too large). With \(n = 4\): 0.00000625 ✓. At least 4 alarms.

Your turn

  1. Six coins are tossed. Find the probability of at least one head.
    Show working

    \(P(\text{no heads}) = \left(\frac12\right)^6 = \frac{1}{64}\), so \(P(\text{at least one head}) = 1 - \frac{1}{64} = \frac{63}{64}\).

  2. Each day for the next three days, the chance of no rain is 0.7 (independently). Find the probability it rains on at least one of the days.
    Show working

    \(P(\text{no rain on all three}) = 0.7^3 = 0.343\), so the answer is \(1 - 0.343 = 0.657\).

Application

Is the game fair?

A game between two players is fair when each player has the same probability of winning. To decide, list the sample space, count each player's winning outcomes, and compare.

Worked example 11

Two dice are rolled and multiplied. Player A wins if the product is even; player B wins if it is odd. Is the game fair?

A product is odd only when both dice are odd: \(3 \times 3 = 9\) of the 36 outcomes.

\[P(\text{B wins}) = \frac{9}{36} = \frac14 \qquad P(\text{A wins}) = 1 - \frac14 = \frac34\]

Not fair — A is three times as likely to win. The rule sounds even ("even versus odd"), but the outcomes are not split evenly.

Your turn

  1. Two dice are rolled. Player A wins if the sum is 6, 7 or 8; otherwise player B wins. Is the game fair?
    Show working

    Sums of 6, 7 and 8 occur \(5 + 6 + 5 = 16\) times, so \(P(\text{A}) = \frac{16}{36}\) and \(P(\text{B}) = \frac{20}{36}\).

    Not fair: B is favoured.

  2. Using one die and one coin, design a rule that makes a two-player game fair. Justify it.
    Show working

    For example: A wins on (even, head) or (odd, tail); B wins on (even, tail) or (odd, head). Each player has 6 of the 12 equally likely outcomes, so \(P = \frac12\) each.

Class activity

How many tries until it is likely?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the "at least one" calculator. Each trial has the same probability of success, independently.

  1. For \(P(\text{success}) = 0.3\), find the smallest number of trials that gives a better than 50% chance of at least one success. Then better than 90%.
  2. Repeat for \(P(\text{success}) = 0.2\).
  3. For \(P(\text{success}) = 0.1\), find the number of trials for better than 50%. Predict whether 20 trials gives better than 90%, then check.
  4. Someone says: "Each go has a 10% chance, so after 10 goes I'm certain to win." Explain what is wrong, using your results.
  5. After 9 failed goes, is the 10th go more likely to succeed? Explain.
