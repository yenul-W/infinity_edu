Method

Drawing a scatter plot

The rules

  1. Independent variable on the horizontal axis, dependent on the vertical.
  2. Choose scales that use most of the available space — the axes need not start at zero, but say so if they do not.
  3. Plot one point per individual. Do not join the points.
  4. Label both axes with the variable name and its units.

Method

Describing the association

Every description of a scatter plot needs three things. Missing one loses a mark.

Form, direction, strength

Form — is the pattern linear (a straight band) or non-linear (curved)?

Direction — positive (as \(x\) increases, \(y\) increases) or negative (as \(x\) increases, \(y\) decreases)?

Strength — strong, moderate or weak, depending on how tightly the points cluster about a line.

The sentence to write

"There is a [strong/moderate/weak] [positive/negative] [linear/non-linear] association between [variable 1] and [variable 2]." Then add the context: "…as age increases, value decreases."

Method

Line of best fit by eye

Drawing it by eye

  1. Follow the general trend of the points.
  2. Aim for roughly equal numbers of points above and below the line.
  3. Keep the points as close to the line as possible overall.
  4. It need not pass through any data point, and usually should not be forced through the origin.

A good check: the line should pass close to the point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) — the mean of each variable.

Fit the line yourself

Adjust the gradient and intercept to fit the points by eye. The tool reports how far your line is from the true least-squares line.

Method

Finding the equation of the line

The equation

\[y = mx + c\]

\(m\) is the gradient — the change in \(y\) for each 1-unit increase in \(x\). \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept — the predicted value of \(y\) when \(x = 0\).

Reading the gradient from a graph

\[m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\]

Pick two points on the line (not necessarily data points), as far apart as possible for accuracy.

Worked example 3

A line of best fit passes through \((2, 33)\) and \((8, 15)\). Find its equation.

Gradient:

\[m = \frac{15 - 33}{8 - 2} = \frac{-18}{6} = -3\]

Intercept, substituting \((2, 33)\):

\[33 = -3(2) + c \quad\Rightarrow\quad c = 33 + 6 = 39\] \[y = -3x + 39\]

Interpreting them in context

Gradient: "For each additional year of age, the car's value decreases by about $3,000."

Intercept: "A brand-new car (age 0) is predicted to be worth $39,000."

Always state the units and the direction. A negative gradient means a decrease.

Worked example 4 — a meaningless intercept

A line relating a person's height (cm) to their weight (kg) is \(w = 0.8h - 65\). Interpret the intercept.

The intercept \(-65\) predicts that a person of height 0 cm weighs \(-65\) kg, which is meaningless. The intercept has no sensible interpretation here because \(x = 0\) is far outside the range of the data and impossible in context.

The gradient is still meaningful: each extra centimetre of height is associated with about 0.8 kg more weight.

Worked example 5 — exam style

A café records the daily maximum temperature (°C) and the number of iced coffees sold. A line of best fit drawn by eye passes through \((15, 40)\) and \((35, 120)\).

(a) Find the equation of the line. [2 marks]

\[m = \frac{120 - 40}{35 - 15} = 4 \qquad 40 = 4(15) + c \Rightarrow c = -20\] \[\text{Sales} = 4T - 20\]

(b) Interpret the gradient and the intercept. [2 marks]

Gradient: each 1°C rise is associated with about 4 more iced coffees sold. Intercept: the model predicts \(-20\) sales at 0°C — impossible, so the intercept has no meaning in context (0°C is outside the data).

(c) Predict sales on a 28°C day. [1 mark]

\(4(28) - 20 = 92\) iced coffees.

Theory

Pearson's correlation coefficient

Pearson's \(r\)

Pearson's correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the strength and direction of a linear association with a single number between \(-1\) and \(+1\).

In this course \(r\) is found with a scientific calculator in statistics mode, not by hand. Enter the paired data and read off \(r\).

The scale

\[-1 \le r \le +1\]

\(r = +1\): perfect positive linear relationship — all points exactly on an upward line.
\(r = 0\): no linear relationship.
\(r = -1\): perfect negative linear relationship — all points exactly on a downward line.

\(|r|\)Strength
0.9 to 1.0Very strong
0.7 to 0.9Strong
0.5 to 0.7Moderate
0.3 to 0.5Weak
0 to 0.3Very weak or none

The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. So \(r = -0.85\) is a strong negative association — just as strong as \(r = +0.85\), but in the opposite direction.

    Show working

Technology

Finding \(r\) with a scientific calculator

The syllabus asks you to calculate and interpret Pearson's correlation coefficient using a scientific calculator. Button names differ between models, but the steps are always the same:

  1. Enter statistics mode and choose linear regression (often shown as \(y = a + bx\) or A+BX).
  2. Enter the data as pairs: the independent variable in the \(x\) column, the dependent variable in the \(y\) column.
  3. Open the regression results and read \(r\). (The same screen gives the least-squares gradient and intercept — Lesson 3.)
  4. Check: does the sign of \(r\) match the direction on your scatter plot? A wrong sign usually means swapped columns or a typing error.

Evaluation

Interpreting \(r\) correctly

What \(r\) does and does not tell you

  • \(r\) measures linear association only. A perfect curve can have \(r\) near 0.
  • \(r\) says nothing about causation.
  • \(r\) is heavily affected by outliers, especially in small samples.
  • \(r\) has no units and does not change if you change the units of measurement.
  • \(r\) is not a percentage. \(r = 0.5\) does not mean "50% related".

Worked example 7 — the non-linear trap

A projectile's height against time follows a perfect parabola. Its \(r\) is close to 0. What does this mean?

It means there is no linear relationship — and there isn't, since the relationship is quadratic. The variables are perfectly related, just not in a straight line. Always look at the scatter plot before trusting \(r\).

Worked example 8 — the outlier effect

Nine points lie almost exactly on a rising line, giving \(r = 0.96\). One extra point sits far away, low and to the right. The correlation drops to \(r = 0.31\).

A single outlier has transformed a very strong association into a weak one. You should investigate the outlier: is it a recording error, or a genuine but unusual case? Report \(r\) both with and without it, and explain the difference.

Your turn

  1. A researcher finds \(r = 0.1\) and concludes "the variables are unrelated". The scatter plot shows a clear U-shape. Comment.
    Show working

    \(r\) only measures linear association. The U-shape shows a strong non-linear relationship, so the variables are related — just not along a straight line.

  2. Heights in a dataset are converted from centimetres to inches. What happens to \(r\)?
    Show working

    Nothing. \(r\) has no units, and rescaling a variable does not change how closely the points follow a line.

Class activity

Guess the correlation

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the association explorer.

  1. Partner A covers the readouts, then sets any gradient and scatter. Partner B looks at the plot only and estimates \(r\). Uncover and score the difference. Swap; play three rounds each. Lowest total wins.
  2. Set gradient 0.2 and scatter 0. Then set gradient 1.5 and scatter 3. Which has the larger \(r\)? Which line is steeper?
  3. Using your answer to step 2, explain why "a steeper line means a stronger correlation" is false.
  4. Find a setting with \(r\) between 0.6 and 0.7. Describe it in a full sentence.
Discussion points

2. Gradient 0.2 with no scatter gives \(r = 1.000\); gradient 1.5 with scatter 3 gives \(r = 0.717\). The second line is much steeper but the correlation is weaker.

3. \(r\) measures how closely the points follow a line, not how steep the line is. Steepness is the gradient; strength is the scatter about the line.

4. For example gradient 0.2, scatter 0.5: \(r = 0.630\), "a moderate positive linear association".
