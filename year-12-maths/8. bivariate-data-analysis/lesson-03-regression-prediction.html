Theory

What least squares means

A line drawn by eye is a judgement call — two people get two different lines. The least-squares regression line is defined precisely, so everyone gets the same answer.

The least-squares criterion

For each data point, the residual is the vertical distance from the point to the line. The least-squares line is the one that makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible.

Why squared? Squaring makes every residual positive, so points above and below cannot cancel out. It also penalises large misses much more heavily than small ones, which is why the line is pulled noticeably towards outliers.

Residuals in action

Move your line and watch the residuals — the vertical gaps. The dashed line is the true least-squares line; no other line makes the sum of squares smaller.

A property worth knowing

The least-squares line always passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) — the point given by the mean of each variable. This is a useful check on your calculator work.

Method

Finding the line on a scientific calculator

The syllabus requires you to determine the equation of the least-squares regression line using a scientific calculator. You are not expected to compute it by hand.

The general procedure

  1. Enter statistics mode with two variables (often MODE → STAT → A+BX).
  2. Type the \(x\) values in one column and the \(y\) values in the other, keeping the pairs aligned.
  3. Read off \(A\), \(B\) and \(r\) from the regression menu.
  4. Write the equation, matching the calculator's convention to \(y = mx + c\).

The notation trap

Most scientific calculators give the line as \(y = A + Bx\), so \(A\) is the intercept and \(B\) is the gradient — the opposite of what the letters suggest. Check which is which before writing your answer.

Worked example 1

For the car data (age 1–10 years, value $38k–$11k), a calculator returns \(A = 38.06\), \(B = -2.83\), \(r = -0.996\).

Equation:

\[v = 38.06 - 2.83a\]

Check: the gradient is negative, matching the negative \(r\) and the downward trend ✓. The correlation of \(-0.996\) is very strong.

Rounding

Keep the full calculator values when making predictions, and round only the final answer. Rounding the gradient to 1 decimal place before predicting can shift the answer noticeably.

Method

Interpreting the gradient and intercept

The two interpretations

Gradient: "For each 1-unit increase in [\(x\) with units], [\(y\)] increases/decreases by [\(m\)] [units of \(y\)]."

Intercept: "When [\(x\)] is 0, [\(y\)] is predicted to be [\(c\)] [units]." — but only state this if \(x = 0\) is meaningful.

Worked example 2

Interpret \(v = 38.06 - 2.83a\), where \(v\) is value in $000 and \(a\) is age in years.

Gradient: "For each additional year of age, a car's value decreases by about $2,830."

Intercept: "A brand-new car (age 0) is predicted to be worth about $38,060." Here \(a = 0\) is meaningful — a new car — and it is close to the data range, so the interpretation is sound.

Worked example 3 — when the intercept is meaningless

A regression of a child's weight (kg) on height (cm) gives \(w = 0.63h - 42.1\).

Gradient: "For each extra centimetre of height, weight increases by about 0.63 kg." Meaningful ✓

Intercept: a child of height 0 cm would weigh \(-42.1\) kg. This is not meaningful — zero height is impossible and lies far outside the data. Say so rather than inventing an interpretation.

Method

Making predictions

The method

To predict \(y\), substitute the given \(x\) into the regression equation. To find the \(x\) that gives a particular \(y\), solve the equation for \(x\).

Worked example 4

Using \(v = 38.06 - 2.83a\), predict the value of a 7-year-old car.

\[v = 38.06 - 2.83(7) = 38.06 - 19.81 = 18.25\]

About $18,250.

Worked example 5 — predicting \(x\) from \(y\)

At what age is the car predicted to be worth $25,000?

\[25 = 38.06 - 2.83a\] \[2.83a = 13.06 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = 4.6 \text{ years}\]

About 4 years and 7 months.

Evaluation

Interpolation and extrapolation

The two kinds of prediction

Interpolation — predicting within the range of the original data. Generally reliable, because the relationship has been observed there.

Extrapolation — predicting outside the range of the data. Unreliable, because there is no evidence the pattern continues.

Worked example 6

The car data covers ages 1 to 10 years, with \(v = 38.06 - 2.83a\).

(a) Predict the value at 6 years.

\[v = 38.06 - 2.83(6) = 21.08\]

About $21,080. This is interpolation (6 is within 1–10), so it is reliable.

(b) Predict the value at 16 years.

\[v = 38.06 - 2.83(16) = 38.06 - 45.28 = -7.22\]

The model predicts negative $7,220, which is impossible. This is extrapolation, far beyond the data, and the linear model has broken down — in reality depreciation slows and cars retain a residual value.

Other limitations to mention

  • A strong \(r\) shows association, not causation.
  • The model assumes the relationship stays linear.
  • Predictions are averages, not guarantees for any individual.
  • Conditions may change over time, invalidating the model.
  • A small sample makes every conclusion less reliable.

Worked example 7 — a model answer

"Using the model, predict a car's value at 20 years and comment."

"\(v = 38.06 - 2.83(20) = -18.54\), so the model predicts a value of \(-\$18\,540\).

This is not a sensible prediction. A car cannot have a negative value. The prediction requires extrapolation well beyond the data range of 1 to 10 years, where there is no evidence the linear relationship still holds. In reality depreciation slows with age and cars retain a residual or scrap value, so the true relationship flattens out rather than continuing to fall."

Evaluation

Judging how reliable a prediction is

Exam questions often ask "how reliable is this prediction?" A strong answer weighs several factors, not just one:

FactorMore reliableLess reliable
Strength of \(r\)\(|r|\) close to 1\(|r|\) weak or moderate
Position of \(x\)InterpolationExtrapolation
Sample sizeMany data pointsOnly a few
FormClearly linearCurved pattern
Who it is forAn average of manyOne individual

Checking a prediction against reality: the residual

\[\text{residual} = \text{actual value} - \text{predicted value}\]

A positive residual means the actual value was higher than predicted; negative means lower. Small residuals across the data mean the model fits well.

Worked example 8

Two predictions of next week's sales:

  • Model A: \(r = 0.95\), 40 weeks of data, predicting within the data range.
  • Model B: \(r = 0.45\), 8 weeks of data, predicting outside the data range.

Model A is far more reliable on every factor: a very strong correlation, a large sample and interpolation. Model B has a weak correlation, a small sample and extrapolates — its prediction should not be trusted.

Residual: on an 18°C day the gas model \(G = 120 - 4.5T\) predicts 39 MJ, but the household used 41 MJ. The residual is \(41 - 39 = 2\) MJ: the model underestimated by 2 MJ, a small error.

Your turn

  1. A model of house price against floor area has \(r = 0.84\) from 45 houses with areas 90–340 m². Rate the reliability of predictions for (a) a 200 m² house and (b) a 600 m² house.
    Show working

    (a) Fairly reliable: strong \(r\), a reasonable sample, interpolation. (b) Unreliable: far outside the data, and very large houses may follow a different pattern.

  2. Using \(\text{Mark} = 44.21 + 3.548h\), a student who studied 7 hours scored 70. Find the residual and interpret it.
    Show working

    Predicted: \(44.21 + 3.548 \times 7 = 69.05\). Residual: \(70 - 69.05 = 0.95\). The student scored about 1 mark more than predicted.

Technology

Spreadsheet modelling

The syllabus asks you to use a spreadsheet to construct a scatter plot and the least-squares regression line.

The steps

  1. Put the independent variable in column A and the dependent in column B.
  2. Select both columns and insert a Scatter (XY) chart — never a line chart, which would join the points.
  3. Right-click a point → Add Trendline → Linear.
  4. Tick Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value.

Useful functions

  • =SLOPE(B2:B9, A2:A9) — the gradient. Note the \(y\) range comes first.
  • =INTERCEPT(B2:B9, A2:A9) — the \(y\)-intercept.
  • =CORREL(A2:A9, B2:B9) — Pearson's \(r\).
  • =FORECAST(x, B2:B9, A2:A9) — a prediction at a given \(x\).

\(R^2\) versus \(r\)

Spreadsheets usually display \(R^2\), the square of Pearson's \(r\). So \(R^2 = 0.81\) corresponds to \(r = \pm 0.9\) — and you must read the direction from the gradient, because squaring destroys the sign.

Worked example 9

A spreadsheet trendline reads \(y = -2.83x + 38.06\) with \(R^2 = 0.992\). Find \(r\) and describe the association.

\[r = \pm\sqrt{0.992} = \pm 0.996\]

The gradient is negative, so \(r = -0.996\): a very strong negative linear association.

Worked example 10 — exam style

A shop records weekly advertising spend \(x\) (in $100s, from 2 to 12) and weekly sales \(y\) (in $000s). A spreadsheet trendline gives \(y = 1.35x + 8.2\) with \(R^2 = 0.7744\).

(a) Find \(r\) and describe the association. [2 marks]

\(r = +\sqrt{0.7744} = 0.88\) (positive, because the gradient is positive): a strong positive linear association.

(b) Interpret the gradient. [1 mark]

Each extra $100 spent on advertising is associated with about $1,350 more in weekly sales.

(c) Predict sales for a week with $900 of advertising. [1 mark]

\(x = 9\): \(y = 1.35(9) + 8.2 = 20.35\), so about $20,350.

(d) The manager plans to spend $3,000 in one week. Comment. [1 mark]

\(x = 30\) is far beyond the data (up to 12). Extrapolating is unreliable: sales cannot keep rising at the same rate, as customer demand is limited.

Class activity

Where does the model break?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the prediction tool, which uses the least-squares line for the car data (ages 1–10 years).

  1. Record the predicted values at ages 0, 6, 10, 12, 13 and 14. Label each as interpolation or extrapolation.
  2. Between which two ages does the model first predict a negative value?
  3. Is the age-0 prediction sensible? Compare it with the youngest car in the data.
  4. As a pair, decide on a sensible range of ages for using this model, and justify it.
  5. Sketch what you think the true relationship looks like from 0 to 18 years. Why would a straight line be the wrong shape?
