Theory

Univariate versus bivariate data

The distinction

Univariate (one-variable) data records a single measurement for each individual. You summarise it with the mean, median, range and standard deviation, and display it with a histogram, dot plot or box plot.

Bivariate (two-variable) data records two measurements for each individual. You analyse the relationship between them, and display it with a scatter plot.

UnivariateBivariate
Measurements per individualOneTwo
Question answered"What is typical? How spread out?""Are these two related? Can I predict one from the other?"
DisplayHistogram, box plot, dot plotScatter plot
SummaryMean, median, standard deviationCorrelation coefficient, line of best fit
ExampleThe heights of 40 studentsThe height and arm span of 40 students

When each is needed

Use univariate analysis when the question is about one quantity on its own — "what is the average rainfall?"
Use bivariate analysis when the question involves a relationship or a prediction — "does more rainfall lead to higher crop yield?"

Worked example 1

Classify each investigation.

(a) "What is the median salary of graduates?" — one measurement per graduate, so univariate.

(b) "Do graduates with more years of study earn higher salaries?" — two measurements per graduate (years of study and salary), so bivariate.

(c) "How much does daily temperature vary across the year?" — one measurement per day, so univariate.

(d) "Does ice-cream sell better on hotter days?" — temperature and sales for each day, so bivariate.

Classify the investigation

Type univariate or bivariate.

Method

Identifying the dependent and independent variables

The two roles

The independent variable (or explanatory variable) is the one used to explain or predict. It goes on the horizontal (\(x\)) axis.

The dependent variable (or response variable) is the one being explained or predicted. It goes on the vertical (\(y\)) axis.

The test question

Ask: "Which one am I using to predict the other?" That one is independent. Equivalently, ask which variable depends on the other — time, age and dosage are almost always independent, because nothing you measure changes them.

Worked example 2

Identify the variables in each study.

StudyIndependent (\(x\))Dependent (\(y\))
Predicting crop yield from rainfallRainfallCrop yield
Car value against ageAgeValue
Exam mark against hours studiedHours studiedExam mark
Fuel used against distance drivenDistanceFuel used
Plant growth against fertiliser appliedFertiliserGrowth

In every case the independent variable is the one you would set or observe first, and the dependent variable is the outcome.

Why the order matters

Swapping the axes changes the line of best fit and therefore every prediction you make from it. Getting this wrong makes the entire analysis wrong, so decide before you plot anything.

Your turn

  1. Identify the independent and dependent variables. (a) Predicting a puppy's weight from its age. (b) Predicting a household's heating bill from the outside temperature. (c) Investigating whether salt intake affects blood pressure.
    Show working

    (a) Independent: age; dependent: weight. (b) Independent: outside temperature; dependent: heating bill. (c) Independent: salt intake; dependent: blood pressure.

  2. For height and arm span, which variable is independent?
    Show working

    It depends on the question. Predicting arm span from height makes height independent; predicting height from arm span (useful when someone cannot stand) reverses it. Neither causes the other — the question decides.

Theory

Association

Association

Two variables show an association when a change in one tends to go with a change in the other. It is a statistical pattern — nothing more. Association alone says nothing about why the pattern exists.

Associations are described in three ways, all covered fully in Lesson 2:

  • Form — linear or non-linear.
  • Direction — positive (both rise together) or negative (one rises as the other falls).
  • Strength — strong, moderate or weak, depending on how closely the points follow the pattern.

Theory

Causation

Causation

A relationship is causal when a change in the independent variable directly produces a change in the dependent variable. Causation is a much stronger claim than association, and the data alone can never prove it.

The rule to remember

Association does not imply causation. A strong correlation shows the variables move together; it does not show that one makes the other happen.

When variables A and B are associated, there are four possible explanations:

ExplanationMeaningExample
A causes B The genuine causal case More fertiliser → more growth
B causes A The causation runs the other way Fit people exercise more, rather than exercise making them want to
A third variable A confounding variable drives both Hot weather drives both ice-cream sales and drownings
Coincidence Chance alone, especially in small samples Two unrelated trends that happen to rise together

What would support a causal claim

  • A controlled experiment where the independent variable is deliberately changed and everything else held constant.
  • A plausible mechanism explaining how one produces the other.
  • The cause precedes the effect in time.
  • The association is consistent across repeated studies.

Worked example 3 — causal

A study finds a strong positive association between the amount of fertiliser applied and plant growth, in a trial where identical plants were grown in identical conditions with only the fertiliser varied.

Causal. This is a controlled experiment: the researcher set the independent variable, everything else was held constant, and there is a well-understood biological mechanism.

Worked example 4 — not causal

A study finds a strong positive association between the number of firefighters sent to a fire and the damage caused.

Not causal. Firefighters do not cause damage. The confounding variable is the size of the fire: larger fires both attract more firefighters and cause more damage.

Your turn

  1. Students who sit at the front of the class get higher marks. Give two explanations other than "sitting at the front causes higher marks".
    Show working

    A confounding variable such as motivation: keen students both choose the front and work harder. Or reverse causation: students who already do well feel confident sitting at the front.

  2. Countries with more televisions per person have higher life expectancy. Is this causal? Explain.
    Show working

    Not causal. National wealth is a confounder: richer countries have more televisions and better health care, nutrition and sanitation.

Application

Confounding variables and coincidence

Confounding variable

A confounding variable is a third factor that influences both of the measured variables, creating an association between them even though neither causes the other.

Observed associationConfounding variable
Ice-cream sales and drowning deathsHot weather
Shoe size and reading ability in childrenAge
Number of churches and number of crimes in a townPopulation size
Coffee drinking and lung cancerSmoking (coffee drinkers smoked more)
Hospital admissions and death rates by suburbAge profile of the suburb

Worked example 5 — writing the answer

A newspaper reports: "Students who own more books score higher in tests. Buy your child books to improve their marks." Comment.

"The report confuses association with causation. The data show only that book ownership and test scores are positively associated.

A likely confounding variable is family income or parental education: wealthier, more educated households tend to own more books and to provide more educational support, tutoring and a quieter study environment. These drive both variables.

Simply buying books would not raise marks unless they were actually read. To establish causation you would need a controlled experiment in which books were provided to a randomly selected group and outcomes compared with a control group."

Notice the structure: name the error, state the association, propose a specific confounder, explain how it drives both, and say what evidence would be needed.

Coincidence and small samples

With few data points, strong-looking correlations can arise by pure chance. Two points always lie exactly on a line. Always ask how large the sample is before treating a correlation as meaningful.

Causal or not?

Worked example 6 — exam style

Data from 12 Sydney suburbs show a strong positive association (\(r = 0.86\)) between the number of cafés and the median house price. A real estate agent says: "Open more cafés and house prices will rise."

(a) Identify the independent and dependent variables in the agent's claim. [1 mark]

Independent: number of cafés. Dependent: median house price.

(b) Evaluate the agent's claim. [3 marks]

The data show association, not causation. Causation may run the other way: wealthy suburbs attract cafés because residents have money to spend. A confounder such as location (closeness to the CBD or the beach) could drive both. And 12 suburbs is a small sample.

(c) What evidence would support the claim? [1 mark]

Prices tracked before and after new cafés open in otherwise similar suburbs, compared with suburbs where none opened.

Your turn

  1. Towns with more fast-food outlets have more hospital beds. Suggest a confounding variable and explain how it drives both.
    Show working

    Population: larger towns have more of everything, including fast-food outlets and hospital beds.

  2. A study finds dog owners live longer than non-owners. Give two reasons this may not mean owning a dog extends life.
    Show working

    Reverse causation: healthier, more active people are more likely to take on a dog. Confounders: dog owners may have higher incomes, more space or more stable lives, each linked to longer life.

Application

Designing a fair investigation

To test whether one variable causes another, design a study that rules out the other explanations. The features of a fair test:

FeatureWhy it matters
Control group that does not receive the changeGives a comparison, so you can see what would have happened anyway
Random allocation to the groupsSpreads confounders (motivation, ability, income) evenly between the groups
Everything else held constantOnly the independent variable differs between the groups
Large enough sampleReduces the chance that the result is a coincidence
Measure before and afterShows that the cause came before the effect

Worked example 7

A school wants to know whether a new revision app improves maths results. Design a fair investigation.

  • Choose 200 Year 12 students and record their current maths marks.
  • Randomly allocate 100 to use the app for 6 weeks and 100 (the control group) to revise as usual.
  • Keep teachers, lessons and the test the same for both groups.
  • Compare the change in marks between the groups.

If the app group improves clearly more, the app is the most likely cause, because random allocation balanced the other factors.

Contrast: simply comparing students who chose to download the app with those who did not is an observational study. Keen students may be more likely to download it, so motivation would confound the result.

Your turn

  1. Design a fair test of whether listening to music while studying affects how many words students can memorise.
    Show working

    Randomly split a large group of students in two. Both groups study the same list of words for the same time in the same room; one group hears music, the control group studies in silence. Test both groups identically and compare the mean number of words recalled.

  2. A gym compares members who chose its new class with those who did not, and finds the class members lost more weight. Identify two flaws in this design.
    Show working

    No random allocation: more motivated members may choose the class, so motivation confounds the result. Other factors such as diet were not controlled, and there may be no before-and-after measurements.

Class activity

Explain the association

About 10 minutes · groups of three. Each statement below describes a real-looking association. For each one, decide which of the four explanations fits best (A causes B, B causes A, a confounder, or coincidence), and justify it in one sentence. Then choose one statement and outline a fair test.

  1. Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases rise together.
  2. Students who learn a musical instrument have higher maths marks.
  3. Hours of study and test marks are positively associated.
  4. Over ten years, a country's mobile phone sales and its number of shark sightings both rose.
  5. People who exercise regularly report being happier.
  6. Among primary-school children, those with bigger feet read better.
Discussion points

1. Confounder: sunny, hot weather drives both.

2. Most likely a confounder: family income and support pay for lessons and also help with maths. Some causal effect is possible but unproven.

3. Plausibly causal (study improves marks), although motivation also plays a part. A controlled trial would be needed to measure the size of the effect.

4. Coincidence: two unrelated trends rising over the same years. (Population growth or more people at beaches could also be argued.)

5. Possibly causal in both directions: exercise may improve mood, and happier people may find it easier to exercise.

6. Confounder: age. Older children have bigger feet and read better.

A fair test needs random allocation, a control group and everything else held constant — for example, randomly assigning some students to free music lessons for a year.
