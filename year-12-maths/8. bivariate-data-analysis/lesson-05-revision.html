Reference

Method sheet

Univariate vs bivariate

One measurement per individual → univariate (mean, spread, histogram). Two measurements per individual → bivariate (relationship, scatter plot, correlation).

Variables

Independent = the predictor, on the \(x\)-axis. Dependent = the outcome, on the \(y\)-axis. Ask: "which one am I using to predict the other?"

Describing an association

Always give form (linear / non-linear), direction (positive / negative) and strength (strong / moderate / weak) — in context.

\(|r|\)Strength
0.9 – 1.0Very strong
0.7 – 0.9Strong
0.5 – 0.7Moderate
0.3 – 0.5Weak
0 – 0.3Very weak or none

The line

\[y = mx + c \qquad m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\]

Calculators give \(y = A + Bx\), where \(A\) is the intercept and \(B\) the gradient. The least-squares line always passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

Spreadsheet

=SLOPE(y,x), =INTERCEPT(y,x), =CORREL(x,y), =FORECAST(x,y,x-range). Trendlines display \(R^2\); take the square root for \(|r|\) and read the sign from the gradient.

Exam technique

Answer templates

Describing an association

"There is a [strength] [direction] [form] association between [\(x\)] and [\(y\)]. As [\(x\)] increases, [\(y\)] tends to [increase/decrease]."

Interpreting the gradient

"For each 1-unit increase in [\(x\), with units], [\(y\)] [increases/decreases] by about [\(m\)] [units of \(y\)]."

Interpreting the intercept

"When [\(x\)] is 0, [\(y\)] is predicted to be [\(c\)]." Add: "However, \(x = 0\) lies outside the data range and is not meaningful here" — whenever that is true.

Commenting on a prediction

"This is [interpolation/extrapolation], since \(x = \) [value] lies [inside/outside] the data range of [range]. It is therefore [reliable / unreliable, because there is no evidence the linear relationship continues]."

Association vs causation

"The data show an association, but not causation. A possible confounding variable is [specific variable], which would influence both [\(x\)] and [\(y\)]. To establish causation, a controlled experiment would be needed."

Exam technique

Mistakes that cost marks

MistakeFix
Putting the dependent variable on the \(x\)-axis The predictor goes on \(x\). Decide before plotting.
Describing an association with only one or two features Always give form, direction and strength, in context.
Concluding causation from a strong \(r\) Association is not causation. Name a specific confounder.
Reading \(A\) as the gradient on a calculator In \(y = A + Bx\), \(A\) is the intercept and \(B\) the gradient.
Quoting \(R^2\) as if it were \(r\) Take the square root, and get the sign from the gradient.
Trusting \(r\) without looking at the plot A perfect curve gives \(r \approx 0\). Always inspect the scatter plot.
Extrapolating without comment State that it is extrapolation and explain why it is unreliable.
Interpreting a meaningless intercept If \(x = 0\) is impossible or far outside the data, say so.
Joining the points on a scatter plot Never join them. Plot points only, then add a line of best fit.
Rounding the gradient before predicting Keep full calculator precision; round only the final answer.
Deleting an outlier silently Investigate it and report \(r\) with and without it.
Omitting units when interpreting the gradient "$2,830 per year", not just "2.83".
