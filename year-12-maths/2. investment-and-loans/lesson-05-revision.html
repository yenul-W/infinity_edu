Reference

Formula sheet

Simple interest

\[I = Prn \qquad A = P + I = P(1 + rn)\]

Linear growth: the same dollar amount is added each period.

Compound interest

\[FV = PV(1+r)^{n} \qquad PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^{n}} \qquad r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/n} - 1\]

Exponential growth. \(r\) is the rate per compounding period and \(n\) is the number of periods — convert both. Interest earned is \(FV - PV\).

Straight-line depreciation

\[S = V_0 - Dn\]

Linear decay. \(D\) is a fixed dollar amount per period. Reaches zero at \(n = V_0/D\).

Declining balance depreciation

\[S = V_0(1 - r)^{n}\]

Exponential decay. \(r\) is a percentage of the current value. Never reaches zero.

Shares

\[\text{Dividend} = (\text{dividend per share}) \times (\text{shares held})\] \[\text{Dividend yield} = \frac{\text{dividend per share}}{\text{market price}} \times 100\%\]

Total return also includes the capital gain, \((\text{sell} - \text{buy}) \times \text{shares}\).

Inflation

\[\text{Future price} = \text{Current price} \times (1+i)^{n} \qquad \text{Purchasing power} = \frac{\text{amount}}{(1+i)^{n}}\]

Reducing-balance loans

\[\text{Interest} = \text{Opening balance} \times r\] \[\text{Closing balance} = \text{Opening} + \text{Interest} - \text{Repayment}\]

\(r\) is the rate per repayment period. If the repayment does not exceed the interest, the balance grows and the loan is never repaid.

Credit cards

\[I = \text{balance} \times \frac{\text{annual rate}}{365} \times \text{days}\]

Method

Which formula do I use?

Most lost marks in this topic come from picking the wrong model, not from arithmetic. These phrases in a question tell you which one:

The question says…Use
"simple interest"\(I = Prn\) — linear
"compound interest", "compounded annually/monthly"\(FV = PV(1+r)^{n}\)
"depreciates by $X per year"Straight-line, \(S = V_0 - Dn\)
"depreciates by X% of its original value"Straight-line, with \(D = X\% \times V_0\)
"depreciates by X% per year" / "declining balance" / "diminishing value"\(S = V_0(1-r)^{n}\)
"appreciates", "inflation", "prices rise by"\((1+i)^{n}\) — same as compound interest
"in today's dollars", "purchasing power"Divide by \((1+i)^{n}\)
"how much must be invested now"Present value — divide
"monthly repayments", "balance owing"Reducing-balance table

The single most useful distinction

Is the percentage applied to the original amount every period (linear — simple interest, straight-line depreciation), or to the current amount (exponential — compound interest, declining balance, inflation)? Everything else follows from that.

Choose the model

Type simple, compound, straight-line or declining for each scenario.

Exam technique

Mistakes that cost marks

MistakeFix
Using \(r = 6\) instead of \(r = 0.06\) Always convert the percentage to a decimal before substituting.
Converting the rate for monthly compounding but not the periods Both change together: \(r \div 12\) and \(n \times 12\).
Mixing an annual rate with a time in months Convert one to match the other before using \(I = Prn\).
Treating "18% per year depreciation" as straight-line A percentage without "of the original value" means declining balance.
Giving \(FV\) when the question asked for the interest Interest is \(FV - PV\). Re-read what was asked.
Multiplying by \((1+i)^n\) to find purchasing power Purchasing power divides — prices went up, so your money is worth less.
Forgetting to add the interest in a loan table Closing = opening + interest − repayment.
Using the original balance for every row of a loan table Each opening balance is the previous closing balance.
Calculating dividend yield on the dividend total rather than per share Yield uses dividend per share ÷ price per share. (Total ÷ total cost also works.)
Rounding to the nearest dollar part-way through Keep full precision in the calculator; round only the final answer.
Answering a "compare strategies" question with one sentence Name the return, the risk, the access to funds, and tie them to the person's time frame.
