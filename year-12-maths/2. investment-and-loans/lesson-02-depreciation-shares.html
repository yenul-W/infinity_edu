Theory

Straight-line depreciation

Depreciation is the loss in value of an asset over time. The straight-line method subtracts the same dollar amount every period.

Straight-line (prime cost) depreciation

\[S = V_0 - Dn\]

\(S\) = salvage value (value after \(n\) periods),   \(V_0\) = initial value of the asset,
\(D\) = depreciation amount per period (in dollars),   \(n\) = number of periods.

This is a linear relationship: \(V_0\) is the \(y\)-intercept and \(-D\) is the gradient. The graph is a straight line sloping down.

Worked example 1

A delivery van costs $62,000 and depreciates by $7,400 per year by the straight-line method.

(a) Value after 5 years?

\[S = 62000 - 7400 \times 5 = 62000 - 37000 = \$25\,000\]

(b) When does it reach a value of $0? Set \(S = 0\):

\[0 = 62000 - 7400n \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = \frac{62000}{7400} = 8.38 \text{ years}\]

Under this method an asset does reach zero — and would go negative after that, which is meaningless. The model is only valid for \(0 \le n \le 8.38\).

Worked example 2 — given as a percentage of the original

Machinery worth $90,000 depreciates by 12% of its original value each year. Find its value after 4 years.

"Of its original value" signals straight-line, not declining balance:

\[D = 0.12 \times 90000 = \$10\,800 \text{ per year}\] \[S = 90000 - 10800 \times 4 = 90000 - 43200 = \$46\,800\]

Your turn

  1. A café's coffee machine costs $28,500 and depreciates by $3,800 per year (straight-line). Find its value after 4 years, and when it will be worth $5,700.
    Show working

    \(S = 28500 - 3800 \times 4 = \$13\,300\).

    \(5700 = 28500 - 3800n \Rightarrow 3800n = 22800 \Rightarrow n = 6\) years.

  2. Equipment worth $45,000 depreciates by 15% of its original value each year. Find its value after 3 years.
    Show working

    \(D = 0.15 \times 45000 = \$6\,750\) per year.

    \(S = 45000 - 6750 \times 3 = \$24\,750\).

Theory

Declining balance depreciation

The declining balance (or diminishing value) method removes the same percentage of the current value each period. This is exponential decay — compound interest running in reverse.

Declining balance depreciation

\[S = V_0(1 - r)^{n}\]

\(S\) = salvage value,   \(V_0\) = initial value,
\(r\) = depreciation rate per period as a decimal,   \(n\) = number of periods.

Compare this with \(FV = PV(1+r)^n\). The only difference is the minus sign — growth becomes decay.

Worked example 3

A tractor bought for $120,000 depreciates at 18% per annum by the declining balance method. Find its value after 6 years.

\[S = 120000(1 - 0.18)^{6} = 120000 \times 0.82^{6}\] \[= 120000 \times 0.304006 = \$36\,480.75\]

Worked example 4 — finding the rate

A computer bought for $3,200 is worth $1,100 after 4 years. Find the annual declining-balance rate to one decimal place.

\[3200(1-r)^{4} = 1100 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (1-r)^{4} = \frac{1100}{3200} = 0.34375\] \[1 - r = 0.34375^{1/4} = 0.76578\] \[r = 1 - 0.76578 = 0.23422\]

The depreciation rate is about 23.4% per annum.

Your turn

  1. A car bought for $36,000 depreciates at 15% per annum (declining balance). Find its value after 4 years.
    Show working
    \[S = 36000 \times 0.85^{4} = 36000 \times 0.522006 = \$18\,792.23\]
  2. A phone bought for $2,400 is worth $1,050 after 3 years. Find the annual declining-balance rate to one decimal place.
    Show working
    \[(1 - r)^{3} = \frac{1050}{2400} = 0.4375 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 - r = 0.4375^{1/3} = 0.75915\]

    \(r = 0.24085\): about 24.1% per annum.

Worked example 5 — exam style

A machine costs $48,000. Its owner can depreciate it either by $8,000 per year (straight-line) or at 20% per annum (declining balance).

(a) Find its value after 3 years under each method. [2 marks]

\[\text{SL: } 48000 - 8000 \times 3 = \$24\,000 \qquad \text{DB: } 48000 \times 0.8^{3} = \$24\,576\]

(b) Which method gives the larger depreciation in the first year? [1 mark]

Straight-line loses $8,000; declining balance loses \(0.2 \times 48000 = \$9\,600\). Declining balance is larger in year 1.

(c) During which year does the straight-line value first fall below the declining-balance value? [2 marks]

Theory

Shares, dividends and yield

Buying a share makes you part-owner of a company. You can gain in two ways: the share price rising (capital growth) and the company paying dividends (a share of its profit).

Share calculations

\[\text{Dividend} = (\text{dividend per share}) \times (\text{number of shares})\] \[\text{Dividend yield} = \frac{\text{dividend per share}}{\text{market price per share}} \times 100\%\]

The yield expresses the dividend as a percentage of what you paid, so you can compare a share against a bank interest rate. (Franked dividends are excluded from this course.)

Worked example 6

Priya buys 1,800 shares at $24.50 each. The company pays a dividend of $1.47 per share.

(a) Total cost?

\[1800 \times 24.50 = \$44\,100\]

(b) Total dividend?

\[1800 \times 1.47 = \$2\,646\]

(c) Dividend yield?

\[\frac{1.47}{24.50} \times 100\% = 6\%\]

A 6% yield compares favourably with a term deposit — but unlike a deposit, the share price itself can fall.

Worked example 7 — total return

A year later Priya's shares are worth $26.95 each. Find her total return.

Capital gain:

\[1800 \times (26.95 - 24.50) = 1800 \times 2.45 = \$4\,410\]

Dividends: $2,646 (from above).

Total return: \(4410 + 2646 = \$7\,056\), which as a percentage of the $44,100 outlay is

\[\frac{7056}{44100} \times 100\% = 16\%\]

Theory

Reading a share price graph

The syllabus asks you to graph and interpret the value of a share over time. Share price graphs are drawn from closing prices, usually daily or monthly, joined by straight segments. When you interpret one, cover four things:

  1. The overall trend — compare the first and last prices.
  2. The extremes — the highest and lowest prices, and when.
  3. The size of changes — in dollars and as a percentage: \(\dfrac{\text{change}}{\text{starting price}} \times 100\%\).
  4. Volatility — how sharply and how often the price moves. Large swings mean higher risk.

Closing share price of a company at the end of each month (month 0 is the start of the year).

Worked example 8

Use the graph. Monthly closing prices: $12.00, $12.60, $13.10, $12.40, $11.20, $10.50, $11.00, $12.20, $13.80, $14.40, $13.90, $14.70, $15.00.

(a) Describe the trend. The price rose for two months, fell to a low of $10.50 at month 5, then recovered strongly to finish the year at its high of $15.00.

(b) Percentage change over the year:

\[\frac{15.00 - 12.00}{12.00} \times 100\% = 25\%\]

(c) Jo bought 800 shares at month 2 and panicked, selling at month 5. Find her loss.

\[800 \times (13.10 - 10.50) = 800 \times 2.60 = \$2\,080 \text{ loss}\]

Had she held on to the end of the year, she would have gained \(800 \times (15.00 - 13.10) = \$1\,520\) instead. Selling during a dip turns a temporary fall into a real loss.

Your turn

  1. Using the graph, find the percentage increase from the lowest closing price to the highest.
    Show working
    \[\frac{15.00 - 10.50}{10.50} \times 100\% = 42.9\%\]
  2. Which month had the largest one-month fall, and by how much? What does this tell an investor about the share?
    Show working

    Month 3 to month 4: \(12.40 - 11.20 = \$1.20\), a fall of about 9.7%. A share that can lose nearly a tenth of its value in a month is volatile — a higher-risk investment.

Theory

Inflation and appreciation

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. Because prices rise, the same amount of money buys less — its purchasing power falls. Appreciation is the opposite of depreciation: an asset gaining value. Both use the compound-growth formula.

Inflation and appreciation

\[\text{Future price} = \text{Current price} \times (1 + i)^{n}\]

\(i\) = inflation (or appreciation) rate per period as a decimal, \(n\) = number of periods. Identical in structure to compound interest.

Worked example 9 — rising prices

A basket of groceries costs $185 today. If inflation runs at 3.2% per annum, what will it cost in 8 years?

\[185 \times 1.032^{8} = 185 \times 1.28779 = \$238.24\]

Worked example 10 — losing purchasing power

Marcus keeps $20,000 in a safe for 10 years. Inflation averages 2.8% per annum. What is that money worth in today's dollars at the end?

Prices have multiplied by \(1.028^{10} = 1.31805\), so the purchasing power is

\[\frac{20000}{1.31805} = \$15\,174.30\]

The $20,000 still says $20,000, but it buys what $15,174 buys today — a real loss of about $4,826.

Worked example 11 — beating inflation

An investment earns 5.5% per annum while inflation is 3%. Is the investor better off?

Over one year $1,000 becomes $1,055, but prices rise by 3%, so in today's dollars that is

\[\frac{1055}{1.03} = \$1\,024.27\]

The real return is about 2.4% — positive, so yes, but much less than the headline 5.5%. An investment paying less than the inflation rate loses purchasing power even though the balance grows.

Your turn

  1. A concert ticket costs $135 today. With inflation at 2.5% per annum, what will an equivalent ticket cost in 6 years?
    Show working
    \[135 \times 1.025^{6} = 135 \times 1.15969 = \$156.56\]
  2. A savings account pays 4.1% per annum while inflation is 4.6%. In today's dollars, what is $5,000 worth after one year? Is the saver better off?
    Show working

    The balance becomes \(5000 \times 1.041 = \$5\,205\). In today's dollars: \(5205 \div 1.046 = \$4\,976.10\).

    No — the rate is below inflation, so the saver loses about $24 of purchasing power even though the balance grew.

Evaluation

Comparing investment strategies

The syllabus asks you to compare and contrast savings accounts, term deposits, shares and property as investment strategies. Exam answers need specific trade-offs, not vague comments.

StrategyTypical returnRiskAccess to fundsOther
Savings account Low Very low — deposits are government guaranteed up to a limit Immediate Return may not beat inflation
Term deposit Low to moderate, fixed and known in advance Very low Locked for the term; penalties for early withdrawal Rate is certain, so easy to plan with
Shares Potentially high — capital growth plus dividends High — prices fluctuate and can fall Usually quick to sell Brokerage costs; dividends are not guaranteed
Property Moderate to high — capital growth plus rent Moderate; values can fall Slow — selling takes weeks or months Large deposit needed; stamp duty, rates, maintenance

The trade-off to state

Higher expected returns come with higher risk and, usually, less ready access to your money. Which is "best" depends on the investor's time frame, their tolerance for risk, and whether they may need the funds at short notice. Say which of these the question's scenario points to.

Worked example 12 — an exam-style judgement

Ana, 24, is saving for a house deposit she expects to need in 18 months. Her friend suggests shares. Comment.

Shares are poorly suited here. Over 18 months share prices can easily fall, and if the market drops just before she buys, her deposit shrinks exactly when she needs it. A term deposit gives a known return with effectively no risk over that horizon. Shares suit longer time frames, where there is time to recover from a downturn.
