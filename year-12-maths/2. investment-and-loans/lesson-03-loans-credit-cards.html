Theory

How a reducing-balance loan works

A reducing-balance loan is compound interest applied to money you owe, with a regular repayment subtracted each period. Every period follows the same two steps:

The monthly cycle

\[\text{Interest} = \text{Opening balance} \times r\] \[\text{Closing balance} = \text{Opening balance} + \text{Interest} - \text{Repayment}\]

\(r\) is the interest rate per repayment period. For a monthly loan at an annual rate, \(r = \dfrac{\text{annual rate}}{12}\).

Because interest is charged on the current balance, and that balance falls each period, the interest portion of each repayment shrinks while the principal portion grows. This is why loans feel slow at the start.

Worked example 1 — one month

A loan of $20,000 at 9% per annum is repaid at $450 per month. Find the balance after one month.

Monthly rate: \(r = \dfrac{0.09}{12} = 0.0075\).

\[\text{Interest} = 20000 \times 0.0075 = \$150\] \[\text{Balance} = 20000 + 150 - 450 = \$19\,700\]

Of the $450 repayment, $150 went to interest and only $300 reduced the debt.

A warning worth remembering

If the repayment is less than the interest charged, the balance grows and the loan is never repaid. In the example above, any repayment below $150 per month would mean the debt increases forever.

Your turn

  1. A loan of $8,400 at 10.2% per annum is repaid at $300 per month. Find the interest and the balance after one month.
    Show working

    \(r = 0.102 \div 12 = 0.0085\). Interest \(= 8400 \times 0.0085 = \$71.40\).

    Balance \(= 8400 + 71.40 - 300 = \$8\,171.40\).

  2. A $45,000 loan is charged 7.8% per annum, calculated monthly. What must the monthly repayment be greater than for the loan ever to be repaid?
    Show working

    First month's interest: \(45000 \times \dfrac{0.078}{12} = \$292.50\). The repayment must be more than $292.50, or the balance never falls.

Modelling a loan in a spreadsheet

The syllabus asks you to use a spreadsheet to model and solve problems involving a reducing balance loan. Lay out the parameters first:

Then the table from row 7, with month numbers in column A:

The one formula that makes it work is B8 =E7: each month's opening balance is the previous month's closing balance. Fill row 8 down and the whole loan appears.

  • The loan is repaid in the first month where column E is zero or negative.
  • Total interest is =SUM(C7:C###) down to that row.
  • Changing B4 (the repayment) and watching the final row move is exactly the "examine the effect of changing the repayment" skill.
  • Charting column E against column A shows the balance curving down — steeply at the end, shallowly at the start.

The last repayment

The final repayment is almost always smaller than the rest, because the balance left is less than a full repayment. If the second-to-last closing balance is $312.40 and the monthly rate is 1%, the final payment is \(312.40 \times 1.01 = \$315.52\), not the usual $500.

Application

Extra repayments and repayment frequency

The syllabus asks you to examine the effect of changing the repayment frequency, additional repayments or a lump sum on the term and cost of a loan. The effects are much larger than students expect.

Why extra repayments work so well

Every extra dollar paid comes straight off the principal, so it never accrues interest again — and neither does the interest it would have generated in every future month. The saving compounds.

Worked example 3

A $300,000 loan at 6% per annum over 30 years has repayments of about $1,799 per month. Compare with paying $2,000 per month.

$1 799/month$2 000/month
Time to repay30 yearsabout 22 years 4 months
Total repaidabout $647 600about $536 000
Total interestabout $347 600about $236 000

An extra $201 per month — about $24,100 of extra payments in total — saves roughly $111,600 in interest and nearly eight years.

Repayment frequency

Paying fortnightly instead of monthly also helps, for two reasons:

  • Interest is calculated on a smaller balance sooner, because money comes off the principal earlier in each month.
  • You make more payments per year. Paying half the monthly amount every fortnight gives 26 half-payments = 13 monthly equivalents per year, not 12 — a whole extra month's repayment, usually unnoticed.

Lump sums

A lump sum early in the loan is far more valuable than the same amount late, because it removes interest from every remaining period. $10,000 paid in year 1 of a 30-year loan saves much more than $10,000 paid in year 25.

Your turn

  1. A home loan's monthly repayment is $1,640. The borrower switches to paying $820 every fortnight. How much extra is repaid each year?
    Show working

    Fortnightly: \(26 \times 820 = \$21\,320\). Monthly: \(12 \times 1640 = \$19\,680\).

    An extra $1,640 a year — one whole monthly repayment.

  2. A borrower owes $250,000 at 6% per annum (monthly) and pays a $10,000 lump sum. How much interest does this save in the very next month? Why is the total saving much larger than this?
    Show working

    Next month: \(10000 \times 0.005 = \$50\) less interest.

    That $50 is saved again in every remaining month, and the money that would have gone to interest reduces the principal instead, so the savings compound over the rest of the loan.

Theory

Amount owing, total paid and time to repay

The syllabus asks you to solve problems involving reducing balance loans, including total amount paid, repayments, amount owing and time to repay. A graph of the balance over the life of the loan answers most of these at a glance.

Three relationships to know

\[\text{Total paid} = \text{repayment} \times \text{number of repayments}\] \[\text{Total interest} = \text{total paid} - \text{amount borrowed}\] \[\text{Interest paid so far} = \text{paid so far} - (\text{amount borrowed} - \text{amount owing})\]

Balance of a $300,000 loan at 6% per annum, repaid at $1,799 per month over 30 years.

The curve is shallow at first and steep at the end: early repayments are mostly interest, late repayments are mostly principal.

Worked example 4

Use the graph of the $300,000 loan.

(a) About how much is owed after 15 years? Reading the graph: about $213,000. Halfway through the term, only about 29% of the loan has been repaid.

(b) When is half the loan repaid? The balance reaches $150,000 after about 21 years.

(c) Find the total paid and the total interest.

\[1799 \times 360 = \$647\,640 \qquad 647640 - 300000 = \$347\,640\]

  2. After 10 years the balance is $251,000. How much was paid in the first 10 years, and how much of it was interest?

Exam answer structure: state both sides. "The 7-year term is easier to afford monthly ($396.71 vs $787.75), but costs $4,965 more in total interest, so it suits a borrower with limited monthly income who accepts the higher overall cost."

Worked example 6 — exam style

Zara borrows $18,000 for a car. Option A: 3 years at 7.8% per annum, repayments $562.40 per month. Option B: 5 years at 6.9% per annum, repayments $355.57 per month.

(a) Find the total interest for each option. [2 marks]

\[\text{A: } 562.40 \times 36 - 18000 = \$2\,246.40 \qquad \text{B: } 355.57 \times 60 - 18000 = \$3\,334.20\]

(b) Option B has the lower interest rate. Explain why it costs more. [1 mark]

Interest is charged for 60 months instead of 36, and the balance falls more slowly, so the lower rate is applied to a larger balance for longer.

(c) Zara can afford $450 per month. Advise her. [2 marks]

Option A ($562.40) is beyond her budget. She should take Option B but pay $450 per month rather than the minimum $355.57: the loan is then cleared in 46 months with about $2,514 interest — cheaper than Option A and within her budget.

Application

Credit cards

The syllabus asks you to explain how credit cards are an example of a reducing balance loan. They are — with three differences that make them expensive.

A credit card as a reducing-balance loan

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, repayments reduce that balance, and the next month's interest is charged on what is left. That is exactly the reducing-balance cycle. What differs: the interest rate is far higher, the balance changes constantly as you spend, and the required minimum repayment is very small.

The interest-free period

Most cards offer up to 55 interest-free days on purchases, but only if you pay the entire closing balance by the due date. Pay even slightly less and interest is typically charged on the full balance from each purchase date — the interest-free benefit is lost entirely.

Daily interest on credit cards

\[\text{Daily rate} = \frac{\text{annual rate}}{365} \qquad I = \text{balance} \times \frac{\text{annual rate}}{365} \times \text{days}\]

Cash advances usually attract a higher rate and get no interest-free period — interest starts from the day of withdrawal.

Worked example 7

A card charges 19.99% per annum. A balance of $2,400 is carried for 28 days. Find the interest.

\[I = 2400 \times \frac{0.1999}{365} \times 28 = 2400 \times 0.00054767 \times 28\] \[= \$36.80\]

Worked example 8 — minimum repayments

A $5,000 balance at 20% per annum with a minimum repayment of 2% of the balance (at least $25). What happens in month 1?

Monthly interest: \(5000 \times \dfrac{0.20}{12} = \$83.33\).

Minimum repayment: \(0.02 \times 5000 = \$100\).

Only \(100 - 83.33 = \$16.67\) reduces the debt — less than 17 cents in the dollar. Because the minimum falls as the balance falls, paying only the minimum takes decades and can cost more in interest than the original purchases.

Fees to know

  • Annual fee — charged whether you use the card or not.
  • Late payment fee — a flat charge for missing the due date.
  • Cash advance fee — a percentage of the withdrawal, on top of interest from day one.
  • Foreign transaction fee — typically around 3% of overseas purchases.

Credit card rates (often 18–22% per annum) are far above home loan rates (around 6%) and personal loan rates (around 10–14%), because the debt is unsecured — there is no asset the lender can claim if you do not repay.

Class activity

Design a repayment plan

About 10 minutes · pairs. Sam needs a $20,000 loan at 9% per annum (calculated monthly) for a used car. Use the repayment table builder.

  1. Find the smallest monthly repayment (to the nearest $50) for which the loan is ever repaid. Why does it have to be more than $150?
  2. Record the months to repay and the total interest for repayments of $450, $650 and $850.
  3. Which repayment clears the loan in three years or less?
  4. Going from $450 to $650 adds $200 per month. How much interest does it save? Is the saving from $650 to $850 bigger or smaller? Explain.
  5. Sam earns $3,200 a month after tax and pays $1,400 in rent. Recommend a repayment, justifying it with your numbers.
Discussion points

1. $200 per month (186 months, about $17,106 interest). The first month's interest is \(20000 \times 0.0075 = \$150\); a repayment of $150 only covers the interest, so the balance never falls.

RepaymentMonthsTotal interest
$45055 (4y 7m)$4,419
$65036 (3y 0m)$2,824
$85026 (2y 2m)$2,087

3. $650 or more ($600 takes 39 months).

4. $450 → $650 saves about $1,596; $650 → $850 saves only about $737. Each extra dollar still saves interest, but the loan is already short, so there is less interest left to save.

5. Many answers are reasonable. For example, $650 clears the loan in 3 years and saves $1,596 compared with $450, while leaving Sam $1,150 a month after rent and repayments for living costs and savings.
