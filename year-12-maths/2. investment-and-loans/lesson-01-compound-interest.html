Simple & Compound Interest

Simple interest is paid only on what you originally invested. Compound interest is paid on the interest too — which turns a straight line into an exponential curve, and is the single most important idea in financial mathematics.

Theory

Simple interest

With simple interest, the interest is calculated on the original amount only. The same dollar amount is added every period.

Simple interest

\[I = Prn\]

\(I\) = interest earned,   \(P\) = principal (the amount invested),
\(r\) = interest rate per period, as a decimal,   \(n\) = number of periods.
The total value is then \(A = P + I\).

The two traps: the rate must be a decimal (6% → 0.06), and \(r\) and \(n\) must use the same time unit.

Worked example 1

$7,500 is invested at 4.8% per annum simple interest for 6 years. Find the interest earned and the final value.

\[I = Prn = 7500 \times 0.048 \times 6 = \$2\,160\] \[A = 7500 + 2160 = \$9\,660\]

The investment earns \(7500 \times 0.048 = \$360\) every single year — a constant amount, which is why simple interest graphs are linear.

Worked example 2 — matching the units

$4,000 is invested at 7.2% per annum simple interest for 30 months. Find \(I\).

The rate is per year but the time is in months. Convert the time:

\[n = \frac{30}{12} = 2.5 \text{ years}\] \[I = 4000 \times 0.072 \times 2.5 = \$720\]

Alternatively convert the rate to a monthly rate of \(0.072 \div 12 = 0.006\) and use \(n = 30\): \(4000 \times 0.006 \times 30 = \$720\) ✓ Either works — just be consistent.

Your turn

  1. $3,600 is invested at 3.75% per annum simple interest for 5 years. Find the interest and the final value.
    Show working
    \[I = 3600 \times 0.0375 \times 5 = \$675 \qquad A = 3600 + 675 = \$4\,275\]
  2. An investment of $5,200 earned $546 simple interest over 3 years. Find the annual interest rate.
    Show working

    Rearrange \(I = Prn\): \(r = \dfrac{I}{Pn} = \dfrac{546}{5200 \times 3} = 0.035\).

    Answer: 3.5% per annum.

Theory

Compound interest and future value

With compound interest, each period's interest is added to the balance and then earns interest itself. The balance is multiplied by \((1 + r)\) every period — exactly the exponential model \(y = ka^x\) from Topic 1.

Compound interest

\[FV = PV(1 + r)^{n}\]

\(FV\) = future value of the investment,   \(PV\) = present value (what you invest now),
\(r\) = interest rate per compounding period, as a decimal,   \(n\) = number of compounding periods.
The interest earned is \(I = FV - PV\).

Worked example 3

$7,500 is invested at 4.8% per annum compounded annually for 6 years. Compare with Worked example 1.

\[FV = 7500(1 + 0.048)^{6} = 7500 \times 1.048^{6}\] \[= 7500 \times 1.32493 = \$9\,937.00\]

The interest is \(9937.00 - 7500 = \$2\,437.00\), which is $277 more than the $2,160 earned under simple interest — the interest on the interest.

Theory

Compounding periods

Interest is often compounded more often than yearly. When it is, you must convert both the rate and the number of periods.

Converting for the compounding period

\[r = \frac{\text{annual rate}}{\text{periods per year}} \qquad n = (\text{years}) \times (\text{periods per year})\]

Monthly: divide the rate by 12, multiply the years by 12. Quarterly: 4. Six-monthly: 2. Daily: 365.

Annually1\(r = 0.08\)\(n = 5\)
Six-monthly2\(r = 0.04\)\(n = 10\)
Quarterly4\(r = 0.02\)\(n = 20\)
Monthly12\(r = 0.0066\overline{6}\)\(n = 60\)

Worked example 5

$12,000 is invested at 6% per annum compounded quarterly for 4 years.

Quarterly means 4 periods per year:

\[r = \frac{0.06}{4} = 0.015 \qquad n = 4 \times 4 = 16\] \[FV = 12000(1.015)^{16} = 12000 \times 1.26899 = \$15\,227.87\]

Compare with annual compounding: \(12000 \times 1.06^{4} = \$15\,149.72\). Compounding more often earns more — here, $78.15 more.

Compounding period comparator

Set the principal, annual rate and term, then compare the future value under each compounding frequency. Notice the gains get smaller as the frequency increases.

Your turn

  1. State \(r\) and \(n\) for (a) 7.2% per annum compounded six-monthly for 4 years and (b) 7.2% per annum compounded quarterly for 30 months.
    Show working

    (a) \(r = 0.072 \div 2 = 0.036\), \(n = 4 \times 2 = 8\).

    (b) \(r = 0.072 \div 4 = 0.018\); 30 months is 2.5 years, so \(n = 2.5 \times 4 = 10\).

  2. $8,000 is invested at 4.8% per annum compounded monthly for 3 years. Find the future value.
    Show working

Theory

Finding the present value or the rate

The syllabus asks you to calculate the future value, present value or interest rate. Each is the same formula rearranged.

Rearrangements

\[PV = \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^{n}} \qquad\qquad r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/n} - 1\]

Present value answers "how much must I invest now?". The rate formula answers "what rate turned this into that?".

Worked example 6 — present value

How much must be invested now at 5.5% per annum compounded annually to have $50,000 in 9 years?

\[PV = \frac{50000}{(1.055)^{9}} = \frac{50000}{1.61909} = \$30\,880.04\]

So about $30,880 must be invested today.

Worked example 7 — finding the rate

An investment of $6,000 grew to $9,000 in 7 years, compounded annually. Find the annual rate to one decimal place.

\[r = \left(\frac{9000}{6000}\right)^{1/7} - 1 = 1.5^{1/7} - 1\] \[= 1.05957 - 1 = 0.05957\]

The rate is about 6.0% per annum.

Practise the formula

Round money to the nearest cent and rates to one decimal place.

Worked example 8 — exam style

Grandparents want to give their grandchild $25,000 on her 18th birthday. She has just turned 5. They can invest in an account paying 4.2% per annum compounded monthly.

(a) How much must they invest today? [2 marks]

The term is \(18 - 5 = 13\) years, so \(n = 13 \times 12 = 156\) and \(r = 0.042 \div 12 = 0.0035\).

\[PV = \frac{25000}{1.0035^{156}} = \$14\,495.37\]

(b) How much of the gift will be interest? [1 mark]

\[25000 - 14495.37 = \$10\,504.63\]

(c) They can only afford $14,000. What annual rate, compounded annually, would still reach $25,000? Give your answer to one decimal place. [2 marks]

\[r = \left(\frac{25000}{14000}\right)^{1/13} - 1 = 0.0456\]

About 4.6% per annum, compounded annually.

Your turn

  1. How much must be invested now at 6% per annum compounded quarterly to have $12,000 in 4 years?
    Show working

    \(r = 0.015\), \(n = 16\).

    \[PV = \frac{12000}{1.015^{16}} = \$9\,456.37\]
  2. $2,500 grew to $3,200 in 5 years with annual compounding. Find the annual rate to one decimal place.
    Show working
    \[r = \left(\frac{3200}{2500}\right)^{1/5} - 1 = 1.28^{0.2} - 1 = 0.0506\]

    Answer: about 5.1% per annum.

Application

Comparing offers and reaching a target

Banks advertise rates with different compounding periods, so the headline rate alone does not tell you which account is better. The syllabus asks you to examine the effect of varying the interest rate, term or compounding period; this is where it matters in practice.

Two practical techniques

Comparing offers: find what the same amount (say $1,000) grows to after one year under each offer. The larger value is the better account.

Time to reach a target: build a table of \(FV\) for successive periods and find the first period where \(FV\) passes the target. The answer is a whole number of compounding periods, because interest is only added at the end of each period.

Worked example 9

(a) Bank A offers 5.2% per annum compounded annually. Bank B offers 5.1% per annum compounded monthly. Which is better?

\[\text{A: } 1000 \times 1.052 = \$1\,052.00 \qquad \text{B: } 1000 \times \left(1 + \tfrac{0.051}{12}\right)^{12} = \$1\,052.21\]

Bank B is (just) better, despite the lower advertised rate — monthly compounding more than makes up the 0.1% difference.

(b) $8,000 is invested in Bank A. After how many years does it first exceed $10,000?

Years345
\(8000 \times 1.052^{n}\)9 314.029 798.3510 307.86

It first exceeds $10,000 after 5 years.

Your turn

  1. Which is better: 6% per annum compounded quarterly, or 6.1% per annum compounded annually?
    Show working

    Quarterly: \(1000 \times 1.015^4 = \$1\,061.36\). Annually: \(1000 \times 1.061 = \$1\,061.00\).

    6% compounded quarterly is better, by 36 cents per $1,000 per year.

  2. $15,000 is invested at 4.4% per annum compounded quarterly. After how many quarters does it first exceed $17,000?
    Show working

    \(r = 0.011\). Trial: \(15000 \times 1.011^{11} = \$16\,918.19\) and \(15000 \times 1.011^{12} = \$17\,104.29\).

    Answer: after 12 quarters (3 years).

Technology

Modelling investments in a spreadsheet

The syllabus asks you to use a spreadsheet to numerically and graphically model investments using both simple and compound interest. The structure:

CellLabelValue
B1Principal10000
B2Rate per period0.06
A — Year \(n\)B — SimpleC — Compound
Row 50=$B$1=$B$1
Row 6=A5+1=$B$1*(1+$B$2*A6)=C5*(1+$B$2)

Two approaches to the compound column, both valid:

  • Recursive (shown above): =C5*(1+$B$2) — each row multiplies the previous balance. This mirrors how the money actually behaves and makes it easy to insert extra deposits later.
  • Direct formula: =$B$1*(1+$B$2)^A6 — computes any row independently from the formula.

Charting columns B and C against column A as a line graph shows the straight line and the curve diverging — exactly the comparison the syllabus asks for. Changing B2 and watching both update is the "examine the effect of varying the interest rate" skill.

Class activity

What grows money fastest: rate, term or compounding?

About 10 minutes · pairs. The baseline is $10,000 at 6% per annum compounded annually for 20 years. Before touching the widgets, each person writes down a prediction: which single change will add the most money?

  1. In the simple vs compound widget, set principal $10,000, rate 6%, 20 years. Record the compound value.
  2. Double the rate to 12% (keep 20 years). Record the compound value. Did the value double?
  3. Return to 6% and double the term to 40 years. Record the value.
  4. In the compounding period comparator, set $10,000, 6%, 20 years. How much extra does monthly compounding give compared with annual? And daily compared with monthly?
  5. Rank the three changes from biggest to smallest effect, and check against your prediction. What advice would you give a 20-year-old about saving?
Discussion points

Doubling the rate roughly triples the value here, because the rate is inside the power. Doubling the term has the biggest effect: the balance at 20 years (about 3.2 times the principal) is multiplied by the same factor again.

Compounding more often helps, but by much less, and the gains shrink: daily compounding adds less than $100 more than monthly.

Advice: time in the market matters most — start early. Next, chase a better rate. The compounding frequency is a tie-breaker.
