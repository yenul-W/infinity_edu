Theory

Ratios and their relationship with fractions

A ratio compares two or more quantities of the same kind, measured in the same units. It is written with a colon: \(3 : 5\).

Ratios and fractions

In the ratio \(a : b\), the total is \(a + b\) parts. So the first quantity is \(\dfrac{a}{a+b}\) of the whole, and the second is \(\dfrac{b}{a+b}\).

The ratio \(a : b\) can also be written as the fraction \(\dfrac{a}{b}\) — this is the comparison of one to the other, not a share of the whole. Keep the two readings distinct.

Worked example 1

A class has boys and girls in the ratio \(4 : 5\).

(a) What fraction are boys? Total parts \(= 4 + 5 = 9\), so boys are \(\dfrac{4}{9}\) of the class.

(b) How many times as many girls as boys? \(\dfrac{5}{4} = 1.25\) — there are 1.25 times as many girls.

(c) If there are 36 students, how many are girls?

\[\frac{5}{9} \times 36 = 20 \text{ girls}\]

Two rules that must hold

  • Same units. \(50\text{ cm} : 2\text{ m}\) must be converted before simplifying — it is \(50 : 200 = 1 : 4\), not \(50 : 2\).
  • Order matters. \(3 : 5\) is not the same as \(5 : 3\). Write the quantities in the order the question names them.

Your turn

  1. A jar holds red and blue marbles in the ratio \(2 : 7\). What fraction are red? If there are 63 marbles, how many are blue?
    Show working

    Total parts 9, so red is \(\frac{2}{9}\). Blue: \(\frac{7}{9} \times 63 = 49\) marbles.

  2. Write \(1.5\text{ m} : 60\text{ cm}\) as a ratio in simplest form.
    Show working

    Same units first: \(150 : 60\). Divide by 30: \(5 : 2\).

Method

Expressing a ratio in simplest form

Divide every part by the highest common factor, so the parts are whole numbers with no common factor left.

Worked example 2

Simplify each ratio.

(a) \(18 : 24\) — divide both by 6:

\[18 : 24 = 3 : 4\]

(b) \(0.6 : 1.5\) — multiply both by 10 to clear the decimals, then divide by 3:

\[0.6 : 1.5 = 6 : 15 = 2 : 5\]

(c) \(\tfrac{2}{3} : \tfrac{1}{4}\) — multiply both by 12 (the LCD):

\[\tfrac{2}{3} : \tfrac{1}{4} = 8 : 3\]

(d) \(45\text{ min} : 2\text{ h}\) — convert to the same unit first:

\[45 : 120 = 3 : 8\]

Method

Dividing a quantity in a given ratio

The three-step method

  1. Add the parts to find the total number of parts.
  2. Divide the quantity by the total parts to find the value of one part.
  3. Multiply each part of the ratio by that value.

Always check your shares add back to the original total. This catches nearly every arithmetic slip.

Worked example 3

Divide $4,200 between three people in the ratio \(2 : 3 : 5\).

Step 1: total parts \(= 2 + 3 + 5 = 10\).

Step 2: one part \(= 4200 \div 10 = \$420\).

Step 3:

\[2 \times 420 = \$840 \qquad 3 \times 420 = \$1\,260 \qquad 5 \times 420 = \$2\,100\]

Check: \(840 + 1260 + 2100 = \$4\,200\) ✓

Worked example 4 — working backwards

Two business partners share profits in the ratio \(7 : 4\). The first receives $21,000 more than the second. Find the total profit.

The difference is \(7 - 4 = 3\) parts, and that equals $21,000, so

\[\text{one part} = \frac{21000}{3} = \$7\,000\]

Total parts \(= 7 + 4 = 11\), so the total profit is

\[11 \times 7000 = \$77\,000\]

Check: shares are $49,000 and $28,000, a difference of $21,000 ✓

Method

The unitary method

The unitary method

Find the value of one unit, then scale up to the quantity you want. It works for almost every proportion problem, and it is easy to show clearly for method marks.

Worked example 5

If 7 identical textbooks cost $182, how much do 12 cost?

One book: \(182 \div 7 = \$26\).

Twelve books: \(12 \times 26 = \$312\).

Worked example 6 — scaling a recipe

A recipe for 6 people needs 450 g of rice. How much is needed for 10 people?

One person: \(450 \div 6 = 75\) g.

Ten people: \(10 \times 75 = 750\) g.

Worked example 7 — concrete mix

Concrete is mixed with cement, sand and gravel in the ratio \(1 : 2 : 4\). How much gravel is needed for 2.8 tonnes of concrete?

Total parts \(= 1 + 2 + 4 = 7\), so one part \(= 2.8 \div 7 = 0.4\) t.

\[\text{Gravel} = 4 \times 0.4 = 1.6 \text{ tonnes}\]

Your turn

  1. 5 L of paint covers 32 m². How much paint is needed for 84 m²?
    Show working

    One square metre needs \(5 \div 32 = 0.15625\) L.

    \(84 \times 0.15625 = 13.125\) L, so about 13.1 L (buy 14 L if sold in whole litres).

  2. A car uses 38 L of fuel to travel 520 km. How far can it travel on 50 L?
    Show working

    One litre: \(520 \div 38 = 13.684\) km. Fifty litres: \(50 \times 13.684 = 684.2\) km.

Theory

Scale and map scales

Scale as a ratio

\[\text{scale} = \text{drawing length} : \text{actual length}\]

A scale of \(1 : 500\) means 1 unit on the drawing represents 500 of the same units in reality. Scales have no units — both sides are the same kind of measurement, so the units cancel.

The two conversions

\[\text{actual} = \text{drawing} \times \text{scale factor}\] \[\text{drawing} = \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{scale factor}}\]

For \(1 : n\), the scale factor is \(n\). Going from the map to the world, multiply; coming back, divide.

Worked example 8

A map has a scale of \(1 : 25\,000\). Two towns are 8.4 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they in reality?

\[8.4 \times 25000 = 210\,000 \text{ cm}\]

Convert to sensible units: \(210\,000 \text{ cm} = 2\,100 \text{ m} = 2.1 \text{ km}\).

The unit conversion is where marks are lost. Remember \(100\text{ cm} = 1\text{ m}\) and \(1000\text{ m} = 1\text{ km}\), so \(100\,000\text{ cm} = 1\text{ km}\).

Worked example 9 — the other direction

On a plan with scale \(1 : 200\), how long should a wall of 14 m actually be drawn?

Convert to a single unit first: \(14\text{ m} = 1\,400\text{ cm}\).

\[\frac{1400}{200} = 7 \text{ cm}\]

Worked example 10 — finding the scale

A 45 m building is drawn 18 cm long. Find the scale.

Same units: \(45\text{ m} = 4\,500\text{ cm}\).

\[18 : 4500 = 1 : 250\]

Divide both sides by 18 to get the \(1 : n\) form.

Application

Reading scale drawings and building plans

Building plans use standard symbols and a stated scale. The syllabus asks you to estimate and compare quantities, materials and costs using measurements taken from scale drawings and building plans.

A floor plan at \(1 : 100\): the 17 cm width represents 17 m, and the 9 cm depth represents 9 m.

Worked example 11 — costing from a plan

On the plan above (scale \(1 : 100\)), the living room measures 9 cm × 5.5 cm. Carpet costs $68 per square metre. Find the cost to carpet the living room.

Actual dimensions: at \(1 : 100\), 1 cm represents 100 cm = 1 m, so

\[9 \text{ cm} \rightarrow 9 \text{ m} \qquad 5.5 \text{ cm} \rightarrow 5.5 \text{ m}\]

Area: \(9 \times 5.5 = 49.5\) m².

Cost: \(49.5 \times 68 = \$3\,366\).

The scale-factor trap with area

If lengths scale by \(k\), areas scale by \(k^{2}\) and volumes by \(k^{3}\). At a scale of \(1 : 100\), 1 cm² on the plan represents \(100^{2} = 10\,000\) cm² \(= 1\) m².

The safe method is always to convert the lengths first, then calculate the area from the real dimensions — as in the worked example above.

Worked example 12 — exam style

A plan with scale \(1 : 100\) shows a rectangular deck 6.4 cm long and 3.5 cm wide. Decking boards are sold in packs that cover 2.5 m², at $260 per pack. A railing will run along the two short sides and one long side, at $85 per metre.

(a) Find the actual area of the deck. [1 mark]

At \(1 : 100\), 1 cm represents 1 m: the deck is 6.4 m by 3.5 m, area \(22.4\) m².

(b) How many packs of boards are needed, and what do they cost? [2 marks]

\(22.4 \div 2.5 = 8.96\), so 9 packs (packs cannot be split): \(9 \times 260 = \$2\,340\).

(c) Find the total cost of boards and railing. [2 marks]

Railing length: \(6.4 + 3.5 + 3.5 = 13.4\) m, costing \(13.4 \times 85 = \$1\,139\). Total: \(2340 + 1139 = \$3\,479\).

Method

Constructing a scale drawing

The syllabus asks you to construct scale drawings, not only read them. The key decision is the scale: big enough to show detail, small enough to fit the page.

  1. Measure the real object's longest dimensions.
  2. Divide each by the space available on the page to find the smallest scale factor that fits, then round up to a convenient scale (1 : 20, 1 : 50, 1 : 100, 1 : 200 …).
  3. Convert every real length to a drawing length, in one unit.
  4. Draw with a ruler, and label the drawing with its scale.

Worked example 13

A backyard is 24 m by 15 m and contains a 6 m by 4 m shed. Draw it on a page with a drawing area of 25 cm by 18 cm.

Choose the scale. \(2400 \text{ cm} \div 25 = 96\) and \(1500 \text{ cm} \div 18 = 83.3\), so the scale factor must be at least 96. Round up to \(1 : 100\).

FeatureRealDrawing at 1 : 100
Yard24 m × 15 m24 cm × 15 cm
Shed6 m × 4 m6 cm × 4 cm

The yard fits the 25 cm by 18 cm area with room for a title and the scale.

Your turn

  1. A bedroom is 7.2 m by 4.8 m. It must be drawn in a space 15 cm by 10 cm. Which scale is best: \(1 : 20\), \(1 : 50\) or \(1 : 100\)?
    Show working

    \(1 : 20\) gives 36 cm by 24 cm — too big. \(1 : 50\) gives 14.4 cm by 9.6 cm — fits. \(1 : 100\) gives 7.2 cm by 4.8 cm — fits, but uses only a quarter of the space.

    Answer: \(1 : 50\), the largest scale that fits.

  2. At \(1 : 50\), what are the drawing lengths of a 0.9 m door and a 2.0 m by 1.5 m bed?
    Show working

    Door: \(90 \div 50 = 1.8\) cm. Bed: \(200 \div 50 = 4\) cm by \(150 \div 50 = 3\) cm.

Class activity

Floor a flat on a budget

About 10 minutes · groups of three. A \(1 : 100\) plan of a flat shows four rooms. The owner has $5,000 for new flooring, and the kitchen must be tiled. Use the plan costing calculator with the scale set to \(1 : 100\).

RoomOn the plan
Living6.5 cm × 4.5 cm
Bedroom 14 cm × 3.5 cm
Bedroom 23.5 cm × 3 cm
Kitchen3 cm × 2.5 cm

Prices: carpet $45/m², timber $95/m², tiles $70/m².

  1. Find the real area of each room and the total area.
  2. Would timber throughout the living room and bedrooms (with a tiled kitchen) fit the budget?
  3. Design a combination that fits the budget and puts timber in at least one room. Show your total.
  4. Justify your design to the owner in two sentences.
Discussion points

1. Living 29.25 m², bedroom 1 14 m², bedroom 2 10.5 m², kitchen 7.5 m²; total 61.25 m².

2. No: kitchen tiles \(7.5 \times 70 = \$525\), plus timber \(53.75 \times 95 = \$5\,106.25\), gives $5,631.25 — over budget.

3. For example, tiles in the kitchen ($525), timber in the living room ($2,778.75) and carpet in both bedrooms ($1,102.50): total $4,406.25. Timber in the living room and bedroom 1 with carpet in bedroom 2 comes to $5,106.25 — just over.

4. Answers vary: timber is most durable in the busiest room, while carpet keeps bedrooms warm and cheap, leaving about $594 spare for installation or underlay.
