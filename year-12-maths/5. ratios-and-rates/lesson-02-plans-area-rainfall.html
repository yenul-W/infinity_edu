Method

Perimeter and area of composite shapes

Most blocks of land are made from rectangles, triangles and part-circles joined together. The technique is always the same: split, calculate, combine.

Area formulas you need

\[A_{\text{rectangle}} = lw \qquad A_{\text{triangle}} = \tfrac12 bh \qquad A_{\text{circle}} = \pi r^{2}\] \[A_{\text{trapezium}} = \tfrac12 h(a + b)\]

In the trapezium formula, \(a\) and \(b\) are the two parallel sides and \(h\) is the perpendicular distance between them.

Perimeter warning

The perimeter is the distance around the outside only. When you split a shape into pieces, the internal cut lines are not part of the perimeter — but they are needed for the areas.

An L-shaped block split into rectangles. The dashed line is an internal cut — it helps with area but is not part of the perimeter.

Worked example 1

Find the area and perimeter of the L-shaped block above.

Area by splitting. The left rectangle is 15 m × 12 m; the right rectangle is 15 m × 16 m:

\[A = (15 \times 12) + (15 \times 16) = 180 + 240 = 420 \text{ m}^{2}\]

Perimeter. Going around the outside: 12 + 15 + 4 + 15 + 16 + 30:

\[P = 12 + 15 + 4 + 15 + 16 + 30 = 92 \text{ m}\]

The 4 m step is the difference between the 16 m and 12 m sides. The dashed internal line is excluded.

Theory

The trapezoidal rule

When a boundary is curved or irregular — a river frontage, a coastline, an aerial photograph — no standard formula applies. The trapezoidal rule approximates the area by slicing the shape into strips and treating each as a trapezium.

One application (a single strip)

\[A \approx \frac{h}{2}(d_{f} + d_{l})\]

\(h\) is the width of the strip, and \(d_f\) and \(d_l\) are the first and last perpendicular offsets (the two parallel sides).

Several applications

\[A \approx \frac{h}{2}\Big[d_{f} + d_{l} + 2(d_{2} + d_{3} + \dots + d_{n-1})\Big]\]

The end offsets are counted once; every middle offset is counted twice, because each internal offset is shared by the strip on either side of it. \(h\) is the constant strip width.

Five offsets create four strips. \(d_1\) and \(d_5\) are counted once; \(d_2\), \(d_3\) and \(d_4\) are counted twice.

Worked example 2 — one application

A block of land is 30 m wide. The boundary offsets at each end are 18 m and 26 m. Estimate the area.

\[A \approx \frac{30}{2}(18 + 26) = 15 \times 44 = 660 \text{ m}^{2}\]

Worked example 3 — several applications

Offsets measured at 20 m intervals across a block are 12, 19, 23, 17 and 9 metres. Estimate the area.

Five offsets means four strips, with \(h = 20\):

\[A \approx \frac{20}{2}\Big[12 + 9 + 2(19 + 23 + 17)\Big]\] \[= 10\Big[21 + 2(59)\Big] = 10(21 + 118) = 10 \times 139 = 1\,390 \text{ m}^{2}\]

The two most common errors: doubling the end offsets, and using the number of offsets instead of the number of strips when finding \(h\).

Finding \(h\) from a total width

\[h = \frac{\text{total width}}{\text{number of strips}} = \frac{\text{total width}}{\text{number of offsets} - 1}\]

Application

Irregular blocks, aerial photos and maps

The syllabus asks you to calculate the perimeter or area of a section of land using the trapezoidal rule where appropriate, from a site plan, an aerial photograph, a radial survey or a map that includes a scale.

The workflow

  1. Measure the offsets on the photo or plan, in centimetres.
  2. Convert each to real metres using the scale.
  3. Apply the trapezoidal rule to the real measurements.
  4. State that the answer is an estimate.

Worked example 4

On an aerial photo with scale \(1 : 2\,000\), offsets measured at 1.5 cm intervals are 2.1, 3.4, 4.0, 3.1 and 1.8 cm. Estimate the real area of the block.

Convert lengths. At \(1 : 2\,000\), 1 cm represents 2,000 cm = 20 m:

\[h = 1.5 \times 20 = 30 \text{ m}\]

Offsets become \(42, 68, 80, 62, 36\) m.

Apply the rule (4 strips):

\[A \approx \frac{30}{2}\Big[42 + 36 + 2(68 + 80 + 62)\Big]\] \[= 15\Big[78 + 2(210)\Big] = 15(78 + 420) = 15 \times 498 = 7\,470 \text{ m}^{2}\]

That is about 0.75 hectares.

Why it is only an estimate

The rule replaces each curved segment with a straight line. Where the boundary bulges outward between offsets the rule underestimates; where it curves inward it overestimates. Using more, narrower strips improves the accuracy.

Application

Radial surveys

The syllabus lists the radial survey as another source for area and perimeter. A surveyor stands at a point O inside the block and measures the distance and bearing to each corner. Joining O to the corners splits the block into triangles.

Radial survey method

  • Angle at O between neighbouring corners = difference of their bearings. All the angles at O must sum to 360°.
  • Area of each triangle: \(\tfrac12 ab\sin\theta\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are the two distances from O and \(\theta\) is the angle between them.
  • Boundary length between neighbouring corners: cosine rule, \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta\).

Worked example 5

A triangular block ABC is surveyed from O: \(OA = 30\) m at \(040°\), \(OB = 45\) m at \(130°\), \(OC = 38\) m at \(250°\). Find its area and perimeter.

Angles at O: \(\angle AOB = 90°\), \(\angle BOC = 120°\), \(\angle COA = 360° - 250° + 40° = 150°\). Check: \(90 + 120 + 150 = 360\) ✓

Area:

\[\tfrac12(30)(45)\sin 90° + \tfrac12(45)(38)\sin 120° + \tfrac12(38)(30)\sin 150°\] \[= 675 + 740.45 + 285 = 1\,700.45 \text{ m}^2\]

Perimeter:

\[AB = \sqrt{30^2 + 45^2} = 54.08 \qquad BC = \sqrt{45^2 + 38^2 - 2(45)(38)\cos 120°} = 71.97\] \[CA = \sqrt{38^2 + 30^2 - 2(38)(30)\cos 150°} = 65.72\]

Perimeter \(\approx 54.08 + 71.97 + 65.72 = 191.77\) m.

Your turn

  1. A block is surveyed from O: \(OA = 20\) m at \(000°\), \(OB = 25\) m at \(080°\), \(OC = 22\) m at \(190°\), \(OD = 18\) m at \(280°\). Find its area.
    Show working

    Angles at O: 80°, 110°, 90°, 80° (sum 360° ✓).

    \[\tfrac12\big[20(25)\sin 80° + 25(22)\sin 110° + 22(18)\sin 90° + 18(20)\sin 80°\big] = 879.88 \text{ m}^2\]

    Show working

Application

Rainfall volume

Volume of rainfall

\[V = Ah\]

\(V\) is the volume, \(A\) is the area of land, and \(h\) is the depth of rain. The units must match — if \(A\) is in m² then \(h\) must be in metres, giving \(V\) in cubic metres.

The conversions you will need

\[1 \text{ m}^{3} = 1\,000 \text{ L} \qquad 1\text{ kL} = 1\,000\text{ L} = 1\text{ m}^{3}\] \[1\text{ mm} = 0.001\text{ m} \qquad 1\text{ ha} = 10\,000\text{ m}^{2}\]

Rainfall is almost always given in millimetres, so dividing by 1000 to get metres is the first step in nearly every question.

Worked example 6

How much water falls on a roof measuring 12 m by 8 m during 25 mm of rain?

Area: \(12 \times 8 = 96\) m².

Depth in metres: \(25 \text{ mm} = 0.025\) m.

\[V = 96 \times 0.025 = 2.4 \text{ m}^{3}\]

In litres: \(2.4 \times 1000 = 2\,400\) L.

Worked example 7 — a large area

A farm covers 45 hectares. How many litres fall during 18 mm of rain?

Area in m²: \(45 \times 10\,000 = 450\,000\) m².

Depth: \(18 \text{ mm} = 0.018\) m.

\[V = 450\,000 \times 0.018 = 8\,100 \text{ m}^{3}\]

In litres: \(8\,100 \times 1000 = 8\,100\,000\) L, or 8.1 megalitres.

Worked example 8 — working backwards

A 5,000 L tank collects runoff from a 60 m² roof. How much rain is needed to fill it, assuming no losses?

Convert the volume: \(5\,000\) L \(= 5\) m³.

\[5 = 60 \times h \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{5}{60} = 0.0833 \text{ m}\]

That is about 83 mm of rain.

Note the assumption: in reality some water is lost to splash, evaporation and gutter overflow, so more rain would be needed.

Application

Tanks and capacity

Volume of a prism or cylinder

\[V = Ah\]

The same formula again — \(A\) is the area of the uniform cross-section and \(h\) is the height or length. For a cylindrical tank, \(A = \pi r^{2}\), so \(V = \pi r^{2} h\).

Worked example 9

A cylindrical water tank has a diameter of 2.4 m and a height of 3 m. Find its capacity in litres.

Radius \(= 1.2\) m:

\[V = \pi (1.2)^{2}(3) = \pi \times 1.44 \times 3 = 13.57 \text{ m}^{3}\]

\(13.57 \times 1000 = 13\,572\) litres, or about 13.6 kL.

Worked example 10 — a full problem

A house has a roof catchment of 180 m². Annual rainfall averages 720 mm. A 22,000 L tank is installed. How many times could the tank be filled in a year, assuming 85% of the rain is captured?

Rain falling on the roof:

\[V = 180 \times 0.720 = 129.6 \text{ m}^{3} = 129\,600 \text{ L}\]

Captured at 85%: \(129\,600 \times 0.85 = 110\,160\) L.

Tank fills:

\[\frac{110\,160}{22\,000} = 5.01\]

About 5 tank-fills per year. The 15% loss covers splash, first-flush diversion and gutter overflow in heavy downpours.

Worked example 11 — exam style

A school plan at \(1 : 200\) shows a rectangular hall roof measuring 9 cm by 6.5 cm. In the driest month 34 mm of rain falls, and 90% of it can be captured. The school garden uses 4,000 L per month.

(a) Find the actual roof area. [1 mark]

1 cm represents 2 m: the roof is 18 m by 13 m \(= 234\) m².

(b) Will the roof supply the garden in the driest month? [2 marks]

\(V = 234 \times 0.034 = 7.956\) m³; captured \(7.956 \times 0.9 = 7.1604\) m³ \(= 7\,160\) L. This exceeds 4,000 L, so yes, with about 3,160 L to spare.

(c) A cylindrical tank 2 m in diameter must hold two months of garden use. How tall must it be? [2 marks]

Two months is 8,000 L \(= 8\) m³. With \(r = 1\):

\[8 = \pi (1)^2 h \;\Rightarrow\; h = \frac{8}{\pi} = 2.55 \text{ m}\]

Your turn

  1. Find the capacity in litres of a cylindrical tank 1.8 m in diameter and 2.2 m tall.
    Show working

    \(V = \pi (0.9)^2 (2.2) = 5.598\) m³ \(\approx 5\,598\) L.

  2. A rectangular tank is 2.5 m by 1.2 m by 1.5 m. How much rain on a 75 m² roof would fill it, assuming no losses?
    Show working

    Tank: \(2.5 \times 1.2 \times 1.5 = 4.5\) m³. \(\;4.5 = 75h \Rightarrow h = 0.06\) m \(= 60\) mm.

Class activity

How good is the trapezoidal rule?

About 10 minutes · pairs. A pond is 40 m long. Its width, measured every 10 m, is:

Distance along (m)010203040
Width (m)1025302510

The pond's true area, found by a detailed survey, is 933 m².

  1. Estimate the area with one strip (offsets 10 and 10, \(h = 40\)).
  2. Estimate it with two strips (offsets 10, 30, 10, \(h = 20\)).
  3. Use the trapezoidal rule calculator for four strips: set \(h = 10\) and the five offsets above.
  4. Find the percentage error of each estimate. What happens to the error as the number of strips doubles?
  5. Every estimate is too small. Use the shape of the boundary to explain why.
Discussion points
StripsEstimateError
1\(20(10 + 10) = 400\) m²57% under
2\(10[10 + 10 + 2(30)] = 800\) m²14% under
4\(5[10 + 10 + 2(25 + 30 + 25)] = 900\) m²3.6% under

4. Each doubling of the strips cuts the error to roughly a quarter. More, narrower strips follow the curve more closely.

5. The pond's edge bulges outward between offsets. The rule joins the offsets with straight lines, cutting off the bulges, so it underestimates.
