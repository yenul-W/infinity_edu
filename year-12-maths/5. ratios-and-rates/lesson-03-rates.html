Theory

Rates versus ratios

The difference

A ratio compares quantities of the same kind, in the same units, and has no units itself: \(3 : 5\).

A rate compares quantities of different kinds and always carries units: $4.50/kg, 80 km/h, 12 L/100 km.

RatioRate
ComparesSame kind of quantityDifferent kinds
UnitsNone — they cancelAlways stated, as "per"
ExampleCement to sand \(1 : 3\)Wage of $32 per hour
Written as\(a : b\)\(a\) per \(b\), or \(a/b\)

The word "per" is the signal: it means "for each one". A rate of $4.50/kg means $4.50 for each single kilogram.

Method

Unit rates and best buys

Finding a unit rate

\[\text{unit rate} = \frac{\text{total quantity}}{\text{number of units}}\]

A unit rate has 1 in the denominator. It is the only fair way to compare options of different sizes.

Worked example 1

A 2.5 kg bag of rice costs $8.75. Find the price per kilogram.

\[\frac{8.75}{2.5} = \$3.50 \text{ per kg}\]

Worked example 2 — comparing best buys

Which is better value: 750 g for $6.30, or 1.2 kg for $9.84?

Convert to the same unit and find the price per kilogram:

\[\text{Option A: } \frac{6.30}{0.75} = \$8.40/\text{kg}\] \[\text{Option B: } \frac{9.84}{1.2} = \$8.20/\text{kg}\]

Option B is better value, by 20 cents per kilogram.

Alternative check: you can instead compare quantity per dollar — A gives \(0.75 \div 6.30 = 0.119\) kg/$, B gives \(1.2 \div 9.84 = 0.122\) kg/$. Either way B wins, but be clear which way round you are comparing: with price per kg lower is better; with quantity per dollar higher is better.

Method

Converting between rates

The method

Convert the numerator and the denominator separately. Ask for each: am I moving to a bigger or a smaller unit? Bigger unit → smaller number.

Worked example 3 — km/h to m/s

Convert 90 km/h to metres per second.

\(90\) km \(= 90\,000\) m, and 1 hour \(= 3\,600\) s:

\[\frac{90\,000 \text{ m}}{3\,600 \text{ s}} = 25 \text{ m/s}\]

Shortcut: to go from km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6. To reverse it, multiply by 3.6.

Worked example 4 — a flow rate

A tap runs at 18 litres per minute. Express this in kilolitres per hour.

Per hour: \(18 \times 60 = 1\,080\) L/h.

In kilolitres: \(1\,080 \div 1000 = 1.08\) kL/h.

Worked example 5 — a pay rate

A salary of $78,000 per year — what is that per hour for a 38-hour week, working 46 weeks a year?

Total hours: \(38 \times 46 = 1\,748\) hours.

\[\frac{78\,000}{1\,748} = \$44.62 \text{ per hour}\]

Application

Speed, distance and time

The three forms

\[S = \frac{D}{T} \qquad D = S \times T \qquad T = \frac{D}{S}\]

The units must be consistent: km with hours gives km/h; metres with seconds gives m/s.

Time in decimal form

Times must be decimals of an hour, not hours-and-minutes. 2 hours 45 minutes is \(2 + \dfrac{45}{60} = 2.75\) hours, not 2.45. Going back, 0.4 hours is \(0.4 \times 60 = 24\) minutes.

Worked example 6

A car travels 245 km in 2 hours 48 minutes. Find its average speed.

Convert the time: \(2 + \dfrac{48}{60} = 2.8\) hours.

\[S = \frac{245}{2.8} = 87.5 \text{ km/h}\]

Worked example 7 — finding time

How long does a 380 km journey take at an average speed of 95 km/h? Give the answer in hours and minutes.

\[T = \frac{380}{95} = 4 \text{ hours}\]

Exactly 4 hours. If it had been 4.25 hours, that would be 4 hours 15 minutes, since \(0.25 \times 60 = 15\).

Worked example 8 — a journey in stages

A truck drives 120 km at 80 km/h, then 210 km at 105 km/h. Find the average speed for the whole journey.

Stage times: \(120 \div 80 = 1.5\) h and \(210 \div 105 = 2\) h.

Totals: distance \(= 330\) km, time \(= 3.5\) h.

\[S = \frac{330}{3.5} = 94.29 \text{ km/h}\]

Not the average of 80 and 105 (92.5). Average speed is always total distance ÷ total time, because more time is spent at one speed than the other.

    \(S = 156 \div 1.8\overline{3} = 85.09\) km/h.

Theory

Distance–time graphs

The syllabus asks you to interpret and analyse graphs to solve problems related to distance, speed and time. On a distance–time graph:

  • The gradient of each segment is the speed: rise (distance) over run (time). A steeper line means a faster speed.
  • A horizontal segment means stopped: time passes but distance does not change.
  • A segment sloping down means travelling back towards the start.

A family's day trip: distance from home against time since leaving.

Worked example 9

The graph passes through \((0, 0)\), \((2, 160)\), \((2.5, 160)\), \((4, 295)\), \((5, 295)\) and \((8.5, 0)\).

(a) Speed on each stage:

Out, part 22.5–4 h135 km90 km/h

(b) Average speed for the whole day, including stops:

\[\frac{295 + 295}{8.5} = \frac{590}{8.5} = 69.4 \text{ km/h}\]

The stops lower the average, even though the car never drove below 80 km/h.

Your turn

  1. Using the graph, how far from home was the family 3 hours after leaving?
    Show working

    At 3 h they had driven 0.5 h of the 90 km/h stage: \(160 + 90 \times 0.5 = 205\) km from home.

  2. Which stage has the steepest segment, and what does that tell you? How long were they stopped in total?
    Show working

Application

Fuel consumption

Fuel consumption rate

\[\text{Fuel consumption} = \frac{\text{fuel used (L)}}{\text{distance (km)}} \times 100 \quad \text{L/100 km}\]

Australian cars quote litres per 100 kilometres. A lower number is more efficient — the opposite of km/L, where higher is better.

Worked example 10

A car uses 43 litres to travel 520 km. Find its fuel consumption.

\[\frac{43}{520} \times 100 = 8.27 \text{ L/100 km}\]

Worked example 11 — cost of a trip

A car has a consumption of 9.4 L/100 km. Fuel costs $1.92 per litre. Find the fuel cost of a 680 km trip.

Fuel needed:

\[\frac{680}{100} \times 9.4 = 6.8 \times 9.4 = 63.92 \text{ L}\]

Cost: \(63.92 \times 1.92 = \$122.73\).

Worked example 12 — comparing vehicles

Car A uses 7.2 L/100 km; Car B uses 11.5 L/100 km. Over 15,000 km a year at $1.95 per litre, how much more does Car B cost to run?

Car A: \(150 \times 7.2 = 1\,080\) L, costing \(1080 \times 1.95 = \$2\,106\).

Car B: \(150 \times 11.5 = 1\,725\) L, costing \(1725 \times 1.95 = \$3\,363.75\).

Difference: $1,257.75 per year.

Application

Power and energy

Power and energy

\[1\,000 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ kW}\] \[\text{Energy (kWh)} = \text{Power (kW)} \times \text{Time (h)}\]

Power is the rate of using energy, measured in watts or kilowatts. Energy is power accumulated over time, measured in kilowatt-hours — the unit on your electricity bill.

Worked example 13

A 2,400 W heater runs for 5 hours. How much energy does it use, and what does it cost at 32 cents per kWh?

Convert to kilowatts: \(2\,400 \text{ W} = 2.4\) kW.

\[E = 2.4 \times 5 = 12 \text{ kWh}\]

Cost: \(12 \times 0.32 = \$3.84\).

Worked example 14 — over a quarter

A 150 W television runs for 6 hours a day. Find the cost over a 90-day quarter at 29c/kWh.

Power: \(150 \text{ W} = 0.15\) kW.

Energy per day: \(0.15 \times 6 = 0.9\) kWh.

Over 90 days: \(0.9 \times 90 = 81\) kWh.

Cost: \(81 \times 0.29 = \$23.49\).

Worked example 15 — is the upgrade worth it?

An old fridge uses 1.6 kWh per day; a new one uses 0.9 kWh per day and costs $1,150. At 31c/kWh, how long until the new fridge pays for itself?

Daily saving: \((1.6 - 0.9) \times 0.31 = 0.7 \times 0.31 = \$0.217\).

Annual saving: \(0.217 \times 365 = \$79.21\).

Payback:

\[\frac{1150}{79.21} = 14.5 \text{ years}\]

Longer than the fridge is likely to last — so on energy savings alone, the upgrade is not justified. This kind of judgement is exactly what the syllabus means by solving problems involving the consumption of energy.

Worked example 16 — exam style: petrol or electric?

Sienna drives 14,000 km a year. A petrol car uses 7.5 L/100 km with fuel at $1.95/L. An electric car uses 16 kWh/100 km with electricity at 30c/kWh, but costs $9,000 more to buy.

(a) Find the annual running cost of each. [2 marks]

Petrol: \(140 \times 7.5 = 1\,050\) L, costing \(1050 \times 1.95 = \$2\,047.50\).

Electric: \(140 \times 16 = 2\,240\) kWh, costing \(2240 \times 0.30 = \$672\).

(b) How many years until the electric car's extra price is recovered? [2 marks]

Annual saving: \(2047.50 - 672 = \$1\,375.50\). Payback: \(9000 \div 1375.50 = 6.5\) years.

(c) Give one factor that could shorten the payback period. [1 mark]

Higher petrol prices, driving more kilometres, or charging from home solar panels would all increase the annual saving.

Your turn

  1. An 800 W microwave runs for 15 minutes a day. Find its cost over a 92-day quarter at 33c/kWh.
    Show working

    \(0.8 \text{ kW} \times 0.25 \text{ h} = 0.2\) kWh per day; \(0.2 \times 92 = 18.4\) kWh.

    Cost: \(18.4 \times 0.33 = \$6.07\).

  2. Convert (a) 0.75 kW to watts and (b) 4,500 W to kilowatts.
    Show working

    (a) \(0.75 \times 1000 = 750\) W. (b) \(4500 \div 1000 = 4.5\) kW.
