Theory

Area of a triangle without the height

The familiar \(A = \tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\) needs a perpendicular height, which real triangles rarely give you. This formula needs only two sides and the angle between them.

Area formula

\[A = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C\]

\(a\) and \(b\) are two sides and \(C\) is the included angle — the angle between them. The angle must sit between the two sides you use, or the formula does not apply.

The included angle \(C\) sits between sides \(a\) and \(b\).

Worked example 1

A triangle has sides 12 cm and 17 cm with an angle of 43° between them. Find its area.

\[A = \frac{1}{2}(12)(17)\sin 43°\] \[= 102 \times 0.68200 = 69.56 \text{ cm}^{2}\]

Worked example 2 — obtuse included angle

A triangular garden bed has sides 8 m and 14 m with an included angle of 125°. Find its area.

\[A = \frac{1}{2}(8)(14)\sin 125° = 56 \times 0.81915 = 45.87 \text{ m}^{2}\]

Note that \(\sin 125°\) is positive (0.819), unlike \(\cos 125°\). Sine is positive for every angle in a triangle, so areas are always positive.

Worked example 3 — working backwards to an angle

A triangle with sides 9 m and 11 m has an area of 38 m². Find the included angle.

\[38 = \frac{1}{2}(9)(11)\sin C = 49.5 \sin C\] \[\sin C = \frac{38}{49.5} = 0.76768\] \[C = \sin^{-1}(0.76768) = 50.15°\]

There is also an obtuse solution, \(180° - 50.15° = 129.85°\), which gives exactly the same area. Without more information both triangles are valid — mention this if the question does not specify.

Worked example 4 — when you must find the angle first

Find the area of a triangle with sides 6, 9 and 13 m.

No angle is given, so use the cosine rule first. Take the angle between the 6 and 9 sides (opposite the 13):

\[\cos C = \frac{36 + 81 - 169}{2(6)(9)} = \frac{-52}{108} = -0.48148\] \[C = 118.78°\]

Now apply the area formula:

\[A = \frac{1}{2}(6)(9)\sin 118.78° = 27 \times 0.87639 = 23.66 \text{ m}^{2}\]

Application

Area of irregular blocks of land

Surveyors find the area of an irregular block by splitting it into triangles, then applying \(A = \tfrac12 ab\sin C\) to each. Two common layouts:

  • Diagonal split — a four-sided block is cut along a diagonal into two triangles.
  • Radial survey — from a point inside the block, measure the distance and bearing to each corner. Neighbouring corners and the centre form triangles; the angle at the centre is the difference between the two bearings.

Worked example 5 — a radial survey

From a point O inside a paddock, the corners are:

CornerABCD
Distance from O40 m55 m48 m36 m
Bearing from O020°110°205°290°

Angles at O: \(\angle AOB = 110° - 20° = 90°\), \(\angle BOC = 95°\), \(\angle COD = 85°\), \(\angle DOA = 360° - 290° + 20° = 90°\). (Check: they sum to 360°.)

\[\begin{aligned} \triangle AOB &= \tfrac12(40)(55)\sin 90° = 1100.00 \\ \triangle BOC &= \tfrac12(55)(48)\sin 95° = 1314.98 \\ \triangle COD &= \tfrac12(48)(36)\sin 85° = 860.71 \\ \triangle DOA &= \tfrac12(36)(40)\sin 90° = 720.00 \end{aligned}\]

Total area \(\approx 3996\) m², or about 0.40 ha.

Theory

True bearings

True bearings

Measured clockwise from north, always written with three digits and a ° symbol: \(000°\), \(045°\), \(135°\), \(270°\). North is \(000°\) (or \(360°\)).

True bearings run clockwise from north through a full 360°.

Back bearings

To reverse a direction, add or subtract 180°:
If the bearing is less than 180°, add 180°.
If it is 180° or more, subtract 180°.
So the bearing of A from B is the back bearing of B from A.

Worked example 6 — back bearings

The bearing of B from A is \(072°\). Find the bearing of A from B.

Since \(072° < 180°\), add:

\[072° + 180° = 252°\]

And if the bearing of Q from P were \(215°\), the reverse would be \(215° - 180° = 035°\).

Theory

Compass bearings

Compass bearings

Written as N or S, then an acute angle, then E or W — for example \(N35°E\), \(S62°W\). Read it as: start facing north (or south), then turn that many degrees toward east (or west).

The angle in a compass bearing is always between 0° and 90°, measured from the north–south line, never from east or west.

QuadrantCompass formTrue bearing
North-east\(N\theta E\)\(\theta\)
South-east\(S\theta E\)\(180° - \theta\)
South-west\(S\theta W\)\(180° + \theta\)
North-west\(N\theta W\)\(360° - \theta\)

Method

Converting between the two systems

Worked example 7 — compass to true

Convert \(S28°W\) to a true bearing.

South-west quadrant, so use \(180° + \theta\):

\[180° + 28° = 208°\]

Worked example 8 — true to compass

Convert \(312°\) to a compass bearing.

\(312°\) is between 270° and 360°, so it lies in the north-west quadrant. Measure back from north:

\[360° - 312° = 48°\]

So \(312° = N48°W\).

Worked example 9 — true to compass, south-east

Convert \(143°\) to a compass bearing.

\(143°\) lies between 90° and 180°, the south-east quadrant. Measure from south:

\[180° - 143° = 37°\]

So \(143° = S37°E\).

Application

Navigation problems

The method that works every time

  1. Draw a diagram, marking north at every turning point.
  2. Mark the bearings clockwise from each north line.
  3. Find the angle inside the triangle — this is the step worth the most marks, and where most errors happen.
  4. Choose the rule: cosine for SAS, sine for a matching pair.
  5. Convert back to a bearing if the question asks for a direction.

Worked example 10

A ship sails 45 km on a bearing of \(062°\), then 38 km on a bearing of \(145°\). How far is it from its starting point?

Finding the interior angle. At the turning point, the new north line is parallel to the original. The incoming leg arrives on a bearing of \(062°\), so its back bearing is \(062° + 180° = 242°\). The next leg leaves on \(145°\). The angle between them is

\[242° - 145° = 97°\]

Now the two legs and the included angle of 97° form an SAS triangle:

\[d^{2} = 45^{2} + 38^{2} - 2(45)(38)\cos 97°\] \[= 2025 + 1444 - 3420 \times (-0.12187)\] \[= 3469 + 416.79 = 3885.79\] \[d = \sqrt{3885.79} = 62.34 \text{ km}\]

The interior angle at the turning point is 97°, found from the back bearing 242° minus the outgoing bearing 145°.

Worked example 11 — finding the return bearing

For the ship above, find the bearing it must take to return directly to its start.

First find the angle at the starting point using the sine rule:

\[\sin\theta = \frac{38 \times \sin 97°}{62.34} = \frac{38 \times 0.99255}{62.34} = 0.60510\] \[\theta = 37.24°\]

This angle sits between the first leg (bearing \(062°\)) and the direct line, so the direct line from start to finish has bearing

\[062° + 37.24° = 99.24° \approx 099°\]

The return bearing is the back bearing:

\[099° + 180° = 279°\]

Worked example 12 — a full question

A walker goes 6 km from A on a bearing of \(035°\) to B, then 9 km on a bearing of \(128°\) to C. Find (a) the distance AC, (b) the bearing of C from A, and (c) the area of triangle ABC.

(a) Back bearing of AB is \(035° + 180° = 215°\). Interior angle at B:

\[215° - 128° = 87°\] \[AC^{2} = 36 + 81 - 2(6)(9)\cos 87° = 117 - 108 \times 0.05234 = 111.35\] \[AC = 10.55 \text{ km}\]

(b) Angle at A, by the sine rule:

\[\sin A = \frac{9 \times \sin 87°}{10.55} = 0.85180 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A = 58.35°\]

Bearing of C from A is \(035° + 58.35° = 93.35° \approx 093°\).

(c) Using the two legs and the included angle at B:

\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(6)(9)\sin 87° = 27 \times 0.99863 = 26.96 \text{ km}^{2}\]

Worked example 13 — exam style: bearings and elevation

From point P, a radio tower T bears \(040°\). From point Q, 500 m due east of P, the tower bears \(320°\). The angle of elevation of the top of the tower from P is 18°.

(a) Find the angles of triangle PQT. [2 marks]

At P: PQ points due east (\(090°\)), so \(\angle QPT = 90° - 40° = 50°\). At Q: QP points due west (\(270°\)), so \(\angle PQT = 320° - 270° = 50°\). Then \(\angle PTQ = 180° - 50° - 50° = 80°\).

(b) Find the distance PT. [2 marks]

\[PT = \frac{500 \times \sin 50°}{\sin 80°} = 388.93 \text{ m}\]

(c) Find the height of the tower. [1 mark]

\[h = 388.93 \times \tan 18° = 126.4 \text{ m}\]

This question uses three tools: bearings for the angles, the sine rule for the horizontal distance, and right-angled trigonometry for the height.

Class activity

Plan the second leg

About 10 minutes · pairs. Use the two-leg journey planner. Leg 1 is 30 km on a bearing of \(050°\); leg 2 is 40 km.

  1. Find a bearing for leg 2 that makes the interior angle 90°. What is the direct distance, and why is it such a neat number? Is there a second bearing that also works?
  2. Which bearing for leg 2 makes the direct distance as large as possible? As small as possible? What are those distances?
  3. Set leg 2 to \(100°\), then to \(000°\). Compare the interior angle, the direct distance and the bearing of the finish. Explain what you see.
  4. A rescue boat must return to base after the trip with leg 2 on \(140°\). On what bearing should it head?
Discussion points

1. \(140°\) gives 90°. The direct distance is 50 km: a 30–40–50 right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras applies. \(320°\) also gives 90°, with the finish on the other side of leg 1.

2. Largest: continue on \(050°\) (interior angle 180°), giving \(30 + 40 = 70\) km. Smallest: turn back on \(230°\) (interior angle 0°), giving \(40 - 30 = 10\) km.

3. Both give an interior angle of 130° and a direct distance of 63.58 km, but the finish bears \(079°\) in one case and \(021°\) in the other. The triangles are mirror images, so a distance alone does not fix a position — you also need the bearing.

4. The finish bears \(103°\) from base, so the return bearing is \(103° + 180° = 283°\).
