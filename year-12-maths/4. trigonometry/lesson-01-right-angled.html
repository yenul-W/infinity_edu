Theory

Labelling the sides

The names of the sides depend on which angle you are working from. Only the hypotenuse is fixed.

The three sides

  • Hypotenuse — always the longest side, always opposite the right angle. Never changes.
  • Opposite — the side facing the angle you are using.
  • Adjacent — the side next to the angle that is not the hypotenuse.

Working from angle θ

Same triangle, from angle α

The same side is "opposite" in one diagram and "adjacent" in the other. Always label relative to the angle in the question, and label the diagram before choosing a ratio.

Theory

The three trigonometric ratios

Choosing the ratio. Identify which two sides the question involves — the one you know and the one you want — then pick the ratio containing exactly those two.

Method

Finding an unknown side

Worked example 1 — unknown on top

A ladder 6.5 m long leans against a wall at 68° to the ground. How far up the wall does it reach?

The height is opposite the 68° angle; the ladder is the hypotenuse. Opposite and hypotenuse means sine:

\[\sin 68° = \frac{h}{6.5}\] \[h = 6.5 \times \sin 68° = 6.5 \times 0.92718 = 6.03 \text{ m}\]

When the unknown is on top of the fraction, multiply.

The ladder is the hypotenuse; \(h\) is opposite the 68° angle.

Worked example 2 — unknown on the bottom

A ramp rises at 9° and climbs a vertical height of 1.2 m. How long is the ramp?

The height is opposite; the ramp is the hypotenuse. Sine again — but this time the unknown is underneath:

\[\sin 9° = \frac{1.2}{L}\] \[L = \frac{1.2}{\sin 9°} = \frac{1.2}{0.15643} = 7.67 \text{ m}\]

When the unknown is on the bottom, divide by the ratio instead.

Worked example 3 — using tan

From a point 45 m from the base of a tower, the angle to the top is 32°. Find the tower's height.

Height is opposite, the 45 m is adjacent — no hypotenuse involved, so tangent:

\[\tan 32° = \frac{h}{45}\] \[h = 45 \times \tan 32° = 45 \times 0.62487 = 28.1 \text{ m}\]

Method

Finding an unknown angle

When two sides are known and the angle is wanted, use the inverse functions \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\) and \(\tan^{-1}\) (often labelled asin, acos, atan, or reached with SHIFT).

Inverse ratios

\[\theta = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\right) \qquad \theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\right) \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\right)\]

Worked example 4

A right-angled triangle has an opposite side of 7 cm and a hypotenuse of 11 cm. Find the angle, to the nearest degree.

\[\sin \theta = \frac{7}{11} = 0.63636\] \[\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.63636) = 39.52° \approx 40°\]

Worked example 5 — a gradient

A road rises 3 m over a horizontal distance of 40 m. Find the angle of inclination.

Rise is opposite, run is adjacent, so tangent:

\[\tan \theta = \frac{3}{40} = 0.075\] \[\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.075) = 4.29° \approx 4°17'\]

A check worth doing

In a right-angled triangle the other two angles must each be between 0° and 90°, and they must sum to 90°. If your inverse gives an impossible answer, or the ratio you fed it exceeded 1 for sine or cosine, you have mixed up the sides.

Theory

Degrees, minutes and seconds

The syllabus requires answers in both degrees, and degrees and minutes. A degree divides into 60 minutes, and a minute into 60 seconds — like time.

Conversions

\[1° = 60' \qquad 1' = 60''\]

Decimal → minutes: multiply the decimal part by 60.
Minutes → decimal: divide the minutes by 60.

Worked example 6 — converting to minutes

Write 37.45° in degrees and minutes.

The whole degrees are 37. Convert the decimal part:

\[0.45 \times 60 = 27'\]

So \(37.45° = 37°27'\).

Worked example 7 — converting to decimal

Write 52°18' as a decimal.

\[\frac{18}{60} = 0.3\]

So \(52°18' = 52.3°\).

Worked example 8 — rounding to the nearest minute

A calculation gives \(\theta = 63.7183°\). Express it to the nearest minute.

\[0.7183 \times 60 = 43.098' \approx 43'\]

So \(\theta \approx 63°43'\).

Most calculators have a °'" button that does this directly — learn where it is, it saves time and avoids slips.

Application

Angles of elevation and depression

Definitions

The angle of elevation is measured upward from the horizontal to a line of sight.
The angle of depression is measured downward from the horizontal to a line of sight.
Both are measured from the horizontal — never from the vertical.

The two horizontals are parallel, so the angle of elevation from the observer equals the angle of depression from the target — alternate angles.

The fact that unlocks most problems

The angle of elevation from A to B equals the angle of depression from B to A. Questions frequently give you one and expect you to use the other.

Worked example 9 — elevation

From 80 m away, the angle of elevation to the top of a building is 34°. The observer's eyes are 1.6 m above the ground. Find the building's height.

The triangle sits above eye level, so find that part first:

\[\tan 34° = \frac{h}{80} \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = 80 \times \tan 34° = 53.96 \text{ m}\]

Then add the eye height:

\[53.96 + 1.6 = 55.56 \approx 55.6 \text{ m}\]

Forgetting to add the observer's height is the single most common error in these questions.

Worked example 10 — depression

From a cliff 120 m high, the angle of depression to a boat is 23°. How far is the boat from the base of the cliff?

The angle of depression from the cliff top equals the angle of elevation from the boat — so inside the triangle, the angle at the boat is 23°.

\[\tan 23° = \frac{120}{d}\] \[d = \frac{120}{\tan 23°} = \frac{120}{0.42447} = 282.7 \text{ m}\]

Never put the depression angle at the top of the triangle. Transfer it to the bottom, where it becomes the angle of elevation.

Worked example 11 — exam style

A drone hovers 85 m above flat ground. The angle of depression from the drone to a parked car is 32°10'.

(a) Find the horizontal distance from the car to the point directly below the drone, to the nearest metre. [2 marks]

The angle of elevation from the car is also 32°10' \(= 32.1667°\).

\[d = \frac{85}{\tan 32°10'} = 135.15 \approx 135 \text{ m}\]

(b) The car drives 60 m directly towards that point. Find the new angle of depression, to the nearest minute. [2 marks]

New horizontal distance: \(135 - 60 = 75\) m.

\[\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{85}{75}\right) = 48.576° \approx 48°35'\]

The angle of depression increases as the car gets closer, as it should.

Application

Problems with two right-angled triangles

Many exam questions hide two right-angled triangles in one diagram. They are linked by a shared side. The method:

  1. Draw the diagram and mark every right angle.
  2. Find the shared side using the triangle where you know enough.
  3. Use that side in the second triangle.
  4. Keep full calculator values until the final answer — rounding the shared side early causes errors.

Worked example 12

From the top of a 25 m building, the angle of depression to the base of a tree is 30° and the angle of elevation to the top of the tree is 12°. Find the height of the tree.

Lower triangle (finds the shared horizontal distance \(d\)):

\[d = \frac{25}{\tan 30°} = 43.301 \text{ m}\]

Upper triangle (height of the tree above the roof line):

\[x = d \times \tan 12° = 43.301 \times 0.21256 = 9.204 \text{ m}\]

Tree height \(= 25 + 9.204 = 34.2\) m.

Your turn

  1. From the top of an 80 m cliff, the angles of depression to two boats, in line with the cliff, are 40° and 25°. How far apart are the boats?
    Show working

    Nearer boat: \(80 \div \tan 40° = 95.34\) m. Farther boat: \(80 \div \tan 25° = 171.56\) m.

    Distance apart: \(171.56 - 95.34 = 76.2\) m.

  2. A vertical post is held by two wires attached 12 m up. The wires are on opposite sides of the post and make angles of 50° and 65° with the ground. How far apart are the anchor points?
    Show working

    \(12 \div \tan 50° = 10.07\) m and \(12 \div \tan 65° = 5.60\) m.

    On opposite sides, so add: \(10.07 + 5.60 = 15.7\) m.

Class activity

Where should a surveyor stand?

About 10 minutes · pairs. A surveyor with eye height 1.6 m measures a building that is really 43.6 m tall. Her clinometer can be out by up to 1°. Use the elevation explorer with eye height 1.6 m.

  1. Set the distance to 15 m. Find the elevation angle (whole degrees) that gives a total height closest to 43.6 m.
  2. Change that angle by 1° down and 1° up. Record the two heights. How far apart are they?
  3. Repeat steps 1 and 2 at 50 m and at 150 m.
  4. From which distance is a 1° error least harmful? Roughly what angle of elevation does that correspond to?
  5. Give the surveyor one piece of advice, with a reason.
