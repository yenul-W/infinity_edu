The Sine & Cosine Rules

SOH CAH TOA needs a right angle. Most real triangles do not have one. Two rules extend trigonometry to every triangle — and knowing which to reach for is most of the skill.

Theory

Labelling any triangle

Both rules rely on one convention, and getting it wrong is the fastest way to lose marks.

The convention

Angles are named with capital letters \(A\), \(B\), \(C\). Each side takes the lower-case letter of the angle opposite it: side \(a\) is opposite angle \(A\), side \(b\) opposite \(B\), side \(c\) opposite \(C\).

Side \(a\) is opposite angle \(A\), and so on. The longest side is always opposite the largest angle.

Two facts to check your answers against

  • The three angles sum to \(180°\).
  • The largest angle is opposite the longest side, and the smallest angle opposite the shortest side. If your answer breaks this, it is wrong.

Theory

The sine rule — finding a side

Sine rule (side form)

\[\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}\]

Put the sides on top when you are looking for a side. Use only the two fractions you need — never all three at once.

When to use it

Use the sine rule when you have a matching pair — a side and the angle opposite it — plus one more piece of information.

Worked example 1

In triangle \(ABC\), \(A = 52°\), \(B = 71°\) and \(a = 14\) cm. Find \(b\).

We have the matching pair \(a\) and \(A\), and we want \(b\) with its opposite angle \(B\) known. Use those two fractions:

\[\frac{b}{\sin 71°} = \frac{14}{\sin 52°}\] \[b = \frac{14 \times \sin 71°}{\sin 52°} = \frac{14 \times 0.94552}{0.78801} = 16.80 \text{ cm}\]

Check: \(B > A\), so \(b\) should be longer than \(a\). It is. ✓

Worked example 2 — finding the third angle first

In triangle \(ABC\), \(A = 43°\), \(C = 28°\) and \(a = 9\) m. Find \(c\).

Here the pair \(a, A\) is known and \(C\) is given, so we can go straight to it:

\[c = \frac{9 \times \sin 28°}{\sin 43°} = \frac{9 \times 0.46947}{0.68200} = 6.20 \text{ m}\]

If instead you had been asked for \(b\), you would first need \(B = 180° - 43° - 28° = 109°\).

Theory

The sine rule — finding an angle

Sine rule (angle form)

\[\frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c}\]

Flip the rule so the sines are on top when hunting for an angle. It keeps the unknown in the numerator, which is easier to solve.

Worked example 3

In triangle \(ABC\), \(a = 12\) m, \(b = 9\) m and \(A = 67°\). Find angle \(B\).

\[\frac{\sin B}{9} = \frac{\sin 67°}{12}\] \[\sin B = \frac{9 \times \sin 67°}{12} = \frac{9 \times 0.92050}{12} = 0.69038\] \[B = \sin^{-1}(0.69038) = 43.66° \approx 43°40'\]

Check: \(b < a\), so \(B\) should be smaller than \(A\). It is. ✓

The syllabus restricts this to cases excluding the ambiguous case — you will not be asked to decide between two possible triangles. But you do need to handle one specific situation: when the question tells you the angle is obtuse.

Theory

When you are told the angle is obtuse

Your calculator's \(\sin^{-1}\) always returns an acute angle. But two different angles share the same sine.

The supplementary relationship

\[\sin \theta = \sin (180° - \theta)\]

For example \(\sin 40° = \sin 140° = 0.6428\). If the question states the angle is obtuse, take \(180°\) minus the calculator's answer.

Two angles, one sine

Slide the acute angle and watch its obtuse partner. Both have exactly the same sine value.

Worked example 4

In triangle \(ABC\), \(a = 8\) cm, \(b = 11\) cm and \(A = 34°\). Angle \(B\) is known to be obtuse. Find \(B\).

\[\frac{\sin B}{11} = \frac{\sin 34°}{8}\] \[\sin B = \frac{11 \times 0.55919}{8} = 0.76889\]

The calculator gives \(\sin^{-1}(0.76889) = 50.25°\), which is acute. Since we are told \(B\) is obtuse:

\[B = 180° - 50.25° = 129.75° \approx 129°45'\]

Check: \(b > a\), so \(B\) must exceed \(A = 34°\) — and \(129.75°\) does. The remaining angle \(C = 180 - 34 - 129.75 = 16.25°\), which is positive, so the triangle is valid. ✓

Theory

The cosine rule — finding a side

The sine rule needs a matching side–angle pair. When you do not have one, use the cosine rule.

Cosine rule (side form)

\[c^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2} - 2ab\cos C\]

\(C\) is the angle between sides \(a\) and \(b\) — the included angle — and \(c\) is the side opposite it.

When to use it

Use the cosine rule when you know two sides and the angle between them (SAS), and want the third side.

Notice the resemblance to Pythagoras. If \(C = 90°\) then \(\cos 90° = 0\) and the rule collapses to \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\). The cosine rule is Pythagoras generalised to any angle.

Worked example 5

A triangle has \(a = 7\) m, \(b = 10\) m and the included angle \(C = 58°\). Find \(c\).

\[c^{2} = 7^{2} + 10^{2} - 2(7)(10)\cos 58°\] \[= 49 + 100 - 140 \times 0.52992\] \[= 149 - 74.19 = 74.81\] \[c = \sqrt{74.81} = 8.65 \text{ m}\]

Do not forget the square root. Stopping at 74.81 is the most common error in cosine rule questions.

Worked example 6 — an obtuse included angle

A triangle has \(a = 12\) cm, \(b = 15\) cm and \(C = 112°\). Find \(c\).

Here \(\cos 112° = -0.37461\), which is negative:

\[c^{2} = 144 + 225 - 2(12)(15)(-0.37461)\] \[= 369 + 134.86 = 503.86\] \[c = \sqrt{503.86} = 22.45 \text{ cm}\]

Subtracting a negative adds. This is correct: an obtuse included angle opens the triangle out, making the opposite side longer than it would be at 90° (\(\sqrt{369} = 19.2\) cm).

Theory

The cosine rule — finding an angle

Cosine rule (angle form)

\[\cos C = \frac{a^{2} + b^{2} - c^{2}}{2ab}\]

Use when you know all three sides (SSS). The angle you find is the one opposite side \(c\) — the side that appears with the minus sign.

Worked example 7

A triangle has sides 8 m, 11 m and 15 m. Find the largest angle.

The largest angle is opposite the longest side, so put \(c = 15\), \(a = 8\), \(b = 11\):

\[\cos C = \frac{8^{2} + 11^{2} - 15^{2}}{2(8)(11)} = \frac{64 + 121 - 225}{176}\] \[= \frac{-40}{176} = -0.22727\] \[C = \cos^{-1}(-0.22727) = 103.14° \approx 103°08'\]

A negative cosine means an obtuse angle — and unlike sine, the calculator handles this automatically, returning an angle above 90°. No adjustment is needed.

Worked example 8

Find the smallest angle in a triangle with sides 6, 9 and 12 cm.

The smallest angle faces the shortest side, so set \(c = 6\), \(a = 9\), \(b = 12\):

\[\cos C = \frac{81 + 144 - 36}{2(9)(12)} = \frac{189}{216} = 0.875\] \[C = \cos^{-1}(0.875) = 28.96° \approx 28°58'\]

Method

Choosing the right rule

Mark the triangle with what you know, then read off the case:

You knowCaseUse
A right angle—SOH CAH TOA — always simplest
Two angles and any sideAASSine rule
Two sides and an angle opposite one of themSSASine rule (angle form)
Two sides and the angle between themSASCosine rule (side form)
All three sidesSSSCosine rule (angle form)

The one-question test

Do you have a matching pair — a side and the angle directly opposite it? If yes, sine rule. If no, cosine rule. That single question resolves almost every problem.

Which rule?

Type sine, cosine or soh cah toa.

Worked example 9 — exam style

A block of land \(ABCD\) is divided by the diagonal \(AC = 48\) m. In triangle \(ABC\), \(AB = 35\) m and \(\angle BAC = 52°\). In triangle \(ACD\), \(\angle CAD = 38°\) and \(\angle ACD = 70°\).

(a) Find \(BC\). [2 marks]

Triangle \(ABC\): two sides and the included angle (SAS), so the cosine rule.

\[BC^2 = 35^2 + 48^2 - 2(35)(48)\cos 52° = 1460.38 \;\Rightarrow\; BC = 38.21 \text{ m}\]

(b) Find \(CD\). [2 marks]

Triangle \(ACD\): two angles and a side. The angle opposite \(AC\) is \(\angle D = 180° - 38° - 70° = 72°\), giving a matching pair, so the sine rule.

\[CD = \frac{48 \times \sin 38°}{\sin 72°} = 31.07 \text{ m}\]

Exam tip: state which rule you are using and why — "SAS, so cosine rule" — before calculating.

Method

Solving a triangle completely

To solve a triangle means to find every side and every angle. It usually takes both rules in turn:

  1. Use the cosine rule first if you have SAS or SSS.
  2. Then use the sine rule for a second angle — but choose the smallest angle (opposite the shortest side). It must be acute, so the calculator's answer is always correct.
  3. Find the last angle from the angle sum, \(180°\).

Why the smallest angle?

A triangle can have at most one obtuse angle, and it is always the largest. If you use the sine rule on the largest angle, the calculator may give an acute answer when the true angle is obtuse. The smallest angle is never obtuse.

Worked example 10

Solve triangle \(ABC\) with \(a = 9\), \(b = 14\) and \(C = 40°\).

Side \(c\) (SAS, cosine rule):

\[c^2 = 81 + 196 - 2(9)(14)\cos 40° = 83.96 \;\Rightarrow\; c = 9.16\]

Smallest angle. The shortest side is \(a = 9\), so find \(A\):

\[\sin A = \frac{9 \times \sin 40°}{9.16} = 0.6314 \;\Rightarrow\; A = 39.15° \approx 39°09'\]

Last angle: \(B = 180° - 40° - 39.15° = 100.85° \approx 100°51'\).

Notice \(B\) is obtuse. Using the sine rule for \(B\) would have given 79.15° — wrong. Choosing the smallest angle avoided the trap.
