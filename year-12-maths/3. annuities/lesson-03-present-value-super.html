Theory

What is present value?

The present value of an annuity is the single amount you would need to invest today to fund all of its future payments.

The idea

Money available now is worth more than the same amount later, because it can be invested in the meantime. So a promise of $10,000 per year for 20 years is not worth $200,000 today — it is worth considerably less.

    Show working

    \(500 \div 1.1 = \$454.55\) and \(500 \div 1.1^2 = 500 \div 1.21 = \$413.22\).

  2. A friend says $5,000 a year for 10 years is "worth $50,000". In what sense is this wrong?
    Show working

    $50,000 is the total paid out, not its value today. Each payment arrives later, and money received later is worth less than money now because it misses out on interest. At any positive interest rate, the present value is less than $50,000.

Method

Present value interest factor tables

As with future value, a table saves you discounting each payment separately.

Using a present value table

\[PV = (\text{payment per period}) \times (\text{present value factor})\]

Each factor is the present value of $1 received at the end of each period for \(n\) periods. Read at the row for \(n\) and the column for the rate per period.

Present value of an annuity of $1 per period for \(n\) periods.

Sense check — the mirror of last lesson

A present value factor is always less than \(n\), because each payment is discounted. (A future value factor is always more than \(n\).) If your PV factor exceeds \(n\), you have read the wrong table.

Worked example 2

A retirement fund must pay $25,000 at the end of each year for 20 years. The fund earns 6% per annum. How much is needed today?

At \(n = 20\), \(r = 6\%\), the present value factor is 11.4699.

\[PV = 25000 \times 11.4699 = \$286\,747.50\]

Note the total paid out is \(20 \times 25000 = \$500\,000\), but only about $286,748 is needed today — interest earned along the way supplies the rest.

Application

Loan repayments as an annuity

A reducing-balance loan is a present-value annuity seen from the lender's side. The bank hands over a lump sum today; you make regular equal repayments that exactly exhaust it.

The loan relationship

\[\text{Loan amount} = \text{Repayment} \times (\text{present value factor})\] \[\text{Repayment} = \frac{\text{Loan amount}}{\text{present value factor}}\]

Exactly the same table as a retirement drawdown — only the story differs.

Worked example 3

Kiran borrows $35,000 over 5 years at 9% per annum, repaid annually. The present value factor at \(n = 5\), \(r = 9\%\) is 3.8897. Find the annual repayment.

\[\text{Repayment} = \frac{35000}{3.8897} = \$8\,998.12\]

Total repaid: \(5 \times 8998.12 = \$44\,990.60\), so the interest cost is $9,990.60.

Application

Superannuation

Superannuation is the most important annuity most people will ever have, and it runs in two phases.

The two phases

Accumulation (working life) — a future-value annuity. Contributions go in every pay period and compound for decades.

Drawdown (retirement) — a present-value annuity. The accumulated balance funds regular withdrawals until it is exhausted.

Worked example 5 — both phases

Ravi contributes $7,000 per year for 35 years at 7% per annum. He then retires and draws an income for 25 years from the accumulated balance, still at 7%.

Phase 1 — accumulation. The future value factor at \(n = 35\), \(r = 7\%\) is 138.2369.

\[FV = 7000 \times 138.2369 = \$967\,658.30\]

He contributed \(35 \times 7000 = \$245\,000\); interest supplied the other $722,658.

Phase 2 — drawdown. The present value factor at \(n = 25\), \(r = 7\%\) is 11.6536.

\[\text{Annual income} = \frac{967658.30}{11.6536} = \$83\,034.06\]

So a $7,000 annual contribution supports an income of about $83,000 per year for 25 years — roughly twelve times what he put in each year. That leverage is entirely the work of compound interest over 35 years.

Application

Savings accounts: save now, spend later

The syllabus asks you to solve annuity problems involving savings accounts. A common structure is two annuities joined together: save regularly until a date, then draw the savings down in regular amounts. Work backwards from the spending:

  1. Use a present value factor to find the balance needed when the spending starts.
  2. Use a future value factor to find the regular saving that builds that balance.

Worked example 6

Priya's parents want her to withdraw $10,000 at the end of each year for the 4 years of university. Uni starts in 6 years. Their savings account pays 5% per annum throughout.

Step 1 — balance needed when uni starts. PV factor at \(n = 4\), \(r = 5\%\): 3.5460.

\[10000 \times 3.5460 = \$35\,460\]

Step 2 — annual saving for 6 years. FV factor at \(n = 6\), \(r = 5\%\): 6.8019.

\[35460 \div 6.8019 = \$5\,213.25 \text{ per year}\]

They save \(6 \times 5213.25 = \$31\,279.50\) and Priya receives $40,000 — interest supplies the difference over the 10 years.

Evaluation

Making the decision: lump sum or instalments?

The syllabus asks you to solve annuity problems involving financial decisions. The recurring question: take a lump sum now, or a stream of payments?

The method

Convert both options to the same point in time — usually today — then compare. Find the present value of the payment stream and compare it with the lump sum on offer.

Worked example 7

A competition winner may take $120,000 today, or $12,000 per year for 15 years. Money can be invested at 7% per annum. Which is better?

The present value factor at \(n = 15\), \(r = 7\%\) is 9.1079.

\[PV_{\text{instalments}} = 12000 \times 9.1079 = \$109\,294.80\]

The instalments total $180,000, but they are worth only $109,295 in today's money — less than the $120,000 lump sum.

Conclusion: take the lump sum. Invested at 7% it would fund payments of \(\dfrac{120000}{9.1079} = \$13\,175\) per year, more than the $12,000 offered.

Worked example 8 — when the rate flips the answer

Same choice, but money can only be invested at 3% per annum. The factor at \(n = 15\), \(r = 3\%\) is 11.9379.

\[PV_{\text{instalments}} = 12000 \times 11.9379 = \$143\,254.80\]

Now the instalments are worth more than the $120,000 lump sum, so the instalments win.

Why the reversal: at a low interest rate a lump sum grows slowly, so the advantage of having money early is small. The decision always depends on the available interest rate — state that in your answer.

Worked example 9 — exam style

Lena is offered a redundancy payment of $85,000 now, or $9,500 at the end of each year for 12 years.

(a) If she can invest at 5% per annum, which option is better? [2 marks]

PV factor at \(n = 12\), \(r = 5\%\): 8.8633. \(\;9500 \times 8.8633 = \$84\,201.35\), less than $85,000, so take the lump sum.

(b) Repeat at 3% (factor 9.9540). [1 mark]

\(9500 \times 9.9540 = \$94\,563\), more than $85,000, so take the instalments.

(c) Between which two whole-number rates do the options become equal? [2 marks]

They are equal when the factor is \(85000 \div 9500 = 8.9474\). Along \(n = 12\): 4% gives 9.3851 and 5% gives 8.8633, so the break-even rate is between 4% and 5%. Below it the instalments win; above it the lump sum wins.

Your turn

  1. Choose between $60,000 now and $8,000 a year for 10 years, if money earns 6% per annum (factor 7.3601).
    Show working

    \(8000 \times 7.3601 = \$58\,880.80\), less than $60,000: take the lump sum.

  2. Repeat at 4% per annum (factor 8.1109). Explain the change.
    Show working

    \(8000 \times 8.1109 = \$64\,887.20\), more than $60,000: take the instalments. At the lower rate the lump sum grows more slowly, so receiving money early is worth less.

Factors beyond the mathematics

A full-mark answer also weighs the non-numerical: instalments provide budgeting discipline and protection from overspending; a lump sum offers flexibility and the chance to clear high-interest debt. Mention both, but lead with the present-value comparison.
