Theory

What is an interest factor?

In Lesson 1 we found that $1,000 contributed annually for 3 years at 10% grows to $3,310. Notice what happens if we contribute $1 instead:

YearOpeningInterest (10%)ContributionClosing
10.00000.00001.00001.0000
21.00000.10001.00002.1000
32.10000.21001.00003.3100

The answer is 3.3100. And \(1000 \times 3.3100 = \$3\,310\) — exactly the earlier result.

The interest factor

A future value interest factor is the final value of an annuity of $1 per period. Because annuities scale proportionally, you multiply the factor by your actual contribution to get the answer.

Using a future value table

\[FV = (\text{contribution per period}) \times (\text{interest factor})\]

Look up the factor at the row for the number of periods \(n\) and the column for the interest rate \(r\) per period.

For reference, the factor is generated by \(\dfrac{(1+r)^{n} - 1}{r}\), but in this course you read it from the table rather than calculating it. The table is provided in the exam.

Your turn

  1. Build the $1-per-year table for 3 years at 5% to find the interest factor. Use it to find the future value of $600 per year for 3 years at 5%.
    Show working

    Closing balances: 1.0000, then \(1 + 0.05 + 1 = 2.0500\), then \(2.05 + 0.1025 + 1 = 3.1525\).

    \(FV = 600 \times 3.1525 = \$1\,891.50\).

  2. Why is the interest factor for \(n = 1\) equal to 1.0000 at every interest rate?
    Show working

    The single $1 contribution arrives at the end of the only period, so it earns no interest. The balance is just the $1 contributed.

Reading a future value table

A future value interest factor table for an annuity of $1 per period:

Future value of an annuity of $1 per period, at the end of \(n\) periods.

Three checks before you use a factor

  1. Is \(n\) the number of periods, not necessarily years?
  2. Is the rate the rate per period, not per annum?
  3. Does the factor look sensible? It should always be at least \(n\) — you contributed $1 \(n\) times, and interest can only add to that.

Application

Finding the future value

Worked example 1

Dev contributes $4,000 at the end of each year for 8 years into an account earning 5% per annum. Use the table to find the future value.

From the table at \(n = 8\), \(r = 5\%\), the factor is 9.5491.

\[FV = 4000 \times 9.5491 = \$38\,196.40\]

Sense check: he contributed \(8 \times 4000 = \$32\,000\), so the interest is $6,196.40. The factor 9.5491 is comfortably above 8, as it must be.

Worked example 2 — with an existing balance

Ellie has $20,000 already invested and adds $3,000 at the end of each year for 10 years, all at 6% per annum. Find the total.

Treat the two parts separately and add them.

The lump sum uses ordinary compound interest:

\[20000 \times 1.06^{10} = 20000 \times 1.79085 = \$35\,817.00\]

The annuity uses the factor at \(n = 10\), \(r = 6\%\), which is 13.1808:

\[3000 \times 13.1808 = \$39\,542.40\]

Total: \(35817.00 + 39542.40 = \$75\,359.40\)

This split is a very common exam structure — never try to force both parts through one formula.

Application

Working backwards to the contribution

The syllabus asks you to determine the contribution amount required to achieve a given future value. Same formula, rearranged:

Finding the required contribution

\[\text{Contribution} = \frac{\text{target future value}}{\text{interest factor}}\]

Worked example 3

Josh wants $50,000 in 10 years. His account pays 6% per annum. How much must he contribute at the end of each year?

At \(n = 10\), \(r = 6\%\), the factor is 13.1808.

\[\text{Contribution} = \frac{50000}{13.1808} = \$3\,793.53\]

Check: \(3793.53 \times 13.1808 = \$50\,000\) ✓. He contributes \(10 \times 3793.53 = \$37\,935.30\) in total, so interest provides the remaining $12,064.70.

Worked example 4 — the equivalent single sum

The syllabus also asks for the single sum contribution required to produce an equivalent future value. What one-off amount invested today at 6% would also give Josh $50,000 in 10 years?

This is a present value question, using ordinary compound interest:

\[PV = \frac{50000}{1.06^{10}} = \frac{50000}{1.79085} = \$27\,920.99\]

The comparison that earns the marks: a single $27,921 today achieves the same goal as $37,935 paid in instalments. The lump sum is smaller because all of it compounds for the full 10 years, whereas the instalments each compound for less.

Worked example 5 — exam style

Liam wants $30,000 for a house deposit in 5 years. His account pays 5% per annum, compounded annually.

(a) Find the annual contribution he needs. [1 mark]

Factor at \(n = 5\), \(r = 5\%\): 5.5256. \(\;30000 \div 5.5256 = \$5\,429.27\).

(b) Liam can only save $5,000 a year. By how much will he fall short? [1 mark]

\(5000 \times 5.5256 = \$27\,628\), a shortfall of \(30000 - 27628 = \$2\,372\).

(c) His grandmother offers a single gift today to cover the shortfall. How much should she give? [2 marks]

The gift must grow to $2,372 in 5 years at 5%:

\[PV = \frac{2372}{1.05^{5}} = \$1\,858.52\]

A gift of about $1,859 today, plus $5,000 a year, reaches the $30,000 target.

Your turn

  1. Ava wants $45,000 in 15 years at 7% per annum. Find the annual contribution she needs.
    Show working

    Factor at \(n = 15\), \(r = 7\%\): 25.1290. \(45000 \div 25.1290 = \$1\,790.76\) per year.

  2. What single sum invested today at 7% per annum would also give Ava $45,000 in 15 years? Compare it with her total contributions.
    Show working

    \(PV = 45000 \div 1.07^{15} = \$16\,310.07\).

    Her instalments total \(15 \times 1790.76 = \$26\,861.40\), about $10,550 more than the single sum.

Method

Using the table in reverse: how long, and what rate?

Sometimes the question gives the contribution and the target and asks how long it will take, or what rate is needed. Work out the factor you need, then search the table for it.

The factor you need

\[\text{required factor} = \frac{\text{target}}{\text{contribution}}\]

How long? Go down the column for the rate until the factor first reaches the required value.
What rate? Go along the row for \(n\) until the factor first reaches the required value.

Worked example 6

Kai saves $2,000 at the end of each year towards a $25,000 goal.

(a) At 6% per annum, how many years will it take?

Required factor: \(25000 \div 2000 = 12.5\). Down the 6% column: \(n = 9\) gives 11.4913 (not enough), \(n = 10\) gives 13.1808. Kai reaches his goal after 10 years.

(b) What rate would get him there in exactly 10 years?

Along the \(n = 10\) row: 4% gives 12.0061 (not enough), 5% gives 12.5779. He needs at least 5% per annum.

Method

When the period is not a year

Interest factor tables are indexed by periods and rate per period — never by years and annual rates. Convert both, exactly as with compound interest.

Worked example 7

Amara contributes $1,500 at the end of every six months for 6 years, at 8% per annum compounded six-monthly. Find the future value.

Six-monthly means 2 periods per year:

\[r = \frac{8\%}{2} = 4\% \text{ per period} \qquad n = 6 \times 2 = 12 \text{ periods}\]

At \(n = 12\), \(r = 4\%\), the factor is 15.0258.

\[FV = 1500 \times 15.0258 = \$22\,538.70\]

The trap: looking up \(n = 6\) at \(r = 8\%\) gives 7.3359 and an answer of $11,003.85 — less than half the correct value. Convert both, always.

Your turn

  1. $600 is contributed at the end of every quarter for 3 years at 8% per annum, compounded quarterly. Find the future value.
    Show working

    \(r = 8\% \div 4 = 2\%\), \(n = 3 \times 4 = 12\). Factor: 13.4121.

    \(FV = 600 \times 13.4121 = \$8\,047.26\).

  2. $250 is saved at the end of every month for one year at 12% per annum, compounded monthly. Find the future value.
    Show working

    \(r = 12\% \div 12 = 1\%\), \(n = 12\). Factor: 12.6825.

    \(FV = 250 \times 12.6825 = \$3\,170.63\).

Evaluation

Varying the parameters

The syllabus asks you to examine the effect of varying the amount initially invested, the value of the periodic payment, the interest rate and the duration. Each behaves differently.

ChangeEffect on future valueWhy
Double the contribution Exactly doubles \(FV\) is directly proportional to the contribution — the factor is unchanged
Double the initial lump sum Doubles only that part The annuity component is untouched
Increase the rate Increases more than proportionally The factor grows faster than linearly — interest compounds on interest
Extend the duration The largest effect of all Adds contributions and gives every earlier contribution more time

Worked example 8 — which change helps most?

Base case: $2,000 per year for 20 years at 6% (factor 36.7856), giving \(FV = \$73\,571.20\). Now change one thing at a time.

ChangeNew factorNew \(FV\)Gain
Base case36.7856$73 571—
Contribution → $2 50036.7856$91 964+$18 393
Rate → 8%45.7620$91 524+$17 953
Term → 25 years54.8645$109 729+$36 158

Five extra years adds almost twice as much as a 25% larger contribution. Time is the most powerful variable in an annuity — the argument for starting superannuation early.

Saving for a goal: instalments or a lump sum?

About 10 minutes · groups of three. Each person takes one goal and uses the target planner.

  2. Work out how much interest does for you in each plan: target minus total paid in.
