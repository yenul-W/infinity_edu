What Is an Annuity?

Compound interest assumes you invest once and wait. An annuity is what happens when you keep adding money — the same amount, every period. It is how superannuation, savings plans and loan repayments actually work.

Theory

Defining an annuity

An annuity is a financial arrangement involving regular, equal payments made at regular intervals, with compound interest applied each period.

The two forms an annuity takes

1. Paying in (accumulating). An investment account with regular, equal contributions, where interest compounds at the end of each period. The balance grows. Examples: superannuation, a regular savings plan.

2. Drawing out (drawdown). A single sum invested, from which regular equal withdrawals are made. The balance falls, but the remainder keeps earning interest. Examples: a retirement pension, a structured payout.

Both are the same mathematics. Each period you do two things: add the interest, then add or subtract the payment.

You have already met the drawdown form under another name. A reducing-balance loan is an annuity: the bank invests a lump sum (the loan) and you make regular equal repayments until the balance reaches zero.

Compound interestAnnuity
PaymentsOne, at the startRegular and equal, every period
Formula\(FV = PV(1+r)^{n}\)Built period by period, or from interest-factor tables
Each contribution—Earns interest for a different length of time
Typical useA term depositSuperannuation, savings plans, loans

The key insight

In an annuity, each contribution earns interest for a different length of time. The first payment sits in the account longest and earns the most; the final payment earns nothing at all. That is why you cannot simply apply \(FV = PV(1+r)^n\) to the total contributed.

  1. Which are annuities, and of which form? (a) $50 paid into a savings account at the end of every week. (b) $10,000 placed in a term deposit for 3 years. (c) A prize fund that pays the winner $1,000 a month for 20 years. (d) A car loan repaid in equal monthly instalments.
    Show working

    (a) Annuity, paying in. (b) Not an annuity — one single deposit, so plain compound interest. (c) Annuity, drawing out. (d) Annuity, drawing out — a reducing-balance loan.

  2. Jack says $1,000 contributed at the end of each year for 5 years at 6% is worth \(5000 \times 1.06^5\). Explain his mistake.
    Show working

    His formula treats all $5,000 as if it were invested at the start and earned interest for 5 years. In fact the contributions earn interest for 4, 3, 2, 1 and 0 years, so his answer is too large.

Method

Modelling an annuity in tabular form

The syllabus asks you to model an annuity in tabular form for up to four time periods. This is an exam staple. Use five columns:

The period-by-period cycle

\[\text{Interest} = \text{Opening balance} \times r\] \[\text{Closing balance} = \text{Opening balance} + \text{Interest} + \text{Contribution}\]

\(r\) is the interest rate per period. Each closing balance becomes the next opening balance.

Worked example 1

Leila contributes $2,000 at the end of each year into an account earning 6% per annum, compounded annually. Model the first four years.

YearOpening balanceInterest (6%) ContributionClosing balance

Reading the table: Leila contributed \(4 \times 2000 = \$8\,000\) and the account is worth $8,749.23, so the interest earned is $749.23.

Notice year 1: the opening balance is $0 and the interest is $0. The contribution arrives at the end of the year, so there is nothing in the account to earn interest during that first year.

Theory

Why the timing of payments matters

In this course, contributions are made at the end of each period, and interest compounds at the end of each period too. Tracing where each contribution ends up makes the structure clear.

Worked example 3 — following each contribution

$1,000 is contributed at the end of each year for 4 years at 10% per annum. How much is each individual contribution worth at the end of year 4?

Contribution made at end ofYears it earns interestValue at end of year 4
Year 13\(1000 \times 1.1^{3} = 1331.00\)
Year 22\(1000 \times 1.1^{2} = 1210.00\)
Year 31\(1000 \times 1.1^{1} = 1100.00\)
Year 40\(1000 \times 1.1^{0} = 1000.00\)
Total$4,641.00

The last contribution earns nothing — it arrives at the very moment the annuity ends. Building the table period by period gives the same $4,641.00.

The pattern to remember

In an \(n\)-period annuity with contributions at the end of each period, the contributions earn interest for \(n-1,\ n-2,\ \dots,\ 1,\ 0\) periods respectively. The number of contributions is \(n\), but the longest any of them compounds is \(n-1\) periods.

Check your understanding

Answers to the nearest cent.

Your turn

  1. $800 is contributed at the end of each year for 3 years at 5% per annum. Find the value of each contribution at the end, and the final balance.
    Show working

    Year 1's contribution earns 2 years: \(800 \times 1.05^2 = \$882.00\). Year 2's earns 1 year: \(\$840.00\). Year 3's earns nothing: \(\$800.00\).

    Final balance: \(882 + 840 + 800 = \$2\,522.00\).

  2. In a 10-year annuity with end-of-year contributions, for how many years does the fourth contribution earn interest?
    Show working

    It is made at the end of year 4 and earns interest in years 5 to 10: \(10 - 4 = 6\) years.

Theory

Monthly and quarterly annuities

Real annuities rarely run yearly: savings plans, super contributions and loan repayments are usually monthly or fortnightly. The table method is unchanged; only the rate changes, exactly as with compound interest.

Matching the rate to the payment period

\[r = \frac{\text{annual rate}}{\text{periods per year}} \qquad n = \text{years} \times \text{periods per year}\]

The contribution period, the compounding period and the rate must all match. In this course they always do.

Worked example 4

Ella saves $300 at the end of each month in an account paying 4.8% per annum, compounded monthly. Model the first four months.

\(r = 0.048 \div 12 = 0.004\), i.e. 0.4% per month.

MonthOpeningInterest (0.4%)ContributionClosing
10.000.00300.00300.00
2300.001.20300.00601.20
3601.202.40300.00903.60
4903.603.61300.001 207.21

The interest is tiny at first: over short periods, almost all the growth comes from contributions. Interest becomes significant only over years, as the balance builds.

Application

The other direction: drawing an income

The second form of annuity starts with a single sum and takes regular equal withdrawals from it. The only change to the table is a minus sign.

Drawdown cycle

\[\text{Closing balance} = \text{Opening balance} + \text{Interest} - \text{Withdrawal}\]

Identical to a reducing-balance loan. If the withdrawal is less than the interest earned, the balance grows and the income lasts forever.

Worked example 5

Nadia retires with $400,000 invested at 5% per annum and withdraws $30,000 at the end of each year. Model four years.

YearOpeningInterest (5%)WithdrawalClosing
1400 000.0020 000.0030 000.00390 000.00
2390 000.0019 500.0030 000.00379 500.00
3379 500.0018 975.0030 000.00368 475.00
4368 475.0018 423.7530 000.00356 898.75

The balance falls slowly at first: of the $30,000 taken in year 1, $20,000 was interest and only $10,000 came from capital. As the balance shrinks the interest shrinks too, so the fund depletes at an accelerating rate.

Worked example 6 — a perpetual income

If Nadia instead withdrew $20,000 per year, what would happen?

Year 1 interest is \(400000 \times 0.05 = \$20\,000\), exactly matching the withdrawal. The closing balance is

\[400000 + 20000 - 20000 = \$400\,000\]

The balance never changes — she could draw $20,000 every year forever. Drawing less than the interest would make the fund grow despite the withdrawals.

Worked example 7 — exam style

Tom invests a $150,000 inheritance at 6% per annum and withdraws $20,000 at the end of each year.

(a) Model the first three years. [2 marks]

YearOpeningInterest (6%)WithdrawalClosing
1150 000.009 000.0020 000.00139 000.00
2139 000.008 340.0020 000.00127 340.00
3127 340.007 640.4020 000.00114 980.40

(b) Of the $60,000 withdrawn, how much came from interest? [1 mark]

The capital fell by \(150000 - 114980.40 = \$35\,019.60\), so \(60000 - 35019.60 = \$24\,980.40\) came from interest.

(c) Tom says the money will run out after \(150000 \div 20000 = 7.5\) years. Explain why he is wrong. [1 mark]

His calculation ignores interest. The remaining balance keeps earning 6%, so the fund lasts longer — it is exhausted during year 11.

Technology

Modelling an annuity in a spreadsheet

The syllabus explicitly requires you to use a spreadsheet to model an annuity. Put the parameters in their own cells so one edit updates everything:

CellLabelValue
B1Opening balance0
B2Contribution per period2000
B3Rate per period0.06

Then the table from row 6, with period numbers in column A:

A — PeriodB — Opening C — InterestD — ContributionE — Closing
Row 61=$B$1 =B6*$B$3=$B$2=B6+C6+D6
Row 7=A6+1=E6 fill downfill downfill down

As with loans, the formula that makes it work is B7 =E6 — each period opens with the previous period's closing balance.

  • For a drawdown annuity, make the contribution negative or change E to =B6+C6-D6.
  • Total contributed is =SUM(D6:D###); total interest is =SUM(C6:C###). They should add to the final balance minus the opening balance.
  • Charting column E against column A shows the balance curving upward — it accelerates, because each year's interest is calculated on a larger balance.
  • Changing B3 and watching the final row move is the "examine the effect of varying the interest rate" skill the syllabus asks for.

A useful check

Final balance = opening balance + total contributed + total interest. If your three sums do not reconcile, a row has been mis-referenced — usually an opening balance pointing at the wrong cell.

Class activity

How long will a retirement fund last?

About 10 minutes · pairs. Kim retires with $500,000. Use the drawdown explorer.

  1. Set the rate to 5%. Record how long the fund lasts for withdrawals of $25,000, $30,000, $40,000 and $50,000 per year.
  2. The $40,000 withdrawal is 60% bigger than $25,000. Compare the effect on how long the fund lasts.
  3. Keep $40,000 per year. How long does the fund last at 3%? At 7%?
  4. Kim expects to live 25 more years. Find the largest withdrawal (to the nearest $2,500) that lasts at least that long at 5%.
  5. Discuss: what risks could make Kim's plan fail even if her calculation is right?
Discussion points
Withdrawal$25,000$30,000$40,000$50,000
Fund lasts (5%)indefinitely37 years21 years15 years

2. $25,000 equals the first year's interest, so the fund never runs down. Above that, every extra dollar eats into capital, which then earns less interest — the fund's life shrinks dramatically.

3. 16 years at 3%; 31 years at 7%. The interest rate matters enormously over a long drawdown.

4. $35,000 per year lasts 26 years; $37,500 lasts only 23. So $35,000 is the largest withdrawal that lasts at least 25 years.

5. Interest rates may fall; inflation means $35,000 buys less each year; she may live longer than 25 years; unexpected costs (health, housing) may force larger withdrawals.
