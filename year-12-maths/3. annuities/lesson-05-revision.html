Method sheet

Tabular modelling

Accumulating annuity (paying in)

\[\text{Interest} = \text{Opening} \times r\] \[\text{Closing} = \text{Opening} + \text{Interest} + \text{Contribution}\]

Drawdown annuity (taking out)

\[\text{Closing} = \text{Opening} + \text{Interest} - \text{Withdrawal}\]

Each closing balance becomes the next opening balance. If the withdrawal equals the interest, the capital is preserved forever.

Using interest factor tables

\[FV = \text{contribution} \times (\text{FV factor}) \qquad \text{contribution} = \frac{FV}{\text{FV factor}}\] \[PV = \text{payment} \times (\text{PV factor}) \qquad \text{payment} = \frac{PV}{\text{PV factor}}\]

Always look up the rate per period and the number of periods — convert for the compounding frequency first.

The equivalent single sum

\[PV_{\text{lump sum}} = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^{n}}\]

Use ordinary compound interest — not an annuity factor — when the question asks for a one-off amount equivalent to a stream of payments.

Method

Future value or present value?

Picking the wrong table costs more marks in this topic than any arithmetic slip. These phrases tell you which one:

The question says…Use
"contributes each year", "how much will they have"FV factor
"what will the account be worth in \(n\) years"FV factor
"how much must be contributed to reach $X"FV: divide target by factor
"how much is needed today", "lump sum required"PV factor
"pays $X per year for \(n\) years" (a fund paying out)PV factor
"find the loan repayment"PV: divide loan by factor
"lump sum now or instalments?"PV of the instalments, then compare
"single sum equivalent to a future amount"\((1+r)^{n}\) — plain compound interest

The one-line test

Are the regular payments going in to build something up? That is future value. Are they coming out of a sum that already exists? That is present value.

The two directions, side by side

The same $5,000 per year at 6%: accumulating for 20 years, then drawing down over 20 years.

Accumulation builds the balance; drawdown returns it to zero. The peak is the point where future value becomes present value.

Converting for the compounding period

Six-monthly: rate ÷ 2, periods × 2. Quarterly: ÷ 4, × 4. Monthly: ÷ 12, × 12. Both conversions, every time.

Exam technique

Mistakes that cost marks

MistakeFix
Using \(FV = PV(1+r)^n\) on the total contributed Each contribution compounds for a different time. Use the annuity factor.
Reading the FV table when the question wants PV Money going in → FV. Money coming out of an existing sum → PV.
Converting the rate but not the number of periods Quarterly over 5 years is \(n = 20\) and \(r = \) annual ÷ 4.
Charging interest on the first contribution in year 1 Contributions arrive at the end of the period, so year 1 earns $0 from an empty account.
Multiplying by the factor when finding a contribution Working backwards means dividing: contribution = target ÷ factor.
Combining a lump sum and an annuity in one formula Calculate them separately — \((1+r)^n\) for the lump sum, the factor for the annuity — then add.
Comparing a lump sum with the undiscounted total of instalments Always compare present values. $210,000 over 15 years is not worth $210,000 today.
Accepting a PV factor greater than \(n\) Impossible. You have read the FV table by mistake.
Not stating that the decision depends on the interest rate Lump-sum-vs-instalments answers reverse at different rates. Say so.
Rounding the factor to 2 decimal places mid-calculation Keep all four decimals; round only the final dollar answer.
