Calculating z-scores

How do you compare 78 in Maths with 64 in Music, when the two tests have nothing in common? You stop counting marks and start counting standard deviations. That count is the \(z\)-score, and it is the single most useful idea in this topic.

Theory

The idea behind the z-score

Suppose you scored 78 on a Maths test where the class averaged 70, and 64 on a Music test where the class averaged 55. On Maths you were 8 marks above average; on Music, 9 marks above. Does that make Music your stronger result?

Not necessarily. Everything depends on how spread out each set of marks was. If Maths marks were tightly bunched (\(\sigma = 4\)) then being 8 above average is a long way ahead of the pack. If Music marks were widely scattered (\(\sigma = 18\)) then 9 above average is barely distinguishable from the middle.

The fix

Stop measuring in marks, centimetres or seconds. Measure in standard deviations. The number of standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean is called its \(z\)-score, or standardised score.

In the example above, Maths gives \(z = 8 \div 4 = 2\) and Music gives \(z = 9 \div 18 = 0.5\). The Maths result is four times as far from average, measured in the only unit that lets the two be compared. Maths was clearly the better performance.

Method

The formula

Standardised score

\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]

where \(x\) is the specific value, \(\mu\) is the mean of the dataset and \(\sigma\) is its standard deviation.

Read the formula in two steps. The numerator \(x - \mu\) is how far the value is from the mean, in the original units. Dividing by \(\sigma\) converts that distance into a number of standard deviations.

Reading the answer

\(z\)Meaning
\(z = 0\)The value is exactly the mean
\(z > 0\)Above the mean
\(z < 0\)Below the mean
\(|z| < 1\)Ordinary — within one standard deviation
\(|z| \approx 2\)Unusual — only about 5% of values are this far out
\(|z| \ge 3\)Extreme — about 3 values in 1000

Worked example 1

A test has \(\mu = 62\) and \(\sigma = 8\). Find the \(z\)-score for a mark of 74.

\[z = \frac{74 - 62}{8} = \frac{12}{8} = 1.5\]

The mark is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean — a good result, better than average but not exceptional.

Worked example 2

The same test. Find the \(z\)-score for a mark of 50.

\[z = \frac{50 - 62}{8} = \frac{-12}{8} = -1.5\]

The negative sign means below the mean — by 1.5 standard deviations. Never drop the sign: it carries half the information.

Worked example 3

Babies' birth weights are normal with \(\mu = 3.4\) kg and \(\sigma = 0.5\) kg. Find the \(z\)-score of a 2.1 kg baby and interpret it.

\[z = \frac{2.1 - 3.4}{0.5} = \frac{-1.3}{0.5} = -2.6\]

The baby is 2.6 standard deviations below the mean birth weight — well into the lower tail, and low enough that a paediatrician would monitor the baby closely.

Theory

The standard normal distribution

Something neat happens when you convert an entire dataset to \(z\)-scores.

Key property

The set of \(z\)-scores for data arising from a normally distributed random variable always has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

The reason is straightforward. Subtracting \(\mu\) from every value shifts the whole distribution so its centre sits at 0. Dividing every value by \(\sigma\) then rescales the spread so that one standard deviation becomes exactly one unit.

Whatever you started with — kilograms, marks, milliseconds, dollars — you end up with the same distribution: the standard normal distribution, centred on 0 with a standard deviation of 1. That is what makes \(z\)-scores from different datasets directly comparable, and it is why one table of areas works for every normal distribution.

Worked example 4

Five students score 54, 62, 70, 78 and 86 on a test with \(\mu = 70\) and \(\sigma = 8\). Convert to \(z\)-scores and verify the property.

\(x\)5462707886
\(x - \mu\)−16−80816
\(z\)−2−1012

The \(z\)-scores sum to 0, so their mean is 0 — exactly as expected. Their spread is now measured in standard deviations rather than marks.

Application

Comparing scores from different datasets

This is the single most examined use of the \(z\)-score, and it always follows the same four steps.

The method

  1. Calculate the \(z\)-score for each result separately, using that dataset's own \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).
  2. Compare the \(z\)-scores, keeping their signs.
  3. The higher \(z\)-score is the better relative performance.
  4. Write a conclusion in the context of the question — this mark is awarded separately, and it is the one most often lost.

Worked example 5

Mia scored 78 in Maths (\(\mu = 70\), \(\sigma = 4\)) and 64 in Music (\(\mu = 55\), \(\sigma = 18\)). In which subject did she perform better relative to her class?

Maths: \(z = \dfrac{78 - 70}{4} = 2.0\)

Music: \(z = \dfrac{64 - 55}{18} = 0.5\)

Conclusion. Mia's Maths \(z\)-score of 2.0 is much higher than her Music \(z\)-score of 0.5. Although her raw Music mark was 9 above average and her Maths mark only 8 above, Maths marks were far more tightly clustered, so 78 placed her much further ahead of her classmates. Mia performed better in Maths.

Worked example 6 — when lower is better

In a 100 m sprint the squad averages 12.40 s with \(\sigma = 0.50\) s. In a 200 m sprint the squad averages 25.60 s with \(\sigma = 1.20\) s. Jo runs 11.80 s and 24.40 s. Which is the better run?

100 m: \(z = \dfrac{11.80 - 12.40}{0.50} = -1.2\)

200 m: \(z = \dfrac{24.40 - 25.60}{1.20} = -1.0\)

Conclusion. For times, a lower value is better, so the more negative \(z\)-score is the stronger run. Jo's 100 m \(z\)-score of −1.2 beats the 200 m score of −1.0, so the 100 m was the better performance relative to the squad.

Always check the direction of the variable before concluding. Times, golf scores, error rates and waiting times are all "lower is better".

Method

Working backwards to the original value

Exam questions often run the other way: you are told the \(z\)-score and asked for the raw value, or told the value and \(z\)-score and asked for \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\). Rearrange the formula.

Rearrangements

\[x = \mu + z\sigma \qquad \mu = x - z\sigma \qquad \sigma = \frac{x - \mu}{z}\]

Worked example 8

A test has \(\mu = 64\) and \(\sigma = 11\). What mark has a \(z\)-score of \(-1.4\)?

\[x = \mu + z\sigma = 64 + (-1.4)(11) = 64 - 15.4 = 48.6\]

A mark of 48.6 sits 1.4 standard deviations below the mean.

Worked example 9

A value of 92 has a \(z\)-score of 2.5 in a dataset with mean 67. Find the standard deviation.

\[\sigma = \frac{x - \mu}{z} = \frac{92 - 67}{2.5} = \frac{25}{2.5} = 10\]

Check: \(z = (92 - 67) \div 10 = 2.5\) ✓

Summary

\(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\) counts standard deviations from the mean. The sign says which side, the size says how unusual. Standardising a normal dataset always gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1, which is what makes different datasets comparable. Lesson 3 turns \(z\)-scores into probabilities.

Application

Setting cut-offs with z-scores

Organisations often write rules in \(z\)-scores — "reject anything more than 2 standard deviations from the target", "invite the top students, \(z \ge 1.5\)". To apply a rule you convert it back to raw values with \(x = \mu + z\sigma\).

Rules of thumb for "unusual"

\(|z| > 2\): unusual — worth a second look. \(|z| > 3\): extreme — check for a recording error or a fault.

Worked example 10

(a) A bakery's loaves have \(\mu = 800\) g and \(\sigma = 12\) g. Loaves with \(|z| > 2\) are withdrawn. Which masses are kept?

\(800 - 2(12) = 776\) and \(800 + 2(12) = 824\). Loaves from 776 g to 824 g are kept.

(b) A maths enrichment program invites students with \(z \ge 1.5\) on a test with \(\mu = 62\), \(\sigma = 9\). What mark is needed?

\(62 + 1.5 \times 9 = 75.5\), so 76 or more (if marks are whole numbers).

Your turn

  1. Cereal boxes have \(\mu = 500\) g and \(\sigma = 6\) g. Boxes with \(z < -2.5\) are underweight and rejected. Below what mass is a box rejected?
    Show working

    \(500 - 2.5 \times 6 = 485\) g.

  2. In a dataset with \(\mu = 80\) and \(\sigma = 6\), a value of 98 is recorded. Is it unusual? What should be done?
    Show working

    \(z = \frac{98 - 80}{6} = 3\): extreme. Check whether it was recorded correctly before using it.
