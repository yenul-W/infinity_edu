Theory

The empirical rule

The area under a normal curve between any two values is the proportion of the data that falls between them — and therefore the probability that a randomly chosen value lands there. The empirical rule gives you three of those areas, and everything else in this lesson is built from them.

The 68 · 95 · 99.7 rule

For data arising from a normally distributed random variable:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{about } 68\% &\ \text{have } -1 \le z \le 1 \\ \text{about } 95\% &\ \text{have } -2 \le z \le 2 \\ \text{about } 99.7\% &\ \text{have } -3 \le z \le 3 \end{aligned}\]

Equivalently, in original units: 68% lie between \(\mu - \sigma\) and \(\mu + \sigma\), 95% between \(\mu - 2\sigma\) and \(\mu + 2\sigma\), and 99.7% between \(\mu - 3\sigma\) and \(\mu + 3\sigma\).

The three bands

Choose how many standard deviations to shade, and set the distribution.

Worked example 1

Adult male heights are normal with \(\mu = 175\) cm, \(\sigma = 7\) cm. Between what heights do the middle 95% of men lie?

95% corresponds to \(-2 \le z \le 2\), so the limits are

\[\mu - 2\sigma = 175 - 14 = 161 \text{ cm} \qquad \mu + 2\sigma = 175 + 14 = 189 \text{ cm}\]

About 95% of adult men are between 161 cm and 189 cm tall.

Method

The eight segments

The three bands and the curve's symmetry break the distribution into eight pieces. Learn these eight percentages and you can answer almost any empirical-rule question by adding them up.

Where the percentages come from

Between \(z = -1\) and \(z = 1\): 68%. Symmetry splits it into 34% each side of the mean.

Between \(z = 1\) and \(z = 2\): \((95 - 68) \div 2 = \mathbf{13.5\%}\), and the same between \(z = -2\) and \(z = -1\).

Between \(z = 2\) and \(z = 3\): \((99.7 - 95) \div 2 = \mathbf{2.35\%}\), and the same on the left.

Beyond \(z = 3\): \((100 - 99.7) \div 2 = \mathbf{0.15\%}\) in each tail.

Three facts worth memorising

  • \(P(z < 0) = 50\%\) — symmetry.
  • \(P(z > 1) = 34 + 13.5 + 2.35 + 0.15 = 16\%\), and likewise \(P(z < -1) = 16\%\).
  • \(P(z > 2) = 2.5\%\) and \(P(z > 3) = 0.15\%\).

Method

Shading the region

Every empirical-rule question is solved the same way. Sketch, convert, shade, add.

The four steps

  1. Sketch a bell curve and mark \(\mu\) and the values at \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3\) standard deviations.
  2. Convert the values in the question to \(z\)-scores.
  3. Shade the region the question asks about.
  4. Add the segment percentages inside the shading.

Worked example 2

A test is normal with \(\mu = 60\), \(\sigma = 10\). Find the percentage of students scoring between 50 and 80.

Convert. \(z = (50 - 60) \div 10 = -1\) and \(z = (80 - 60) \div 10 = 2\).

Shade from \(z = -1\) to \(z = 2\).

Add. \(34 + 34 + 13.5 = 81.5\%\).

About 81.5% of students score between 50 and 80.

Worked example 3

Same test. Find the percentage scoring above 70.

Convert. \(z = (70 - 60) \div 10 = 1\).

Add everything to the right of \(z = 1\): \(13.5 + 2.35 + 0.15 = 16\%\).

Alternatively, \(100 - 50 - 34 = 16\%\). Both routes give 16%.

Worked example 4

Same test. Find \(P(\text{score} < 40)\).

Convert. \(z = (40 - 60) \div 10 = -2\).

Add everything left of \(z = -2\): \(2.35 + 0.15 = 2.5\%\).

So \(P = 0.025\). Written as a probability, percentages become decimals.

Application

Tails, and events "more extreme" than a given one

The syllabus asks you to use \(z\)-scores to identify the probability of events less or more extreme than a given event. In practice this means reading a tail.

Tail probabilities

EventProbability
\(z > 1\) or \(z < -1\)16% each — 32% combined
\(z > 2\) or \(z < -2\)2.5% each — 5% combined
\(z > 3\) or \(z < -3\)0.15% each — 0.3% combined

Worked example 5

Battery lifetimes are normal with \(\mu = 400\) hours, \(\sigma = 30\) hours. A battery lasts 460 hours. What is the probability of a battery lasting at least this long?

\(z = (460 - 400) \div 30 = 2\), and \(P(z > 2) = 2.5\%\).

So there is a 2.5% chance — about 1 battery in 40 lasts 460 hours or more.

Worked example 6 — "more extreme" in either direction

Same batteries. What is the probability that a battery's lifetime is more than two standard deviations from the mean, in either direction?

That is \(z < -2\) or \(z > 2\): \(2.5 + 2.5 = 5\%\).

Equivalently, \(100 - 95 = 5\%\). Note the difference from Worked example 5 — read carefully whether the question means one tail or both.

Careful with the wording

"Longer than 460 hours" is one tail. "Differs from the mean by more than 60 hours" is two tails. "More extreme" almost always means both tails. Sketching the shading is the reliable way to tell.

Your turn

  1. Commute times are normal with \(\mu = 35\) minutes and \(\sigma = 5\) minutes. Find the probability that a commute takes longer than 45 minutes.
    Show working

  2. Find the probability that a commute differs from the mean by more than 10 minutes.
    Show working

Application

Expected numbers

Once you have a percentage, multiplying by the size of the group converts it into an expected count — the same \(np\) idea as the probability topic.

Expected frequency

\[\text{expected number} = n \times P\]

where \(n\) is the number in the group and \(P\) is the probability from the empirical rule, written as a decimal.

Worked example 7

In a school of 1,200 students, IQ scores are normal with \(\mu = 100\), \(\sigma = 15\). How many students would you expect to have an IQ above 130?

\(z = (130 - 100) \div 15 = 2\), so \(P(z > 2) = 2.5\% = 0.025\).

\[1200 \times 0.025 = 30 \text{ students}\]

Worked example 8

A factory produces 8,000 bolts a day, with lengths normal at \(\mu = 40\) mm, \(\sigma = 0.5\) mm. Bolts outside 39 mm to 41 mm are rejected. How many are rejected daily?

\(39\) mm is \(z = -2\) and \(41\) mm is \(z = 2\), so 95% are accepted and 5% rejected.

\[8000 \times 0.05 = 400 \text{ bolts}\]

Application

Making judgements from z-scores

The final dot point asks you to use \(z\)-scores to make judgements about outcomes. This is a written-response skill: state the \(z\)-score, state the probability, then state the decision it supports.

A rough scale for judgement

\(|z|\)How commonTypical judgement
Under 1About 2 in 3Entirely ordinary; no action
1 to 2About 1 in 3Noticeable but not remarkable
2 to 3About 1 in 20Unusual; worth investigating
Over 3About 3 in 1000Extreme; strong evidence something has changed

Worked example 9

A machine fills bags with \(\mu = 1000\) g and \(\sigma = 8\) g. A sample bag weighs 975 g. Should the machine be stopped?

\(z = (975 - 1000) \div 8 = -3.125\).

Judgement. A \(z\)-score beyond \(-3\) occurs in fewer than 0.15% of bags when the machine is working correctly — roughly 1 bag in 700. Getting one in a small sample is strong evidence that the machine has drifted out of calibration, so it should be stopped and checked.

Worked example 10

A scholarship is offered to students with a \(z\)-score above 2 on an entrance test. The test is normal with \(\mu = 65\), \(\sigma = 12\), and 1,500 students sit it. What mark is required, and roughly how many will qualify?

Mark. \(x = 65 + 2 \times 12 = 89\).

Number. \(P(z > 2) = 2.5\%\), so \(1500 \times 0.025 = 37.5 \approx 38\) students.

The cut-off is 89, and about 37 or 38 students would be expected to qualify.

Judgement practice

Type ordinary, unusual or extreme, using the scale above.

Worked example 11 — exam style

A café's daily takings over 360 trading days are normal with \(\mu = \$2\,400\) and \(\sigma = \$300\).

(a) On how many days would takings be expected to exceed $3,000? [2 marks]

\(z = \frac{3000 - 2400}{300} = 2\), so 2.5%: \(360 \times 0.025 = 9\) days.

(b) On how many days would takings be between $2,100 and $3,000? [2 marks]

\(z\) from \(-1\) to 2: \(34 + 34 + 13.5 = 81.5\%\), so \(360 \times 0.815 \approx 293\) days.

(c) One day the takings were $1,350. Make a judgement. [2 marks]

\(z = \frac{1350 - 2400}{300} = -3.5\): beyond \(-3\), which happens on fewer than 0.15% of days if conditions are normal. Something unusual almost certainly happened — a storm, a closure for part of the day, or a register error — and it is worth investigating.

Summary

Areas under the normal curve are probabilities. The empirical rule gives you 68%, 95% and 99.7%, which symmetry breaks into eight segments: 0.15, 2.35, 13.5, 34, 34, 13.5, 2.35, 0.15. Convert values to \(z\)-scores, shade the region, add the segments, and multiply by \(n\) if a count is wanted.

Method

Working backwards from a percentage

Sometimes the percentage is given and the value is wanted: "what mark puts a student in the top 16%?" Reverse the process: percentage → \(z\)-score → value, using \(x = \mu + z\sigma\).

The question says\(z\)
Top 16% / bottom 16%\(z = 1\) / \(z = -1\)
Top 2.5% / bottom 2.5%\(z = 2\) / \(z = -2\)
Top 0.15% / bottom 0.15%\(z = 3\) / \(z = -3\)
Middle 68%, 95%, 99.7%\(z = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3\)

Worked example 12

(a) A test has \(\mu = 64\), \(\sigma = 9\). What mark puts a student in the top 16%?

Top 16% means \(z > 1\): \(x = 64 + 1 \times 9 = 73\).

(b) A machine fills bottles with mean 750 mL. Only 2.5% of bottles contain less than 735 mL. Find \(\sigma\).

Bottom 2.5% means \(z = -2\) at 735 mL: \(735 = 750 - 2\sigma\), so \(\sigma = 7.5\) mL.

Your turn

  1. Heights are normal with \(\mu = 168\) cm and \(\sigma = 6\) cm. Below what height is the shortest 0.15%?
    Show working

    \(z = -3\): \(168 - 18 = 150\) cm.

  2. 16% of eggs weigh more than 66 g, and \(\sigma = 4\) g. Find the mean egg mass.
    Show working

    Top 16% starts at \(z = 1\): \(66 = \mu + 4\), so \(\mu = 62\) g.

Class activity

Region race

About 10 minutes · pairs. For each region, both partners work out the percentage on paper using the eight segments, then check it with the segment adder. Score a point for each correct answer before checking.

  1. \(-2 < z < 1\)
  2. \(0 < z < 3\)
  3. \(1 < z < 3\)
  4. \(-3 < z < -1\)
  5. \(-1 < z < 2\)
  6. Reverse challenge: find every pair of whole-number \(z\)-values that encloses exactly 47.5%.

Then discuss: why do questions 1 and 5 give the same answer, and so do 3 and 4?

6. 0 to 2 and \(-2\) to 0 (each \(34 + 13.5 = 47.5\%\)).

Symmetry: \(-2 < z < 1\) is the mirror image of \(-1 < z < 2\), and \(1 < z < 3\) mirrors \(-3 < z < -1\). A normal curve is symmetric about the mean, so mirrored regions have equal areas.
