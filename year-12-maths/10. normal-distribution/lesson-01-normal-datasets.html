Theory

The bell curve

Take a large sample of a naturally varying measurement — the heights of adult women, the birth weights of babies, the diameters of ball bearings off a production line — and draw a histogram. Most values cluster near the middle, and the further you move from the middle in either direction, the rarer the values become. Narrow the class intervals and increase the sample size, and the histogram settles into a smooth, symmetric, bell-shaped curve.

A random variable whose values behave this way is called normally distributed, and the curve is the normal distribution.

Features of every normal curve

  • Symmetric about the mean — the left half mirrors the right.
  • Single peak at the mean. The most common value is the central one.
  • Tails that fall away smoothly on both sides and never quite touch the horizontal axis — extreme values are rare but not impossible.
  • Total area under the curve is 1, because every observation lies somewhere under it. Areas therefore represent proportions or probabilities.
  • Completely determined by two numbers: the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\).

Build the curve

Change the mean and standard deviation and watch what each one does. The mean slides the curve; the standard deviation stretches it.

Worked example 1

The heights of adult Australian men are approximately normally distributed with a mean of 175 cm and a standard deviation of 7 cm. Describe what this tells you.

Centre. 175 cm is the most common height, and the histogram peaks there.

Symmetry. A man is just as likely to be 168 cm (7 cm below) as 182 cm (7 cm above).

Spread. Typical heights sit within a few centimetres of 175 cm. A height of 200 cm is far out in the upper tail — possible, but rare.

Why the normal curve turns up so often

A measurement that is the combined result of many small, independent influences tends to be normally distributed. Adult height depends on hundreds of genes plus nutrition, illness and sleep; no single factor dominates, so the small pushes up and down average out into a symmetric bell. Measurements driven by one dominant factor — income, for example, or time spent waiting in a queue — usually are not normal.

Your turn

  1. True or false? (a) A normal curve can have two peaks. (b) The tails of a normal curve touch the axis. (c) The total area under a normal curve is 1.
    Show working

    (a) False — one peak, at the mean. (b) False — the tails approach the axis but never touch it. (c) True.

  2. Egg masses are normal with mean 60 g and standard deviation 4 g. Which is more likely: an egg of 56 g or an egg of 64 g?
    Show working

    Equally likely: both are 4 g (one standard deviation) from the mean, and the curve is symmetric.

Theory

Mean, median and mode

For a perfectly symmetric distribution with a single peak, the three measures of centre land on exactly the same value.

The key result

For data arising from a random variable that is normally distributed, \[\text{mean} \approx \text{median} \approx \text{mode}\] and all three sit at the centre of the curve, under the peak.

MeasureDefinitionWhy it lands at the centre
Mode The most frequently occurring value The curve is highest at the centre, so values there occur most often
Median The middle value — half the data lies below it Symmetry means exactly half the area lies on each side of the centre
Mean The balance point of the data A symmetric shape balances at its line of symmetry

In real data the three will not be exactly equal — a sample is never perfectly symmetric — which is why the syllabus says approximately equal. If your sample gives a mean of 62.3, a median of 62 and a mode of 62, that is exactly what you would expect from a normal population.

Worked example 2

A class of 30 students sits a test. The mean is 64.1, the median is 64 and the mode is 65. Is the data consistent with a normal distribution?

Answer. Yes. All three measures agree to within about one mark on a scale of 100, which is the closeness you would expect from a sample of only 30 drawn from a normal population. There is no evidence of skew.

Compare with a second class where the mean is 71, the median is 78 and the mode is 82. Here the mean is pulled well below the median by a few very low marks — the data is negatively skewed and not normal.

Theory

The role of the standard deviation

The mean tells you where the curve sits. The standard deviation \(\sigma\) tells you how wide it is — how far a typical value falls from the mean.

Reading σ

Small \(\sigma\) — a tall, narrow curve. Values cluster tightly around the mean; the process is consistent.

Large \(\sigma\) — a low, wide curve. Values spread far from the mean; the process is variable.

Both curves still enclose a total area of 1, which is why a wider curve must also be flatter.

Two processes compared

Two machines fill 500 mL bottles. Both average 500 mL, but one is more consistent. Adjust each machine's standard deviation.

Worked example 3

Two archers each shoot 100 arrows. Archer A's distances from the bullseye are normally distributed with \(\mu = 6\) cm, \(\sigma = 1.5\) cm. Archer B's are normal with \(\mu = 6\) cm, \(\sigma = 4\) cm. Who is the better archer?

Answer. Both are equally accurate on average — the same mean. But Archer A is far more consistent: a standard deviation of 1.5 cm against 4 cm means A's arrows cluster much more tightly. For most purposes A is the better archer, because consistency is what can be coached into an improved mean. B's results include both very good and very poor shots.

Distance from the mean, measured in σ

The reason \(\sigma\) matters so much is that it becomes the unit of measurement for the whole topic. "3 cm above the mean" means something quite different on a curve with \(\sigma = 1.5\) than on one with \(\sigma = 4\). Lesson 2 turns this idea into the \(z\)-score.

Your turn

  1. Two bakeries' loaves both average 800 g. Bakery A has \(\sigma = 5\) g and bakery B has \(\sigma = 20\) g. Which is more consistent, and whose curve is taller?
    Show working

    Bakery A is more consistent. Its curve is taller and narrower, because the same total area of 1 is packed into a smaller spread.

  2. The standard deviation of a normal distribution is halved and the mean is unchanged. Describe the new curve.
    Show working

    Same centre, half as wide, and twice as tall at the peak — the area must still be 1.

Theory

When data is not normal

Plenty of real datasets are not bell-shaped, and the normal-distribution methods of this topic do not apply to them. The most common departure is skew.

ShapeAppearanceCentre measuresExample
Symmetric (normal) Bell-shaped, equal tails mean ≈ median ≈ mode Adult heights
Positively skewed Long tail to the right mean > median > mode Household income; house prices
Negatively skewed Long tail to the left mean < median < mode Age at death; marks on an easy test
Bimodal Two separate peaks Two modes; mean is misleading Heights of a mixed adult group, measured together

The tail names the skew

The skew is named after the direction the long tail points, not where the bulk of the data sits. The long tail also drags the mean towards it — which is why in positively skewed income data the mean income always exceeds the median, and why the median is the fairer summary.

Shape identifier

Given the three measures of centre, name the shape. Type normal, positive or negative.

Your turn

  1. Sydney house prices have a long tail of very expensive homes. Name the skew, and say which is larger, the mean or the median.
    Show working

    Positively skewed (long right tail). The expensive homes drag the mean above the median.

  2. On a very easy test, most students score 85–100 but a few score much lower. Name the skew and order the mean, median and mode.
    Show working

    Negatively skewed (long left tail): mean < median < mode.

Method

Sketching a normal curve with a scale

A good sketch of a normal distribution is more than a bell shape: its horizontal axis is labelled in steps of one standard deviation. This is the picture you will shade in Lesson 3.

The routine

  1. Draw a symmetric bell with its peak in the middle.
  2. Mark the mean \(\mu\) under the peak.
  3. Mark \(\mu \pm \sigma\), \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\) and \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) at equal spacing on either side.
  4. Make the curve almost reach the axis by \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\): nearly all the data lies within three standard deviations of the mean.

The curve changes from bending down to bending up at \(\mu \pm \sigma\) — one standard deviation either side of the peak.

Method

Recognising normality

Exam questions rarely ask you to prove a dataset is normal. They ask whether it is reasonable to model it as normal. Three checks answer that.

Three checks

  1. Shape. Does the histogram have a single peak, roughly symmetric, with tails falling away on both sides?
  2. Centre. Are the mean, median and mode close together?
  3. Context. Is the measurement the result of many small independent influences, with no hard boundary close to the mean?

Worked example 5

Is it reasonable to model each of these as normally distributed?

(a) The birth weights of 5,000 babies. Yes — a classic example. Many small influences, symmetric histogram, mean and median both near 3.4 kg.

(b) The number of children per household. No. The values are whole numbers, cannot be negative, and pile up at 0, 1 and 2 with a tail to the right. The distribution is discrete and positively skewed.

(c) The annual salaries of everyone in a city. No. Strongly positively skewed — a small number of very high earners drag the mean well above the median.

(d) The diameters of bolts from a well-maintained machine. Yes. Manufacturing variation is the sum of many tiny random errors, giving a tight symmetric spread around the target diameter.

Normal or not?

Type yes or no.

Worked example 6 — exam style

A café records how long customers wait for coffee. The times range from 0.5 to 15 minutes, with mean 4.2 minutes, median 3.6 minutes and mode 3.0 minutes.

(a) Is it reasonable to model the waiting times as normal? Justify. [2 marks]

No. Mean > median > mode shows positive skew, and the range stretches much further above the mean (about 11 minutes) than below it (under 4 minutes), where times cannot go below 0.

(b) Which measure of centre best describes a typical wait? [1 mark]

The median, 3.6 minutes. The few very long waits pull the mean up.

(c) After a second coffee machine is installed, the mean, median and mode are 3.1, 3.1 and 3.0 minutes, and the histogram is symmetric. Comment. [1 mark]

The long waits have gone. The distribution is now approximately symmetric, so a normal model is reasonable.

Summary

A normal distribution is a symmetric, single-peaked, bell-shaped model with mean \(\mu\) at its centre and standard deviation \(\sigma\) setting its width. Its mean, median and mode coincide, and the area under it totals 1. The next lesson gives you the tool that makes all of this calculable: the \(z\)-score.

Class activity

Normal or not? A sorting challenge

About 10 minutes · groups of three. For each dataset, decide whether a normal model is reasonable. Use the three checks (shape, centre, context) and write a one-line justification.

  1. Heights of 500 Year 12 girls.
  2. Weekly pocket money of 500 teenagers.
  3. Number of siblings of each student in your school.
  4. Daily maximum temperatures in Sydney during January, over 30 years.
  5. Masses of 1,000 bags of rice filled by a machine set to 1 kg.
  6. Ages of people attending a primary school (students and staff together).

Then, in Build the curve, model a test with mean 65 and standard deviation 10. Between which two marks would you expect almost all students to score?

Discussion points

1. Normal: many small influences, symmetric.

2. Not normal: cannot be negative, many small amounts and a long right tail — positively skewed.

3. Not normal: whole numbers piled up at 0–2 with a right tail.

4. Approximately normal: many weather influences around a typical value (a hot tail on heatwave days is possible).

5. Normal: small random filling errors around 1 kg.

6. Not normal: bimodal — one peak for children around 5–12 and another, smaller one for adults.

Test: almost all marks lie within three standard deviations of the mean, from 35 to 95.
