Reference

Formula sheet

Standardised score

\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]

\(x\) the value, \(\mu\) the mean, \(\sigma\) the standard deviation. A positive \(z\) is above the mean, a negative \(z\) below it.

Rearrangements

\[x = \mu + z\sigma \qquad \mu = x - z\sigma \qquad \sigma = \frac{x - \mu}{z}\]

The empirical rule

\[68\% : -1 \le z \le 1 \qquad 95\% : -2 \le z \le 2 \qquad 99.7\% : -3 \le z \le 3\]

Expected numbers

\[\text{expected number} = n \times P\]

\(n\) the size of the group, \(P\) the probability as a decimal.

Properties to quote

  • A normal curve is symmetric, single-peaked and encloses a total area of 1.
  • Mean \(\approx\) median \(\approx\) mode.
  • Standardising gives \(z\)-scores with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  • Areas under the curve are proportions, and therefore probabilities.

Method

Which method do I use?

The question says…Do this
"Find the \(z\)-score"\(z = (x - \mu) \div \sigma\)
"What value has \(z = \ldots\)"\(x = \mu + z\sigma\)
"Which performance was better"Both \(z\)-scores, then a sentence in context
"What percentage lies between…"Convert to \(z\), shade, add segments
"Find the probability that…"Same, then divide the percentage by 100
"How many of the \(n\) would you expect"\(n \times P\)
"The middle 95% lie between…"\(\mu \pm 2\sigma\)
"More extreme than…"Both tails, unless a direction is stated
"Should the machine be stopped?"\(z\)-score, then probability, then judgement

Always, in every question

Sketch the curve. Mark \(\mu\) and the \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3\) values. Shade what is asked for. Three-quarters of lost marks in this topic come from shading the wrong region, and a sketch takes fifteen seconds.

Exam technique

Mistakes that cost marks

MistakeFix
Dropping the negative sign on a \(z\)-score The sign says which side of the mean the value is on — half the information.
Dividing by \(\sigma^2\) instead of \(\sigma\) The formula uses the standard deviation, not the variance.
Using \(x = \mu - z\sigma\) It is \(x = \mu + z\sigma\); the sign of \(z\) handles the direction.
Comparing raw marks across different tests Standardise first — different means and spreads make raw marks incomparable.
For times, picking the higher \(z\)-score as better When lower is better, the more negative \(z\)-score wins.
Giving a \(z\)-score with no interpretation Comparison questions carry a separate mark for the conclusion in context.
Shading the wrong side of the curve Sketch and label \(\mu \pm \sigma, \pm 2\sigma, \pm 3\sigma\) before shading.
Using 68% for \(P(z > 1)\) 68% is the two-sided band. One tail is 16%.
Halving 95% to get 47.5% for a tail The tail is \((100 - 95) \div 2 = 2.5\%\), not half of 95%.
Confusing one tail with two "Above 86" is one tail; "differs from the mean by more than 16" is two.
Giving a percentage where a probability was asked for Divide by 100 — 2.5% is \(P = 0.025\).
Applying the empirical rule to skewed data It depends on symmetry. Check the shape first.
Rounding an expected count to a whole number without saying so 22.5 students is a valid expected value; state it, then round if the context needs it.
